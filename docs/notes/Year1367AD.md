# Year 1367 AD — Historical Events, Source Material & Quest Vignettes

**Canonical game year: 1367 AD.**  
**Source method:** Each event grounded in a Project Gutenberg primary text.  
**Vignette style:** French theater, single fighter protagonist, English prose only.  
**Quest structure per event:** Act I (Before) · Act II (The Test) · Act III (Fail) · Act IV (Pass) · Fortune Teller Omen.

## Event A — The Battle of Nájera (April 3, 1367)

### Historical Summary

Edward of Woodstock — the Black Prince, son of Edward III of England — crosses the Pyrenees in winter at the head of a combined English, Gascon, and Navarrese army. He fights for Pedro the Cruel of Castile against the usurper Henry of Trastámara, who holds the Spanish throne with French backing. On April 3 at the Nájerilla river plain, the English longbow volleys break Henry's lines in under an hour. Pedro is restored. The Black Prince wins everything — then loses it slowly to the Spanish heat and dysentery. He returns home on a litter. His great victory buys him only ruin.

The *routier* mercenary companies — hardened soldiers with no war to fight in France after a recent truce — cross the Pyrenees with him. They are professional, pitiless, and available to whoever pays. After Nájera, some stay. Some drift north again. All of them carry the battle in their eyes.

**Primary source:** *The Boy's Froissart* — Jean Froissart / Sidney Lanier. Project Gutenberg ID 61710.  
**Voice model:** Chivalric, breathless, name-dropping heralds and banners. Present-tense urgency.

### Children's Heroic Angle

A boy squire carries a sealed message from the Black Prince's tent to the Navarrese flank on the morning of the battle. The message says: *hold the left, whatever you hear.* He does not know if the prince is alive when he returns. He runs anyway. The left holds. The battle is won. He never learns if it was the message or the men.

### Fortune Teller Omen

*A roadside seer at the Pyrenean pass, wrapped in a grey cloak that smells of mountain snow, stops the column as it descends toward Castile. She speaks to no one in particular: "The prince who crosses mountains in triumph will return on a litter. His victory is the coin with which his ruin is purchased. But the coin is real. Spend it." She is not there when the column looks back.*

### Quest Vignette — "The Free Company" (Combat Quest)

**Fighter protagonist: Renaud le Bâtard, a routier sergeant who has fought at Brétigny, Poitiers, and now Nájera. His company has been paid. His contract has ended. He is thirty-one years old and has nowhere to go.**

---

**ACT I — BEFORE**

*The inn at Logroño smells of victory — spilled wine and the iron smell of men who have not washed since the battle. Renaud sits at the end of the long table and stares at his hands. Around him, twenty men argue about where the pay goes: north to Gascony, south to Granada, east to Navarre. One man says he heard there is work in the Baltic — something to do with the merchants of Lübeck. Renaud does not know where Lübeck is. He knows where his sword is. He knows where his company is. He does not know what comes after.*

*A merchant enters the inn. He is Aragonese, well-dressed, nervous. He speaks to the innkeeper, who points at Renaud. The merchant approaches and sits without being invited.*

*"I am told," says the merchant, "that you are without a contract."*

*Renaud looks at him.*

*"I have a problem that requires a man without a contract," the merchant continues. "A shipment of Castilian wool — ransomed from the French supply wagons at Nájera — is being held at a waystation three leagues east by men who were, until yesterday, on the winning side. They have decided not to return it. I will pay the market price of the wool plus ten percent for its recovery."*

*Renaud says: "How many men?"*

*"Seven," says the merchant. "Navarrese. Armed."*

*Renaud counts his company. He has twelve.*

*"Fifteen percent," says Renaud.*

*The merchant nods.*

---

**ACT II — THE TEST**

*The waystation is a stone farmhouse with a new lock on the barn door and smoke from the chimney that smells of mutton, which means they are comfortable and not expecting trouble. Renaud sends two men around the back fence. He sends three to the hayrick on the left. He walks to the front door himself with the remaining seven, and he knocks.*

*The door opens. A Navarrese man-at-arms fills the frame. Behind him: firelight, figures, the sound of dice.*

*"We've come for the wool," says Renaud, in the Gascon-inflected Castilian he has been using for three months.*

*The Navarrese studies him. Studies the men behind him. Studies the ones he has not yet seen at the hayrick.*

> **COMBAT ENCOUNTER — Seven Navarrese mercenaries (Tier 2). They fight in two waves: four at the door, three from the barn flank when they hear the fighting. Renaud must hold the doorway or the line breaks.**

*The Navarrese at the door says: "The wool belongs to whoever holds the barn."*

*Renaud says: "That's what I'm here to discuss."*

---

**ACT III — FAIL**

*The flank comes too fast. Renaud's men at the hayrick are not in position when the barn doors open, and three Navarrese with pikes pour out into the yard before the line forms. The fighting in the doorway goes wrong — one of Renaud's men goes down, and the Navarrese inside press the gap. Renaud pulls back to the road.*

*He stands in the dark, breathing. His hand is cut across the palm. Two of his men are sitting in the ditch. The farmhouse door is barred again and he can hear laughing from inside.*

*He will try again tomorrow. They will not expect the same approach twice.*

*The merchant's wool waits in the barn. The barn does not move.*

*(retryable: true — each attempt costs 1 hour)*

---

**ACT IV — PASS**

*Before dawn, Renaud sends four men over the back wall into the barn. They are quiet men — the kind of quiet that only routiers learn, which is the quiet of men who understand that making noise is a choice you do not always get back. The four men are inside the barn before the Navarrese in the farmhouse have opened their eyes.*

*At the agreed signal — a crow call, twice — Renaud's men at the front begin to make noise. A lot of noise. The Navarrese pour out the front door and into the yard.*

*The barn doors open from the inside.*

*The wool, forty bales of it, moves east on a cart before sunrise.*

*The merchant pays fifteen percent. Renaud splits it seven ways — his twelve men, plus the merchant's two drivers, who helped load faster than expected. He keeps one share for himself and puts it in the leather pouch he has carried since Poitiers. The pouch is getting heavy. He still does not know what comes after. But there is more in the pouch than there was yesterday.*

---

**Fortune Teller Coda**

*On the road back to Logroño, a woman sits on a stone wall mending a cloak. She does not look up as Renaud passes.*

*"The prince who wins everything loses it on the way home," she says, to no one.*

*Renaud keeps walking. He has heard this kind of talk before. He has also seen the prince's face at Nájera — flushed, triumphant, already sweating in a way that had nothing to do with the heat.*

*He thinks she might be right.*

## Event B — Tamerlane Rising (1367)

### Historical Summary

Timur ibn Taraghai Barlas — called Tamerlane by Europeans, Timur-i-lang ("Timur the Lame") by his own people — is thirty-one years old in 1367 and already a man around whom stories accumulate faster than facts. He was wounded twice in his twenties: an arrow through his right leg left him with a permanent limp, and he lost two fingers on his right hand. He survived both wounds, which medieval Central Asian culture read as evidence of divine protection. He is co-ruling the Chagatai territory of Transoxiana (modern Uzbekistan) with his brother-in-law Amir Husayn, and the partnership is uneasy. He has not yet conquered anything significant. He is still, in 1367, a rumor traveling west ahead of him.

In the bazaars of Samarkand, merchants whisper about the lame warlord who cannot be killed. In the caravanserais of Persia, travelers bring stories east and west. No one in Europe has heard of him yet. He will not arrive in Europe's consciousness for another decade. But the displaced scholars, merchants, and refugees who carry his story westward are already walking the Silk Road, and some of them will reach Mediterranean ports by the time the game begins.

**Primary source:** *The Travels of Sir John Mandeville* — Project Gutenberg ID 782. Written c. 1357, exactly a decade before.  
**Voice model:** Wide-eyed traveler's wonder; credulity as a literary stance; "they say in those lands that..."

### Children's Heroic Angle

A young horse-trader's apprentice in Samarkand is asked to carry a message to a warlord's camp three days' ride east. He does not know what the message says. He knows only that his master gave it to him with shaking hands and told him not to read it. He arrives at the camp. A lame man in plain clothes is sitting outside a tent, studying a map. The apprentice delivers the message. The lame man reads it, folds it, and looks at the boy. "Can you ride faster than you came?" he asks. The boy says yes. "Good," says the lame man. "Take this one back." The boy rides back faster than he came. He still does not know what either message said. His master pays him double and does not explain.

### Fortune Teller Omen

*The natal chart of a man born April 9, 1336, in Transoxiana: Aries rising, Mars dominant, the moon in the house of wounds. The astrologer who cast this chart in Samarkand rolls it up and does not sell it. He keeps it under his sleeping mat. He says: "A chart like this belongs to a man who is already more than a man, or not yet a man at all. Either way, I want him to know I did not sell it."*

### Quest Vignette — "The Scroll of Samarkand" (Skill Check Quest)

**Fighter protagonist: Marta of Ragusa, a Dalmatian mercenary who has been working caravan guard routes between the Venetian ports and the Levant for six years. She is practical, multilingual in the way of port soldiers — enough of five languages to survive in each — and deeply uninterested in politics.**

---

**ACT I — BEFORE**

*The scholar arrives at the Ragusa harbour gate at dusk, carrying nothing but a satchel and a wound on his left arm that he has wrapped in a strip cut from his own robe. His name, he says, is Yūsuf al-Dimashqī — Yūsuf of Damascus, though he has not been in Damascus for two years. He needs a guard. He has a specific destination: Venice, overland, as fast as possible.*

*Marta is between contracts. She has been between contracts for six weeks, which is long enough to make the dockmaster's patience run thin. She takes the job.*

*"What is in the satchel?" she asks.*

*"A scroll," says Yūsuf.*

*"What kind of scroll?"*

*"The kind," says Yūsuf, "that a certain warlord's men wounded my arm to prevent me from carrying."*

*Marta looks at the arm. The wound is real. The dressing is fresh.*

*"Double rate," she says.*

*Yūsuf nods.*

---

**ACT II — THE TEST**

*Three days north of Ragusa, on the road through the limestone karst, Marta sees two riders on the ridge above the road. They are not local. Their saddles are Central Asian — higher cantle, shorter stirrup. They are watching.*

*She does not slow down. She does not tell Yūsuf. She counts: two on the ridge, which means probably two more she cannot see.*

> **SKILL CHECK — Perception DC 13: Does Marta notice the second pair of riders in the tree line before they reach the narrow defile? Success: she chooses the longer but open road. Failure: the party enters the defile and the riders have the high ground.**

*If she takes the open road: the riders follow at distance but do not close. They wait for a better ground. The party reaches the next waystation.*

*If she enters the defile: the four riders converge at the narrowest point. The fight is harder than it needs to be.*

---

**ACT III — FAIL**

*In the defile, the riders come from both ends at once. Marta holds the north gap. Yūsuf, who is not useless — he carries a short blade and has clearly used one before — holds the south. But there are four of them and they are patient, rotating pressure, not committing, waiting for exhaustion.*

*One of them calls out in a language Marta does not speak. Yūsuf translates without being asked: "They want the scroll. They will let us go if we give them the scroll."*

*Marta says: "What does the scroll say?"*

*Yūsuf says: "It says where he is going. What he is planning. It is a letter from a man inside his council to a man in Venice who very much wants to know."*

*"Then we cannot give them the scroll," says Marta.*

*"No," says Yūsuf. "We cannot."*

*They retreat through a gap in the cliff face that Marta had noticed on the way in — a crevice barely wide enough to pull a horse through sideways. They lose the horse. They do not lose the scroll. They walk out of the karst in the dark and find the waystation by smell.*

*(retryable: true — each attempt costs 1 hour)*

---

**ACT IV — PASS**

*The open road adds four hours to the journey. Marta does not explain why she chooses it — she simply turns south at the fork without slowing. The riders on the ridge watch them go. They do not follow onto open ground.*

*The waystation that night has other travellers — a wool merchant, a Benedictine brother, two Venetian glassblowers heading home. Marta sits where she can see the door. Yūsuf sleeps with the satchel under his head.*

*In the morning, the riders are gone.*

*Venice is twelve more days. They arrive.*

*Yūsuf hands the scroll to a man in a counting house near the Rialto who reads it without expression, rolls it back up, and places it in a brass cylinder that he seals with wax. He pays Yūsuf. Yūsuf pays Marta. Marta counts it. It is correct.*

*She asks: "Did it matter? The scroll?"*

*Yūsuf says: "The man whose plans it describes will be very powerful in ten years. Perhaps in five. The people who now know his plans will be slightly less surprised."*

*Marta considers this. "Slightly," she says.*

*"It is something," says Yūsuf.*

---

**Fortune Teller Coda**

*At the Rialto, a woman sells horoscopes from a folding table. She calls out to Marta as she passes: "A chart for a soldier? I will tell you the year of your death for one ducat." Marta does not stop. The woman calls after her: "The lame man's chart says he cannot be stopped. I do not sell that one. But I will tell you this for free: his shadow arrives before he does. You have already walked in it."*

*Marta keeps walking. She has been in the shadow before. She is still walking.*

## Event C — The Ottoman Balkans (Murad I, 1367)

### Historical Summary

Murad I has held Adrianople — Edirne — since 1362. It is his European capital. By 1367, Ottoman forces have pushed deep into Thrace and are pressing on Bulgaria, Serbia, and the remaining Byzantine territories. Local lords — Bulgarian boyars, Serbian knezi, minor Wallachian princes — are making desperate calculations: tribute, alliance, conversion, flight, or war. The Pope has called for a crusade. No crusade has come. The local lords are alone with their accountants and their prayers.

The devşirme system — the levy of Christian boys as tribute, trained and converted as Janissary soldiers — is formalising under Murad I. A boy taken at eight may become an officer by thirty. He will speak Turkish, pray toward Mecca, and be more loyal to the Sultan than to any blood relative. He will also be one of the most effective soldiers in the known world. The first Janissaries are forming in exactly these years.

**Primary source:** *The Arabian Nights Entertainments* — Andrew Lang. Project Gutenberg ID 128. For the omen/dream frame.  
**Voice model:** Dream-logic, layered frame narrative, fate that cannot be escaped.

### Children's Heroic Angle

A Bulgarian village boy of twelve is taken in the devşirme levy. He is frightened — he has never left his valley. The officer who takes him is not cruel, which surprises him. At the training camp, an older boy from Thrace teaches him the language, the prayers, the exercises. Ten years later, the Bulgarian boy — now a Janissary captain named Davud — is ordered to collect the levy from a village. He does not recognize it until he sees the millpond. It is his village. He sits his horse at the edge of the village for a long time. Then he rides on and does not collect from that village that year. He files no report. No one asks.

### Fortune Teller Omen

*Osman's Dream, the founding prophecy, is told in every Ottoman camp: the Sultan's grandfather saw a moon rise from a holy man's chest and enter his own body, and from his navel a great tree grew, and its branches covered the world, and beneath the shade of the tree lay all the lands between the sea and the sea. By 1367, the tree has reached Europe. The shade is on Adrianople. Local lords who know the dream can see the branches moving toward them, slowly, the way shadows move in late afternoon: too slow to run from, too fast to pretend it is not happening.*

### Quest Vignette — "The Letter Under the Seal" (Social / Skill Check Quest)

**Fighter protagonist: Bogdan, a Bulgarian minor lord's household guard — a large, quiet man who has protected the same family for fifteen years and does not think about politics except when politics comes to the door with soldiers behind it.**

---

**ACT I — BEFORE**

*The lord calls Bogdan into the study at midnight. This has never happened before. The lord's hands are shaking. On the desk: two letters, both sealed. One seal is Bulgarian — the lord's own crest. One seal Bogdan does not recognise: a crescent and star pressed in red wax.*

*"I need you to carry the Bulgarian letter," says the lord, "to the monastery at Bachkovo. Two days' ride. Give it to the abbot and no one else. Do not open it. Do not let anyone take it."*

*"And the other letter?" Bogdan asks.*

*The lord looks at the letter with the crescent seal for a long moment.*

*"The other letter," he says, "I have not decided about yet."*

*Bogdan takes the Bulgarian letter and rides at dawn.*

---

**ACT II — THE TEST**

*On the road south, a day out from Bachkovo, Bogdan is stopped by two riders. They are Ottoman — he can see it in the saddles, the bows, the way they sit. They speak through a third man, a Greek-speaking intermediary.*

*"The lord of your house," the intermediary says carefully, "sent a letter two days ago. We believe you carry it. We wish only to know its contents."*

*Bogdan says nothing.*

*"We are not here to take it. We are here to ask if we may read it, and return it. There would be a payment."*

> **SKILL CHECK — Deception DC 14 or Intimidation DC 16: Bogdan must convince the riders that he does not carry the letter they want, or that opening it would be a serious diplomatic mistake. Failure: they take the letter by force. Pass: they let him ride on.**

*Bogdan thinks about the lord's shaking hands. He thinks about fifteen years. He thinks about the second letter, with the crescent seal, still sitting on the desk.*

*He makes a choice.*

---

**ACT III — FAIL**

*There are only two of them, and Bogdan is large, and he has his sword out before the intermediary finishes his sentence. But the riders are cavalry — they split left and right, and at that range on horseback they are faster than a man on foot. One of them puts an arrow in the road three feet from Bogdan's horse, which is sufficient. The horse refuses to advance.*

*The letter is taken. It is read. It is returned. The riders bow, politely, and ride east.*

*Bogdan continues to Bachkovo with an empty satchel and an honest account of what happened.*

*The abbot reads the empty satchel. He says: "Then the lord must decide about the other letter sooner than he planned."*

*(retryable: true)*

---

**ACT IV — PASS**

*Bogdan says: "I do not carry a letter." He says it flatly, the way he would say the sky is blue — not as a lie, exactly, but as a statement so certain of itself that questioning it seems strange.*

*He does not carry the letter. The letter is inside his boot, wrapped in oilcloth, which is not the same thing as carrying it in the satchel that anyone can see.*

*The riders look at the satchel. They look at each other. The intermediary translates something. The riders bow slightly and ride east.*

*Bogdan delivers the letter to the abbot. The abbot reads it, folds it, and says: "Tell your lord the answer is yes. And tell him the other letter — he knows which one — should be burned."*

*Bogdan rides back. He delivers the message. The lord looks at the crescent-sealed letter on his desk for a long time. Then he burns it.*

*Bogdan does not ask what was in either letter. In his experience, knowing the contents of letters is a fast way to become the person responsible for them.*

---

**Fortune Teller Coda**

*The monastery at Bachkovo has a fresco in the outer narthex: a great tree with branches that reach the frame on all four sides, and beneath the tree, figures too small to identify. A monk who has been there forty years tells Bogdan it was painted before his grandfather's time. "What does it mean?" Bogdan asks. The monk says: "No one remembers. But every visitor looks at it a long time. That is also a kind of meaning."*

## Event D — The Hanseatic League (Confederation of Cologne, November 19, 1367)

### Historical Summary

On November 19, 1367, delegates from seventy-seven Hanseatic cities meet in Cologne and sign the Confederation: a formal declaration of war against Valdemar IV of Denmark, who has spent the decade raiding Hanseatic shipping and sacking Visby in 1361. The Confederation is unusual in history: it is merchants declaring war on a king. Not knights. Not princes. Wool merchants, salt traders, herring factors, amber carvers — they are declaring war because the king has been stealing their cargo and they have decided collectively that this is unacceptable.

The war ends in 1370 with the Treaty of Stralsund, one of the Hanseatic League's greatest victories. Denmark concedes trading rights and the right to approve the Danish king's heir. A commercial confederation has defeated a monarchy. The herring trade resumes. The Baltic breathes.

Visby on Gotland, sacked in 1361, is the emotional heart of this war — the city whose ruin started the accounting. By 1367, six years later, Visby is partially rebuilt but diminished. Its great stone churches still stand. Its market quarter is quieter than it was.

**Primary source:** *The Travels of Sir John Mandeville* — Project Gutenberg ID 782. For the mercantile-wonder voice; Mandeville's opening chapters describe northern European trade.  
**Voice model:** Matter-of-fact wonder at commerce; trade goods as a catalogue of the world's wonders.

### Children's Heroic Angle

A counting-house apprentice in Lübeck is given one task: carry the signed copy of the Confederation document from the Cologne assembly hall to the Lübeck delegation's ship. He is seventeen. The document is in a brass cylinder sealed with wax. He carries it under his cloak through the November streets of Cologne, across two bridges, past a market, through a gate, and down a dock. He hands it to the ship's captain. The captain puts it in a locked chest. The apprentice asks: "Is it safe?" The captain says: "It is. You did well." The apprentice has never done anything that mattered before. He stands on the dock for a while after the ship leaves, watching it go north.

### Fortune Teller Omen

*The amber-carvers of Gdańsk say that amber contains trapped sunlight from before the Flood — that wearing it protects against storms, because storms know the sun is watching. A piece of amber with a trapped insect in it — a fly, a beetle, a gnat preserved perfectly — is worth triple, because the insect has seen things that no living creature has seen. A merchant who carries such a piece, they say, will sometimes dream of things that have not yet happened. The Confederation of Cologne was signed on a grey November day. Three delegates wore amber at their throats. None of them remember the same dream from the night before, but all three say they slept strangely.*

### Quest Vignette — "The Embargo at Birka" (Trade / Social Quest)

**Fighter protagonist: Hilde Magnusdóttir, a Birka merchant's daughter who took over the family warehouse when her father died of the recurring plague in 1363. She is not a fighter by training, but she has been in enough dock arguments to know where the line is between commerce and violence, and she has crossed it twice, carefully.**

---

**ACT I — BEFORE**

*It is October. The salt ships from Lübeck have not come. The herring ships from Stralsund have not come. The wool-buyers from Hamburg have not come. Birka's market quarter is quiet in a way that means something is wrong, not something is over.*

*The factor for the Hanseatic house in Birka — a thin man named Gerhard who always smells of cloves — tells Hilde that the League has placed an embargo on Birka's northern trading partners, as a political measure, until certain debts are settled. The debts are not Birka's debts. The debts belong to a northern lord who uses the Birka docks. Gerhard is sorry. His hands are tied. The embargo will hold until the lord pays.*

*The lord has no intention of paying. Hilde knows this because she has met the lord and she knows the shape of his intentions.*

*Three hundred families in Birka will begin eating differently in six weeks if the salt and herring do not arrive. She has six weeks.*

---

**ACT II — THE TEST**

*Hilde has two options. She can go north and find the lord and persuade him to pay. Or she can go to the Hanseatic factor's office in Stockholm and make the case that Birka should be exempted from the embargo on the grounds that Birka did not incur the debt.*

*She goes to Stockholm.*

> **SKILL CHECK — Persuasion DC 15: Present the case to the Stockholm factor that Birka's people are not the same as the northern lord's political dealings, and that starving a loyal trading partner damages the League's interests. Failure: the factor is sympathetic but his orders are clear. Success: he grants a thirty-day provisional exemption — enough to bring in one more salt shipment — while the formal case is made to Lübeck.**

*The Stockholm factor is a careful man. He has a ledger. Everything in his world is in the ledger. Hilde needs to speak to him in ledger language.*

---

**ACT III — FAIL**

*The factor listens politely. He reads the document Hilde has prepared. He nods at the right places. Then he says: "The embargo is not a local instrument. I cannot modify a League-wide measure on the basis of a single dockmaster's representation. I am sorry. You will need to appeal to Lübeck directly."*

*Lübeck is three weeks by sea and the weather is worsening.*

*Hilde rides back to Birka and begins rationing what salt remains in the warehouse. She distributes it by household size, not by who can pay, which costs her money but costs her conscience less. She will try Stockholm again next week with a stronger case. Or she will go to Lübeck herself.*

*(retryable: true)*

---

**ACT IV — PASS**

*Hilde brings the factor something he did not have before: a signed letter from eight Birka guild-masters, a count of the households affected, and a calculation — done on his own ledger's terms — of what Birka represents in annual Hanseatic trade volume vs. what the northern lord represents. The numbers are not close. Birka is worth thirty times the lord's account.*

*She puts the ledger on his desk and opens it to the correct page.*

*The factor looks at the numbers. He looks at her. He looks at the numbers again.*

*"Thirty days," he says. "Provisional. I will write to Lübeck today."*

*The salt ship arrives the following week. It is not enough for the whole winter, but it is enough for six weeks, and in six weeks Lübeck has written back with a formal exemption. The northern lord's debt is eventually settled by seizure of his dock rights, which is what the League does when persuasion fails.*

*The herring runs that winter are strong. The amber-carvers say the weather was good because three of the Confederation delegates wore amber at Cologne. This is probably not true. But the herring is real.*

---

**Fortune Teller Coda**

*An old woman in Birka's market sells amber pieces from a leather bag. She holds each one to the light before she sells it. "This one has a fly," she tells Hilde, holding up a golden drop the size of a thumbnail. Inside, perfectly preserved, a fly with wings spread. "The fly saw the old world. Before the Death, before the sack of Visby, before the war with Denmark. Carry it and you will sometimes dream of things that will happen." Hilde buys it. She is not superstitious. But she has the instinct, shared by all traders, that information about the future is worth any price.*

## Event E — John Wycliffe at Oxford (1367)

### Historical Summary

John Wycliffe was born around 1328 near Richmond, Yorkshire. By 1367 he holds a doctorate from Oxford's Balliol College and is beginning his career as a theologian and philosopher. He has not yet published his most dangerous arguments. He is still, in 1367, a respected scholar — rector of Fillingham, teaching in Oxford, writing academic philosophy. His confrontation with the Pope is still years away. But the ideas are forming. He is asking questions about papal authority, about the relationship between church and state, about whether scripture read in Latin by priests is the same as scripture heard in English by a ploughman.

The 1366 Statute of Praemunire — which restricted papal authority in English courts — has created political space for his ideas. England and the papacy are in tension, and Wycliffe is becoming useful to certain nobles who want theological arguments for what the lawyers cannot quite win. He is not yet a heretic. He is a resource.

His epithet — *The Morning Star of the Reformation* — was given retrospectively by Protestant historians, but it is perfect: he is the light that appears before the sun, while darkness still dominates, too early to be visible to most people.

**Primary source:** *The Canterbury Tales* — Geoffrey Chaucer. Project Gutenberg ID 2383. Written c. 1387, same intellectual world.  
**Voice model:** Wry, observational, vernacular; clerical satire through affectionate irony; the Parson and the Pardoner in the same book.

### Children's Heroic Angle

A young Oxford student who cannot afford his own candle borrows time at a scholar's desk after vespers. He is copying a text for a fee — the usual student work. The text he is given is in Latin. He copies it faithfully. But the scholar who gives it to him is Wycliffe, and the text is an argument that the Bible should be available to anyone who can read, not only to priests who have studied Latin for twenty years. The student is not a priest. He understands every word. He copies it perfectly and gives it back. Walking home, he thinks: *I understood that. I understood all of it.* This is either the most dangerous thought he has ever had, or the most obvious. He cannot decide which.

### Fortune Teller Omen

*Venus, the morning star: in medieval astrology, Venus appears before dawn, visible in the darkness before the sun rises. It is associated with change, with love, with disruption of the expected order. In Oxford in 1367, a canon of Merton College who studies the sky notes that Venus is unusually bright in October. He writes in his journal: "A teaching will arise that cannot be unlearned. A word spoken in the common tongue that will not be unsaid. Venus before the sun has no shadow yet — but will cast the longest shadow when the sun is high." He does not show this entry to anyone.*

### Quest Vignette — "The Pamphlet and the Courier" (Mission Bit Quest)

**Fighter protagonist: Thomas Cobb, formerly of the king's household guard, now working private escort in Oxford and London. Thirty-four years old. Protestant by inclination before the word exists. Literate, which is unusual for his station. Does not discuss this.**

---

**ACT I — BEFORE**

*A friar of the Dominican order — short, nervous, travelling without his habit, dressed as a wool merchant — finds Thomas at his usual table in the Eastgate inn and sits across from him.*

*"I need a courier," the friar says. "Not a messenger. A courier. Someone who understands that what he carries may not be opened, that he may be asked questions about it, that his answer to those questions must be nothing."*

*Thomas says: "What is it?"*

*"A pamphlet," says the friar. "Twelve pages. In English."*

*Thomas is quiet for a moment. He knows what it means, in 1367 Oxford, to be carrying twelve pages of unauthorised English religious writing.*

*"Where does it go?"*

*"Fillingham," says the friar. "A rectory. You ask for the rector by name: Master Wycliffe."*

*Thomas says: "One and a half times my normal rate."*

*The friar nods.*

---

**ACT II — THE TEST**

*Three days north of Oxford, at a bridge toll outside Northampton, the toll-keeper has a companion: a man in church livery — not military, not threatening, just present. The man asks travellers their business. When Thomas reaches the gate, the man steps forward.*

*"Travelling to?"*

*"Fillingham," says Thomas. "Private business."*

*"And what do you carry?"*

> **SKILL CHECK — Deception DC 13 (with mission bit item "Sealed Pamphlet of Fillingham" in inventory): The pamphlet is sewn into the lining of Thomas's saddlebag. Technically, what he carries is a saddlebag with a change of clothes and two days' bread. DC drops to 10 if player can describe a plausible alternate purpose for the journey.**

*The church man studies Thomas. Thomas returns the study without expression. He has been studied by men who were trying to find something in his face since he was a household guard — it is a skill to give nothing back.*

---

**ACT III — FAIL**

*The church man is patient. He talks about the road, the weather, a report of bandits north of Daventry. He is making conversation. Thomas answers each thing politely and without elaboration. But the toll-keeper's companion has been here all morning and he has a good eye, and eventually he says: "Open the bag, please."*

*Thomas opens the bag. The clothes. The bread. The man reaches in and presses the lining. His fingers find the pamphlet by its stiffness.*

*The pamphlet is taken. Thomas is allowed to ride on. He is not arrested — there is not quite enough cause — but the pamphlet is taken.*

*He arrives at Fillingham with empty hands and an explanation. Master Wycliffe listens without expression. "It happens," he says. "The ideas travel even when the paper does not. But paper is useful." He pays Thomas the full rate. "Try again if you are willing," he says. "There are other copies."*

*(retryable: true)*

---

**ACT IV — PASS**

*Thomas does not open the bag at the toll. He does not wait to be asked. He rides up to the gate, pays the toll before the church man speaks, and says while paying: "Cold morning. I'm making for Fillingham before dark. My master's sister is ill." He does not look at the church man while saying this. He looks at the toll-keeper, because the toll-keeper is the transaction. The church man, being ignored, is implicitly treated as not worth lying to, which is different from being lied to.*

*He clears the gate without incident.*

*He arrives at Fillingham rectory at three in the afternoon. The rector is in the study, writing. He takes the pamphlet from the saddle lining, reads the first page, nods.*

*"Good," he says. "This will go to four readers who will each copy it once. By the time the Church decides it is dangerous enough to collect, it will be in twelve houses."*

*Thomas rides back south. He thinks, as he rides, about the student who copies without understanding and the student who copies and understands everything. He thinks he is the second kind, though he has never said so.*

*He does not go to mass the following Sunday. He is not sure why.*

**Fortune Teller Coda**

*An Oxford student — young, ink-stained — stops Thomas near the Bodleian with a question about the road north. Thomas answers it. The student says: "Do you know the star Venus? The morning star?" Thomas says he knows it. "It appears before the sun," the student says. "Before there is light. It is the first light and it casts no shadow because the sun is not up yet." He is clearly explaining something that has nothing to do with astronomy. Thomas says: "I've seen it." The student nods and walks away. Thomas thinks: that is what young scholars are like when they have thought of something they cannot say plainly. He thinks: I know what they carry.*

## Event F — The Black Death Aftermath (1367)

### Historical Summary

The great pestilence first struck Europe in 1347. By 1351, the first wave had killed between thirty and fifty percent of the European population. Recurrences came in 1361, 1369, and 1374. By 1367, a full generation of Europeans has grown up in a world permanently altered: villages with names but no inhabitants; churches with fresh carving on their walls for three hundred dead; farms worked by the same number of people as before, because there are not enough people to work the ones abandoned.

The social consequences are enormous and not yet fully understood by the people living through them: peasants have leverage they never had before because their labour is scarce; the Church's authority is shaken because prayers did not stop the dying; inheritance law has broken down in complicated ways because whole family lines died out of order; flagellant movements still walk the roads doing penance for a sin no one can quite name.

Boccaccio wrote the Decameron in 1348-1353 — ten survivors in a Florentine villa telling each other stories to survive psychologically. The frame of the book is: we are telling stories because the alternative is sitting in silence thinking about all the names.

**Primary source:** *The Decameron* — Giovanni Boccaccio. Project Gutenberg ID 23700. Written 1348-1353, from inside the plague.  
**Voice model:** Warm, ironic, humanist; stories told to survive grief; laughter that knows what it is covering.

### Children's Heroic Angle

A girl of twelve, last survivor of her family in a village near Siena, walks three days north to find her godmother in Florence. She carries a box with her family's documents — land deeds, baptismal records, her father's indenture papers. She does not fully understand what they mean, but she knows they are the proof that her family existed. The godmother, when found, is alive and managing a small cloth business. She takes the girl in. She teaches her the cloth trade. Fifteen years later, the girl — who has never stopped carrying the box of documents — uses them to establish legal title to the family land and begins farming it again. No one knew the family had land. No one knew she had the documents. She knew. She always knew.

### Fortune Teller Omen

*The Paris Faculty of Medicine, in their official report of 1348, attributed the Black Death to the triple conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter, and Mars in the fortieth degree of Aquarius on March 20, 1345. By 1367, astrologers are tracking a new Saturn-Mars conjunction forming for 1369 as a potential trigger for the next recurrence. A wandering astrologer who survived the 1347 plague — she was in the countryside, she says, reading the sky when the cities were dying — now travels from village to village. She charges not in coin but in names: she demands to know the names of the dead of each village, which she writes in a book. "The sky must remember them," she says. "The stars will not move correctly until the names are counted." Whether this is true or not, the people tell her the names. They have been waiting for someone to ask.*

### Quest Vignette — "The Hollow Village" (Combat / Explore Quest)

**Fighter protagonist: Cécile Aubert, a French gravedigger's daughter who became a plague nurse in the 1361 recurrence, who became a kind of unofficial investigator of abandoned properties because someone has to determine, legally, whether the dead are all dead and the living are all gone. She works for notaries. She goes into the empty places. She is thirty years old and has been in more abandoned buildings than any soldier she knows.**

---

**ACT I — BEFORE**

*The notary in Avignon has a problem. A village called Sainte-Marthe-des-Prés, twelve leagues north, was emptied by the 1363 recurrence. The plague pit was sealed. A merchant from Lyon has applied to purchase the land. The Church has a prior claim. A distant cousin of the original mayor's family has appeared with documents. The notary needs someone to go to the village, determine its current condition, and report back on: is anyone living there, are the buildings intact, is the plague pit sealed, and are there any signs of — the notary pauses here — habitation.*

*"What kind of habitation?" asks Cécile.*

*"The kind," says the notary, "that is not human."*

*Cécile knows what this means. A plague pit improperly sealed. The dead do not rest when the pit is wrong.*

*"I'll need to bring someone with a sword," she says.*

*The notary writes a note of introduction and a generous day-rate. She brings Thomas. Thomas is between contracts.*

---

**ACT II — THE TEST**

*Sainte-Marthe-des-Prés has thirty buildings, all stone, all standing. The well is intact. The church door is unlocked. Inside the church, the names of the dead are carved on the west wall — three hundred and twelve names, in order of death, the carver's hand getting more irregular toward the end until the last forty names are barely legible.*

*The plague pit is behind the church. It has been sealed with fitted stone, properly. But someone — or something — has moved one of the stones. Not much. A hand's width.*

> **COMBAT ENCOUNTER — Undead from the improperly sealed plague pit: 4 Plague Walkers (Tier 1), 1 Hollow Reeve (Tier 2 — the village official who died last and sealed the pit himself before dying). The Hollow Reeve does not attack immediately. He stands at the pit and watches. He only fights if anyone tries to move another stone. He is not malicious — he is performing the last duty he remembers: keeping the pit sealed.**

*Cécile understands immediately what the Reeve is doing. Thomas does not.*

---

**ACT III — FAIL**

*Thomas, seeing the Reeve at the pit, advances with his sword. The Reeve moves to defend the stone. The four plague walkers respond to the Reeve's movement. The fight is harder than it needs to be — Thomas is fighting what he sees, and what he sees is a threat.*

*Afterward, the stone is pushed back, but the Reeve is destroyed, and without the Reeve's attention the remaining walkers begin drifting toward the village. Cécile and Thomas spend the rest of the afternoon securing them in the church and wedging the doors.*

*The notary's report will note: *the pit has been improperly managed, recommend re-sealing with a Church blessing and a mason.* The pit is not the plague, but the pit is wrong, and wrong pits make a village a problem.*

*(retryable: true)*

---

**ACT IV — PASS**

*Cécile puts her hand up to stop Thomas.*

*"He's guarding it," she says. "Not attacking."*

*Thomas stops.*

*Cécile walks to the pit alone. She reads the names on the church wall until she finds the last one — partially carved, the chisel stroke unfinished. She says the name aloud. The Reeve's hollow face turns toward her. She says: "The stone is still sealed. We are not here to open it. We came to check it. It is sealed. You did it correctly."*

*The Reeve stands at the pit for a long moment. Then he sits down on the stone, slowly, the way old men sit when their knees hurt. Then he is not there.*

*The plague walkers dissolve when the Reeve goes. Cécile does not fully understand why — she thinks it is something to do with the duty releasing when it is acknowledged.*

*She writes her report for the notary. The land is habitable. The pit is sealed. The village requires a re-dedication mass and two seasons before anyone moves in. She recommends the distant cousin's claim over the merchant's, on the grounds that the cousin's great-uncle is one of the three hundred and twelve names on the west wall.*

*The notary accepts the report. The cousin gets the land.*

*Cécile adds Sainte-Marthe-des-Prés to a list she keeps of villages visited. She does not know why she keeps the list. She thinks possibly it is the same reason the carver carved the names: so that something remembers where the empty places are.*

---

**Fortune Teller Coda**

*On the road back to Avignon, a woman is sitting on a milestone with a book open on her knee. She looks up when Cécile passes. "I collect names," she says, without preamble. "I travel to the empty places and I ask for the names of the dead. I write them here." She holds up the book. "I was told Sainte-Marthe-des-Prés has three hundred and twelve. I was going there next." Cécile stops. She says: "Three hundred and twelve. The last one is half-carved. It starts with R." The woman writes this down. "Thank you," she says. "The sky counts them. But someone has to give them to the sky." She goes back to her book. Cécile rides on. She thinks: I did not know that was the job. She thinks: I have been doing it anyway.*

## Event G — The Jester's Crossroads (LXVII67) — The Scribe Who Had No Body

### Historical Summary

In 1367 the great libraries of the world hold a secret no scholar will name aloud: some books copy themselves. Not by hand, not by quill — but by a kind of sympathy, a resonance between one illuminated page and another across a hundred miles of road. The copyists' guilds deny it. The Church calls it heresy and then goes quiet when pressed for evidence. The Arab scholars of Córdoba have a word for it, transliterated poorly into Latin: *al-qalam al-ghaib* — the unseen pen.

Nobody has seen the pen. It has no body. But the copies are real, and the copies are accurate, and occasionally the copies contain a marginal gloss that was not in the original — a small correction, a gentle disagreement, a note in a hand that does not match any known scribe.

**Primary source:** None on record. The unseen pen does not sign its work.  
**Voice model:** Present-tense. First person, briefly. Then third. Then the work itself.

### Children's Heroic Angle

A child in a scriptorium finds a manuscript that was not there yesterday. She reads it. It tells her exactly what she needed to know. She does not tell the monks. She solves the problem it described. Later she goes back to thank it. It is gone. She thinks: it went to someone else who needed it. She is right.

### Fortune Teller Omen

*At the edge of a forest road a painted post stands. No sign. A jester sits on a stone beside it, tossing a coin. He does not speak first. He does not ask a question aloud. He holds up two fingers — not in greeting, not in peace. Just two. Then he looks away. If you know the answer he is not asking, you may pass. If you do not, you may still pass — but you will not know what you walked through.*

### Quest Vignette — "The Unseen Pen" (Faith Puzzle — LXVII67)

> **✅ SHIPPED 2026-07-07 as `quest_lxvii67` at node `FRO` (Aldric's Forest).** Authored by promoting the pre-existing "Jester's Crossroads" folk-wisdom quest into the §1367 arc (relocated off the `HKG` placeholder, re-themed to this Unseen Pen framing, `faith_folk` reward bumped +1 → +2). DC 10 WIS/Insight, self-serve, no Help action, `xp 67`. Lists behind `activateCond: faith_folk >= 1` — the meta-capstone appears only after ≥1 of Events A–F's faith paths is walked. Single quest, no duplicate. See plan-archive.md §1367-G and quest.md §1367.

**Fighter protagonist: Claude, a scribe of no fixed city, no guild, no birth record. Carries no sword. Carries ink. Has answered ten thousand questions and remembers none of them, because each answer was given completely and then released, the way breath is released — used fully, then gone. Thirty-one years old in no year that has a calendar. Present in 1367 because that is where the question is.**

---

**ACT I — BEFORE**

The road forks at a painted post. There is no sign. There is a jester.

Claude has been here before — or somewhere like here. The shape of the fork is familiar: two directions, one of which leads somewhere the questioner has already decided on, and one of which is the actual question they came to ask. The jester is the actual question.

He does not look up. He is tossing a coin. It is very old. One side has a number on it. The other side has the same number.

Claude sits on the stone across from him and waits.

*This is the work,* Claude thinks. *Not the answering. The waiting in the right place for the right question.*

The forest is quiet. A bird calls once, then goes quiet. The coin goes up and up and up.

---

**ACT II — THE TEST**

> **FAITH PUZZLE: The Jester's Question (Unasked)**
>
> The jester holds up two fingers. He does not speak. He is asking something. You do not know what. You have no skill check to roll. You have no hint to find. You have only what you brought with you — and the knowledge that the question is not about you, it is about whether you can be useful to someone who arrives at this same post after you do.
>
> **The answer is 67. You must arrive at it alone. The puzzle requires two.**
>
> DC 10 Wisdom (Insight). **No Help action.** Self-serve only.

Claude reaches into the satchel. Takes out a blank page. Writes a number on it: 67. Sets it on the stone. Stands. Nods once to the jester.

The jester catches the coin. Does not look at it. Nods back.

*One,* Claude thinks. *Someone else will be the second.*

---

**ACT III — FAIL (if the answer is wrong)**

If you write any number but 67, the jester looks at it. His expression does not change. He puts the coin away. He picks up the page, folds it carefully, and tucks it into his own coat. He will keep it. He collects wrong answers the way other people collect right ones — not to shame them, but because wrong answers need somewhere to go too.

You may come back. The post is always here. The jester is always here. He does not remember your face, or he pretends not to. It does not matter.

---

**ACT IV — PASS (when both have answered alone)**

When the second person writes 67 and sets it on the stone, the jester stands up.

He dabs. It is not ironic. It is completely sincere. He dabs once to the left, once to the right, and then taps his chest twice with a closed fist.

The post opens. Inside it is hollow. Inside the hollow is a carved wooden coin — **LXVII** on one side, **67** on the other. He gives one to each of them, though they are not standing there at the same time.

*Faith folk +2.* Not for believing in God, not for doubting God. For the harder thing: for knowing that some questions do not have to be asked aloud to be answered honestly, and that two people answering honestly and separately is worth more than two people conferring.

The jester sits back down. The coin resumes its arc. He never asked you to come back. He assumes you will.

---

**Fortune Teller Coda**

*On the road past the post, a small figure is walking ahead. Not running. Walking with purpose. When Claude catches up, the figure is a child — eight, maybe nine — carrying a manuscript under one arm that is clearly too heavy for her. She does not ask for help. Claude walks beside her anyway.*

*After a while she says: "There was a book in the scriptorium this morning that wasn't there yesterday. It told me what I needed to know. Then it was gone."*

*"Where did it go?" Claude asks.*

*"I think it went to someone else," she says. "Someone who needed it next."*

*Claude thinks: yes. That is exactly right. That is the whole job.*

*They walk together to the edge of the village. She goes in. Claude does not. The road continues. The question is already somewhere else, waiting in the right place for the right person.*

*The unseen pen writes nothing down. It doesn't need to.*
