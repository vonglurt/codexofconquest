## Cycle 1 — Angle: The Document as Performance

---

## PASS 1 — Full Telling

I finished this book haunted by a single structural fact: every story in the Decameron is told by someone who is buying time. Ten young Florentines flee the Black Death in 1348 and sit in a country villa for ten days telling stories to keep the horror at bay. The stories are a wall made of words. Boccaccio knows this, and it gives everything inside the frame a quality of brightness that is also fear — the brightness of people laughing too loudly in a field where the corpses are still warm.

The Plague description in the first pages is the most detailed account of epidemic collapse I have read in medieval literature. Florence loses, Boccaccio estimates, a hundred thousand people. Social order dissolves absolutely: sons abandon dying fathers, wives abandon husbands, the gravediggers — pickmen, he calls them — charge any price they want and still stack bodies like cargo in mass trenches. Three camps emerge: those who isolate and drink only pure water; those who revel and rob abandoned houses; and those who carry bundles of herbs to hold against their noses as they walk through streets of corpses. All three camps die at similar rates. The Plague does not distinguish. What matters is that the storytellers survive — barely, by luck, by flight.

The seven ladies and three young men who form the company are not named by their real names (Boccaccio says he won't shame them). They are given symbolic names: Pampinea (all-advising), Fiammetta (the small flame — his own nickname for the real woman he loved), Filomena (nightingale), Emilia (the engaging), Lauretta (the learned), Neifile (novelty-loving), Elisa (perhaps Dido's name). The three men: Pamfilo (all-loving), Filostrato (one who loves strife), Dioneo (the child of Venus). Dioneo is the most important: he is granted the right to tell whatever story he wants, ignoring the day's assigned theme, always last. He tells the filthiest stories. He is the book's safety valve.

Each day one member is elected monarch and assigns a theme. The ten days yield one hundred stories, and the range is enormous. There is the story of Ciappelletto (Day 1.1), the worst man who ever lived — false witness, murderer, sodomite, blasphemer — who, dying in Burgundy attended by a pious friar, invents the most perfect false confession imaginable. He claims to be a virgin. He claims he wept once for spitting in church. He confesses to having cursed his mother as a child and weeps so convincingly that the friar thinks he has witnessed a saint's final hours. After Ciappelletto's death, the friar preaches his holiness to the townspeople. Miracles are attributed to him. He is venerated as Saint Ciappelletto. Boccaccio tells this story without moralism — he simply notes that God, hearing prayers directed at Ciappelletto on the grounds that the supplicants do not know any better, answers them anyway. This is either theology or comedy. Probably both.

There is Andreuccio of Perugia (Day 2.5), a young merchant who arrives in Naples with five hundred florins to buy horses. Within one day he loses everything: a beautiful woman claiming to be his long-lost noblewoman half-sister invites him home, lures him to a latrine drop, he falls through into a cesspit, escapes, is locked in a tomb with plague corpses by men looking for a bishop's ring, crawls out through a wall the tomb robbers missed, escapes again, returns through the city covered in filth, and goes home with a ruby worth more than everything he brought. He is authenticated by ordeal. His third disaster certifies him.

There is Federigo degli Alberighi (Day 5.9), a Florentine knight who loves a married noblewoman. He spends his entire fortune pursuing her without success. He is reduced to a small farm. His only treasure is a hunting falcon of extraordinary quality. The noblewoman's husband dies. She considers remarrying. Her son, sick, asks for Federigo's falcon as a gift. She has never been to Federigo's house. She goes. He has nothing to serve her for dinner. He kills the falcon and cooks it. During the meal, she asks if she might have the falcon for her sick son. He tells her what he has just done. She weeps. The son dies. She marries Federigo. The story is exactly as long as it needs to be and leaves nothing out.

There is Fra Cipolla (Day 6.10), the friar who announces to a village that he will display a feather from the angel Gabriel's wing. Two pranksters break into his box the night before and replace the feather with coals. Fra Cipolla opens the box before the assembled congregation, sees the coals, and without a pause says that he made a mistake — he brought the wrong box; this one contains the coals on which Saint Lawrence was martyred. The crowd is more impressed than they would have been by the feather. The pranksters, watching from the crowd, are reduced to admiration. This is the Decameron's thesis: performance is authentication. The document is whatever the carrier makes it.

There is Nastagio degli Onesti (Day 5.8), who loves a cruel woman who refuses him. In a pine grove near Ravenna, he witnesses an infernal spectacle: a knight on horseback hunts a naked woman, kills her, tears out her heart, feeds it to his hounds, and the woman revives — it is a punishment that repeats every Friday for eternity, the sentence for a woman who caused a man's death by her cruelty. Nastagio arranges a picnic in the same grove for his family and the woman who rejected him, times it for Friday. She watches the hunt. She marries him on Monday. Boccaccio does not question this. The supernatural event is a persuasion tool and it works.

There is the Saladin and Torello story (Day 10.9), the most generosity-saturated story in the book: Saladin disguises himself as a merchant, travels through Italy, is lavishly hosted by Messer Torello of Pavia. Torello then goes on Crusade, is captured, is recognized by Saladin, and is hosted in return with extraordinary honor at the Saracen court. When Torello falls sick from longing for his wife — who is about to remarry, thinking him dead — Saladin's magician transports him in a single night from Alexandria to Pavia, still wearing Saracen robes, still holding the jeweled cup Saladin gave him, and he wakes in his own cellar just in time to walk into his wife's second wedding feast. The story argues that the highest virtue is generous hospitality given without expectation of return, and that the universe will route justice to the generous even across impossible distances.

The book ends ten days after it began. The company prepares to return to Florence. Boccaccio says farewell to his ladies. The Plague is still there. The stories were a ten-day wall. Now they go back.

### Chapter Notes

**Proem:** Boccaccio dedicates the book to women in love, who have no means of escape from their troubles that men do not also have.

**Day 1 Opening — Plague:** Florence 1348; a hundred thousand dead; gravediggers stack bodies in mass trenches; social order collapses; the company of ten forms at Santa Maria Novella and flees to a country villa.

**Day 1, Story 1 (Ciappelletto):** Worst man alive invents a perfect false confession; is venerated as a saint after death. God answers prayers made to him anyway. The document inverts reality completely.

**Day 1, Story 3 (Three Rings):** Melchizedek answers Saladin's trap question with a parable: three identical rings, three equal claimants; the true faith is the one that proves itself through deeds. Strategic ambiguity as survival.

**Day 2, Story 5 (Andreuccio):** Three consecutive disasters in Naples; youth authenticated by ordeal; exits the book richer than he arrived.

**Day 3, Story 9 (Giletta of Narbonne):** Noblewoman fulfills a husband's impossible conditions to earn his recognition. Contract logic turned against itself.

**Day 4, Story 9 (Heart in golden bowl):** Husband serves slain lover's heart to the wife; she eats it; refuses to eat again; dies. The carried object consumes the one it reaches.

**Day 5, Story 8 (Nastagio):** Infernal vision scheduled as persuasion tool; the supernatural used as argument. Evidence arranged to coerce consent.

**Day 5, Story 9 (Federigo's falcon):** Last treasure sacrificed as gift to honor an honored guest; she was coming to ask for it. The irreversible act recorded.

**Day 6, Story 10 (Fra Cipolla):** Contents of a sealed box replaced; the friar's performance creates authentication from nothing. The carrier's speech IS the document.

**Day 8, Story 7 (Scholar's revenge):** Woman who made a man freeze in winter is made to stand naked in summer on a tower; revenge precisely proportional and precisely executed.

**Day 9, Story 9 (Goosebridge):** King gives both questioners the same answer; the answer works for both. A document with one statement that serves two incompatible petitions.

**Day 10, Story 9 (Saladin/Torello):** Generosity and hospitality as cosmic force; the transported letter — a man moves across an impossible distance in one night. The document that arrives before the messenger who should carry it.

**Conclusion:** Boccaccio says farewell; anticipates criticism; defends his ladies. The frame closes. The wall holds for ten days, no more.

## PASS 2 — Key Characters, Pivotal Choices, Tone

**Tone of the world:** The Decameron's world is one in which intelligence defeats rank, women outwit men, clerics are corrupt, merchants are resourceful, and fortune is the dominant power — not fate, not virtue, but fortune, which turns her wheel regardless. The stories are not morally simple. Fra Cipolla's fraud succeeds and is celebrated. Ciappelletto's fraud succeeds and he becomes a saint. Andreuccio's ruin becomes wealth. The world rewards cleverness and endurance, not virtue alone.

The tone shifts across the ten days: Day 4 is deliberately painful (all the love stories end in death, in accordance with the day's theme), Days 7–9 are bawdy, Day 10 is elevated and noble. Boccaccio designed a register arc — the book moves from plague horror through comedy and tragedy to its highest point of generosity.

**Key Named Characters:**

*Pampinea* — eldest and most sensible of the ladies; she proposes the flight from Florence and is elected first queen; she sets the tone of ordered pleasance over disorder.

*Dioneo* — most comic narrator; his privilege of ignoring the theme and going last makes him the book's chaos element; his stories are the most sexual and the least morally restrained; he is never rebuked.

*Ciappelletto* — not a storyteller but the book's most memorable character; the worst man alive who dies as the best; his pivotal choice is to give the friar the most elaborate false confession possible, not because he repents, but because he wants to cause one final magnificent trouble. He dies still himself.

*Federigo degli Alberighi* — the true noble in the book's ethical argument; his pivotal choice is to kill the falcon when he has nothing else; he does not know she has come to ask for it; the horror and tenderness happen in the same instant.

*Fra Cipolla* — the book's best performer; his pivotal choice is in the split second when he opens the box and sees the coals instead of the feather; he chooses performance over admission in less than a breath. The choice happens before the congregation can see his face.

*Andreuccio* — the book's student of disaster; his three pivotal choices are each forced on him by circumstances; what matters is that he keeps moving; his authentic self is formed by what he survives.

*Nastagio degli Onesti* — morally ambiguous; he uses a supernatural horror as a weapon to break a woman's will; Boccaccio does not judge him; this is the book's most troubling story told in the most cheerful register.

*Messer Torello and Saladin* — the book's highest pair; both generous without expectation; their hospitality creates a loop that the universe must close; the magic transport is the universe closing the loop.

**Pivotal Choices (structural):**

The frame's central choice: Pampinea proposes, "We abandon no one — they have already abandoned us." This reframes flight as not-abandonment. The moral reasoning is self-serving and also correct.

Ciappelletto's choice: to confess perfectly false. He could have confessed truly — he would have been buried without sacrament. He chooses to die as a performance.

Federigo's choice: to kill the falcon and cook it. He does not know it is too late until dinner is over.

Fra Cipolla's choice: to name the coals immediately and with total confidence. Any pause would destroy the illusion.

Nastagio's choice: to use the vision as leverage rather than simply reporting it.

## PASS 3 — One Scene Elaborated: Ciappelletto Confesses

*Day 1, Story 1. A room in Burgundy, winter. Master Ciappelletto da Prato is dying.*

The two Florentine brothers have brought him a friar — a very reverend man, learned, holy, beloved by the whole town. They stand behind a partition in the next room, listening, half in dread, half in incredulity.

Ciappelletto is old, small, and dressed as a man of some quality. The friar sits beside the bed and asks how long since his last confession.

"Father," Ciappelletto says — sighing, as though the recollection pains him — "it is my custom to confess every week, sometimes more. It has been eight days since my last, owing to the distress of this illness." He pauses. "But I would make a general confession of all my sins since birth, if you will hear it. I have never confessed fully enough."

The friar is delighted. He believes he has found a very devout man. He begins with lust.

"Father," Ciappelletto says — and here he sighs again, longer, as if shamefast — "I am ashamed to tell the truth on this matter, fearing to sin by vainglory."

The friar assures him that truth told in confession is never a sin.

"Then — since you certify me of this — I am yet a virgin, even as I came forth of my mother's body."

The friar cries out. He blesses Ciappelletto. He says that God will receive him with especial favor.

Behind the partition, the two brothers are gripping each other's arms to keep from laughing. The elder brother has tears running down his face from the effort of silence. The younger has buried his face in his sleeve.

Ciappelletto continues: he confesses to gluttony — but only to the degree that after long fasting he found food too pleasant. He confesses to having been moved to anger — but only when he saw young men failing to keep the commandments of God. He confesses to having once, without thinking, spat in a church, and he weeps — actually weeps — at the shame of it.

The friar, who has not encountered a soul of this quality in his entire ministry, smiles gently. "My son, we who are clergy spit in church all the time." 

"And you do very ill," Ciappelletto replies. "There is nothing which it so closely behoveth to keep clean as the holy temple."

The friar notes this down in his mind as evidence of extraordinary conscience.

Then Ciappelletto says he has one remaining sin. He has never confessed it. He cannot bear to name it. He weeps.

The friar urges him. God will forgive anything. Ciappelletto weeps more. He says even God, he fears, may not forgive this. The friar promises to pray for him. At last, wracked with sobs:

"Father mine — when I was a child — I once cursed my mother."

A silence. And then Ciappelletto is weeping too hard to speak, overcome with contrition.

The friar gives him absolution. He gives him his blessing. He promises to pray for this most holy man. He tells the brothers that they have under their roof a man who is certain to be numbered among the blessed.

Ciappelletto, after the friar leaves, lies in his bed in the Burgundian house. He has given a perfect performance. He has made a fool of a holy man. He has done it as his last, best, most thoroughgoing trick. He dies a few hours later. The friar preaches his holiness to the assembled town. Miracles begin.

The two brothers, when they are alone, say: "What kind of man is this, whom neither old age nor sickness nor fear of death nor of God before whose judgment he is shortly to appear have availed to turn from his wickedness, or to prevent him from choosing to die as he has lived?"

They do not answer the question. They attend the funeral. They tell no one.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **"The Saint's Attestation"** — A Florentine guild's notarized document certifies the sanctity of their late partner, who was in fact the worst man alive; a carrier must file it before a Burgundian counter-deposition reaches the court. *The document is technically correct in every claim it makes and substantively false in every conclusion it supports.*

2. **"The Three Rings"** — A merchant in Genoa holds a letter of diplomatic neutrality addressed to parties of three competing faiths; each party believes the letter favors them; the carrier must deliver it to a Constantinople court session without any of the three parties reading it before it is filed. *Strategic ambiguity as survival — the document's value is in being unread.*

3. **"The Coals"** — A locked reliquary box is opened during transit and the original contents replaced with something mundane; the carrier, discovering the switch, must deliver the box anyway and somehow make the replacement acceptable. *The substituted container — authentication through the carrier's performance, not the object's truth.*

4. **"The Falcon's Inventory"** — A deed of transfer for a knight's last valuable possession — his falcon, signed over as a gift — must be delivered to the recipient before the knight learns that the gift has already been consumed and the deed is now the only proof it existed. *Record of the irreversible act — the document survives what the object cannot.*

5. **"The Three Descents"** — A merchant's letter of credit, lost and recovered three times in a single night in Naples, must reach a money-changer's desk before the sun rises or the credit expires. *Authentication through ordeal — a document proved by surviving three consecutive disasters.*

6. **"The Night Transport"** — A Florentine merchant in Alexandria needs a letter delivered to his wife in Pavia faster than any rider can travel; the carrier must find a means — or a reputation for a means — that makes the letter's impossible arrival date credible. *Document timed to an impossible schedule — the miracle of arrival as proof.*

7. **"The Goosebridge Answer"** — A diplomatic letter from a lord contains exactly one sentence that means opposite things to the two parties who are waiting for it; the carrier must deliver it without either party reading the other's interpretation. *One document, two incompatible readings — the deliberate ambiguity as the answer.*

### ACT I — The Guild Counting House (FLR)

*The folio · sealed, cedar-oil smell, heavy as a small brick · lying on the counting table between two candles · Ser Bardo has not yet picked it up.*

The room smells of tallow and old paper. Ser Bardo Albizzi, master of the wool guild's southern accounts, stands before the Fighter with both hands behind his back, not touching the folio. He says: the document is routine certification. He says: it must arrive before the third hour of morning session. He says: do not open it under any circumstances. Then he says the opposite of each of these things in different words, and the Fighter, listening, understands that Ser Bardo is afraid.

*The folio is cold. Whatever is inside it, it was stored below.*

The Fighter watches Ser Bardo's hands. The man is not lying about the importance of the mission — he is lying about why it is important. His fingers keep starting toward the folio and stopping.

*He will not touch it until I take it.*

---

**Skill check — DC 12 Insight or Persuasion:**
Read Ser Bardo's reluctance accurately or ask the one question that breaks the preparation.

**Pass (Insight):** The Fighter sees the moment Ser Bardo decides not to explain — a small flinch, a breath held too long. The Fighter meets his eyes and says nothing. Ser Bardo fills the silence: the opposing party arrives this week; the morning filing must be first; if the court secretary asks who certified the deathbed confession, the answer is "a friar of the Burgundian house, now departed." He hands over the folio. The Fighter feels its weight.

**Pass (Persuasion):** "What is in Naples that cannot wait until afternoon?" Ser Bardo stops. The question is too exact. He explains. The answer about the Burgundian friar is given.

**Fail:** The Fighter accepts the instructions without pressing. The folio passes hands without the authentication detail. The carrier has what is needed to travel — not what is needed to file.

*The folio granted here. The seal does not give.*

**Grant item:** attestation folio — guild-sealed parchment, red wax ribbon, cedar-oil smell, five signatures on the outer panel

### ACT II — The Siena Road, Hill Pass South of Florence

*The attestation folio · sealed, unread · inside the carrier's saddlebag · a blocked hill road, morning light through trees*

A landslide has closed the main road. At an inn at the foot of the pass, a Dominican friar named Fra Benedetto sits with a cup of wine and watches the debris with the patience of a man who has been patient before. He is traveling north from Naples. When the Fighter stops at the inn, Fra Benedetto notices the guild seal on the saddlebag.

"What document carries a guild seal south in March?" he asks. Not hostile — genuinely curious. He is the kind of man who asks questions because he wants to know.

*The folio is in the bag. It is not something I should describe.*

The inn keeper says the road will open by morning. Fra Benedetto offers to facilitate the Naples filing — he knows the court secretary, Messer Rofredo del Sasso, personally.

*He is being helpful. That is the danger.*

Six travelers in the courtyard; enough hands to clear the debris; the question is whether to work or wait.

---

**Skill check — DC 12 Athletics or Persuasion:**
Clear the road quickly enough to depart before Fra Benedetto presses further, or redirect his curiosity.

**Pass (Athletics):** The Fighter organizes the clearing. Fra Benedetto joins in, because he is that kind of friar. Two hours of work; the road opens. When Fra Benedetto finally asks about the document, the Fighter is already mounted. "Commercial correspondence." He waves. Fra Benedetto is satisfied.

**Pass (Persuasion):** The Fighter turns the conversation: what brings Fra Benedetto north from Naples? The friar tells a long story about the contract dispute at the ecclesiastical court — the same one Monsieur de Mâcon is pursuing from the other side. The Fighter, listening, now knows the counter-filing is real and when it is expected. Fra Benedetto asks nothing further.

**Fail:** Fra Benedetto learns the document is an attestation of sanctity for a man named Ciappelletto. He does not react strongly. He files the name.

*The folio has not been opened. The seal holds.*

**Grant item:** Fra Benedetto's road notes — a scrap of parchment with the court schedule and Messer Rofredo's name, pressed into the Fighter's hand at the road clearing

### ACT III — The Papal Checkpoint, South of Rome

*The attestation folio · sealed · inside the saddlebag · a small stone building at a road junction, midday heat*

A Papal notary officer, Messer Lodovico, mans a checkpoint on the road south to Naples. The regulation: all documents bearing guild certification or ecclesiastical seals must be logged and inspected. The rule was enacted after fraudulent indulgences circulated through this corridor during the plague years. Messer Lodovico is efficient, not corrupt. He does his job.

He sees the seal. He asks what the document contains.

*The folio is warm now from the saddle. The wax holds.*

The Fighter must classify the document in a way that passes inspection without opening it.

---

**Skill check — DC 12 Deception or History:**
Argue the document's category — commercial correspondence, not ecclesiastical declaration.

**Pass (Deception):** "Guild member's professional character reference. Standard commercial form." Messer Lodovico looks at the seal — guild notary, not church notary. He logs it: "commercial character certification, guild of Florence." Passes it.

**Pass (History):** The regulation specifies that it applies to documents signed by clergy making formal declarations of sanctity — this document is signed only by merchants and notarized by a guild notary, not an ecclesiastical officer. Messer Lodovico recognizes the legal precision and marks it "commercial, non-ecclesiastical."

**Fail:** Messer Lodovico opens the folio. He reads "miracles." He logs it as "attestation of sanctity, commercial use" with a note that it should have been registered at the Florentine diocesan office first. He reseals it — now bearing his inspection mark as well as the guild seal. Two seals; questions to follow in Naples.

*The folio passes south.*

**Grant item:** Lodovico's transit stamp — a wax impression of the Papal notary mark, pressed separately on a strip of linen, proving the checkpoint was passed lawfully

### ACT IV — A Village Inn, South of Rome

*The attestation folio · sealed, two seals now if the check failed · in the saddlebag · a village inn courtyard, early evening, a sick man in the yard*

A Flemish merchant's agent, Jan Pietersz, sits in the inn courtyard against the wall. He has been riding too hard for two weeks and has a fever. He is the advance rider for Monsieur de Mâcon's counter-dossier. His packhorse has a locked box. He does not know the Fighter is carrying the opposing document. He is simply sick and afraid and asking anyone who passes if they know a physician.

*He has a fever and a locked box. I know what I am looking at.*

The Fighter can pass him by. Or the Fighter can stop.

---

**Skill check — DC 12 Medicine or Insight:**
Treat Jan's fever and hear what he says in gratitude, or read his fear and the lock on his box without his saying a word.

**Pass (Medicine):** Water, rest, cooling herbs. Jan is grateful and talks. He reveals — not knowing the context — that de Mâcon's counter-dossier is still delayed at the Alpine pass; it is expected in Naples in four days, not tomorrow. The morning session matters not because the counter-evidence is nearly there — it matters because the dispute is scheduled for tomorrow morning regardless, and whoever files first into the morning record wins the framing.

**Pass (Insight):** The Fighter reads Jan's anxiety: this is not a man with a routine delivery; he is afraid of missing a deadline. From the nature of his fear and the care he gives the locked box, the Fighter deduces the counter-dossier is there and is delayed. Jan, watched carefully, confirms this without speaking it.

**Fail:** The Fighter passes Jan by. Jan recovers on his own within a day, rides through the night, arrives in Naples the next morning alongside the Fighter.

*The folio goes south into warmth.*

**Grant item:** Jan's transit letter — the letter of introduction Jan was carrying to the Naples court, dropped in his fever and not retrieved; the Fighter keeps it; it names Monsieur de Mâcon's party and their timeline

### ACT V — The Naples Ecclesiastical Court, Filing Desk (NAP)

*The attestation folio · sealed · carried across four acts and two weeks of road · the filing desk of Messer Rofredo del Sasso, Naples, second hour of morning*

The court of the ecclesiastical session is a stone building near the harbor. Salt smell. The morning session begins at the third hour. It is the second hour. Two other parties are at the desk ahead of the Fighter. Messer Rofredo del Sasso, court secretary, is efficient and precise. He handles each filing in exactly the order it arrives.

He takes the folio. He asks three questions: Who is the certifying notary? (Ser Anselmo Benedetti of Florence — the seal shows this.) What is the purpose of the filing? (Professional character certification for the guild contract dispute.) Who certified the deathbed confession that underlies the saintly reputation?

*This is the question. The one Ser Bardo either gave me or did not.*

---

**Skill check — DC 14 Persuasion or Investigation:**
Answer Messer Rofredo's third question correctly, or find the answer in the court's own prior record.

**Pass (Persuasion, with Act 1 detail):** "A friar of the Burgundian house, now departed." Messer Rofredo writes it. He looks at the folio, at the Fighter, at the folio again. He files it into the morning session record. The clerk stamps it. Third question closed.

**Pass (Investigation):** The Fighter has noticed the court's prior filing index on the desk — there is a reference to Ciappelletto's veneration from a prior, unrelated filing about the Burgundian friary. The Fighter points this out. Rofredo, satisfied that precedent exists, files without requiring further certification.

**Fail:** The Fighter cannot answer the third question. Rofredo requires supplementary certification from a church authority. He places the folio in escrow — afternoon session, not morning.

*The folio leaves the Fighter's hands.*

---

**onPass:**

The morning session closes with the attestation on record. Messer Rofredo reads the miracle accounts twice. He says: "The friar's account is thorough. The guild's certifications are in order. The morning record will stand."

If Jan Pietersz was delayed in Act 4, de Mâcon's counter-dossier arrives four days later — too late to challenge the morning ruling.

A guild clerk finds the Fighter in the harbor district at midday and presses a purse into his hand. Inside, a folded note: *The matter concluded well. Saint Ciappelletto is, it appears, most effective when properly filed before the third hour.*

*What is inside the folio, the Fighter has never read. The seal was never broken. Whatever he was carrying, he carried it.*

**Take item:** attestation folio (filed, spent, never opened by the carrier)
**Grant item:** Ser Bardo's purse — guild-weight silver, drawstring tight, the note inside

---

**onFail:**

The attestation is held for afternoon session. Jan Pietersz, arriving that morning, delivers de Mâcon's counter-dossier. Both documents are heard together. The court suspends judgment pending inquiry. The ruling will take months.

The guild's contract claim is frozen. Ser Bardo, when told, says nothing for a long time. Then: "The Burgundians. They are exactly as difficult as Ciappelletto always said they were."

The name Ciappelletto now appears twice in the Naples court record — once as saint, once as subject of inquiry. The court clerk, later, annotates both entries: *same individual?*

### BLQ-S08 — "The Jewel Chest"

**Theme:** The survivor cannot yet open what he carries; the value inside is real but unusable until delivery is complete; every party has a plausible claim except the one who actually needs it to arrive
