## Cycle 1 — Angle: Sir Nigel as the moral center — the knight whose smallness of body and greatness of spirit define the age

---

### Pass 1 — Quick

The White Company is Arthur Conan Doyle's finest historical novel — a deliberate
attempt to write an English national epic in the tradition of the chanson de geste,
set in the precise moment when the Hundred Years War enters its most dramatic phase.
Alleyne Edricson, a young scholar raised in Beaulieu Abbey, leaves the monastery
when his novice period expires and enters a world he is entirely unprepared for.
He falls in with two companions — Hordle John, an enormous expelled monk, and
Sam Aylward, a veteran archer who has been soldiering in France for twenty years —
and together they join the White Company, a mercenary force of English longbowmen
being reformed under the command of Sir Nigel Loring to serve the Black Prince in
his Castilian campaign. The novel traces their journey from Hampshire to Gascony
to Spain, where they fight at the Battle of Najera in 1367, and returns Alleyne —
changed, bloodied, in love — to England and to Maude Loring, Sir Nigel's daughter.
What the novel argues is that the medieval chivalric code, for all its theater and
extravagance, produced real courage and real honor — and that Sir Nigel Loring,
who is small and half-blind and utterly convinced of his own duty, is England.

---

### Pass 2 — Characters, Choices, Tone

**Alleyne Edricson:** Doyle's chosen eye on the medieval world. Raised in a
monastery, he has Latin, courtesy, and a scholar's observation; he lacks
everything practical. His education across the novel is the standard Bildungsroman
form — naivety tested by violence, love, and responsibility — but Doyle executes
it warmly. Alleyne never becomes cynical. He becomes capable. By Najera he is a
soldier who can still read Latin, and that is exactly what the book asks of him.

**Sir Nigel Loring:** The novel's moral center and greatest creation. He is small —
diminutively so, almost comically — with failing eyesight, a gentle manner, and a
mode of speech so formal it approaches parody. And he is the bravest man in the book
without contest. His quest is structural: he has promised Lady Mary he will win three
honorable deeds before returning home. He pursues these opportunities with the
cheerful single-mindedness of a man who has organized his entire moral life around a
concept and finds it sufficient. Every encounter with a larger, fiercer man ends with
Sir Nigel challenging him. Every challenge is eventually answered. He is never afraid.
His courage is not the result of suppressing fear — it is the result of genuinely not
organizing his inner life around fear at all.

**Hordle John:** The jovial giant. Expelled from Beaulieu for fighting another monk
over a cheese. He is Alleyne's first friend in the world and the most physically
capable member of their party. Doyle uses him for comedy and for one moment of
extraordinary pathos — when John weeps after his first battle and is ashamed of it,
and Aylward tells him quietly that every man wept after his first.

**Sam Aylward:** The novel's technical expert and its worldliest voice. Twenty years
of French campaigning, a Gascon wife somewhere, debts in three countries. He loves
the Company because it is his family, and he loves Sir Nigel because Sir Nigel is
worth loving. He is the English archer at his best: direct, skilled, loyal, and
entirely without illusions about anything except the value of his companions.

**Maude Loring:** Sir Nigel's daughter. Quiet, capable, possessed of a dry wit that
comes out only in proximity to Alleyne. The love story between them is conducted
entirely within the strict limits of what is possible — he is sworn to her father's
service; she cannot declare herself first; they must wait. She gives him one glove
at departure. That is all. He carries it. He keeps it. The reader carries it with him.

**Lady Mary Loring:** Sir Nigel's wife. She runs the manor, manages the debt, and
says goodbye to her husband every time he goes to war with perfect composure.
She is the novel's quiet heroism — the endurance that no one writes songs about
because it never ends.

**The Black Prince (Edward of Woodstock):** Appears briefly as the ideal lord —
commanding, fair, direct. His treatment of the Company is exactly what the chivalric
code promises: merit rewarded, honor recognized. He is what all lords are supposed
to be and almost none are.

**Bertrand du Guesclin:** The great French captain, the antagonist at Najera. He
is treated with full chivalric respect — Doyle admires him openly. The medieval
convention that your enemy's honor reflects on your own is applied throughout.

**Tone:** The novel is earnest, warm, and deliberately epic in ambition. Doyle
writes it as if the medieval world were lit from the inside by its own conviction.
Violence is present — sieges, battles, ambushes — but never savored. The book's
emotional register is consistently generous: generosity toward the past, toward
courage, toward the ideals that produced men like Sir Nigel even if they also
produced cruelty and waste. It is the right book for 1366. It was written to be.

### Pass 3 — The Departure from Tilford

The morning the White Company leaves England, Sir Nigel is still in the stable
yard at Tilford when Lady Mary comes to him. She has been up since before dawn.
Everyone knows this because the provisions are already packed, the horses ready,
the grooms standing back. She did all of it herself.

She does not make a speech. She takes a silk scarf — pale blue, her work, everyone
recognizes it from the solar — and ties it around the hilt of Sir Nigel's sword
with three knots. Each knot is small and precise. She ties the third one and looks
at him.

**Lady Mary:** "Come back to me when you have done what you must do."

**Sir Nigel:** "I shall bring you something worthy of those knots."

She steps back. That is all. He mounts. He does not look back — not because he
doesn't want to, but because looking back is not in his code for this kind of
departure. He has organized himself for forward motion.

Alleyne watches this from across the yard, and then Maude appears beside him.
She has been standing slightly behind him for longer than he knew. She says
nothing. She removes one glove — her right one, good leather, worked at the cuff
— and holds it out.

**Maude:** "I have no sword."

He takes it. He does not know what to say. She walks back into the house.

He stands in the yard with one glove in his hand — her warmth still in the
leather — and Aylward appears at his shoulder.

**Aylward:** *(very quietly)* "Put it inside your coat, lad. Not where anyone
will see it. She'll know what you did with it when you come back."

Alleyne puts it inside his coat. He follows Sir Nigel out of the gate.

The story begins here. Everything before was preparation. Everything after is
the consequence of these two things — the scarf on the sword hilt, the glove
against Alleyne's chest — being carried three countries away and brought home.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Challenge at the Crossroads** — Sir Nigel encounters a French knight
   who is three times his size and challenges him to single combat. The knight
   attempts to flee rather than face the tiny English lord. The Fighter must
   physically prevent the knight's escape — holding the crossroads — while Sir
   Nigel methodically recites the rules of honorable engagement. Pure crowd-control
   combat with a comic-chivalric overtone.

2. **The Fortune-Knot** — Lady Mary's scarf ties Sir Nigel's vow to his sword.
   When Sir Nigel is wounded at a castle assault and cannot hold it, he gives
   the scarf to the Fighter: "Keep it until I can take it back." The Fighter
   must guard it through the remaining campaign — across Spain, through the
   battle — and return it to Sir Nigel's hand before the charge at Najera.
   Pure escort quest: the token is the vow itself.

3. **Aylward's Last Arrow** — Before the battle, Aylward makes a bet on a
   shot that should be impossible. His best arrow is in the Fighter's keeping.
   A rival archer tries to sabotage the shot by bribing the Fighter. The
   Fighter must hold the arrow, refuse the bribe, and get to Aylward in time.
   Deception and Athletics check through a military camp at night.

4. **John's First Battle** — Hordle John has never killed a man. He freezes
   at the moment of first contact during a castle assault. The Fighter must
   hold the breach alone long enough for John to recover, then get both of
   them out. Combat quest with a specific moral weight: protecting a friend's
   first failure so it doesn't become his last.

5. **Maude's Glove** — Alleyne has the glove. During the chaos of the Najera
   withdrawal, he is badly wounded and cannot carry it further. He gives it to
   the Fighter with one instruction: "Don't let it burn." The Fighter must get
   the glove off the battlefield and to the surgeon's tent without losing it
   in the retreat. Escort through a collapsing military situation.

6. **The French Hostage** — A French prisoner (a minor noble) knows the layout
   of a fortified pass the Company needs to cross. Sir Nigel refuses to torture
   him. He gives the Fighter one hour to persuade him voluntarily. Pure
   Persuasion quest — the prisoner is not a villain; he is afraid and loyal
   to his own lord. The Fighter must give him a reason to choose.

7. **Du Guesclin's Parole** — After Najera, Bertrand du Guesclin is captured.
   His ransom is enormous. While arrangements are being made, a group of English
   soldiers intends to kill him — ransom or no ransom. Sir Nigel has given his
   personal word for du Guesclin's safety. The Fighter must hold the tent door
   through the night. Combat quest with diplomatic consequence: killing English
   soldiers to protect the honor of an English lord.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Fortune-Knot*

*Storyline 2 — Escort Quest. The Fighter carries Lady Mary's silk scarf —
tied around Sir Nigel's sword hilt when he left England — across Spain to the
battle at Najera and returns it to Sir Nigel's hand before the charge. Sir Nigel
is wounded at a castle assault in Act III and cannot hold it himself. The Fighter
is his luck until he can take it back.*

**THE TOKEN: Lady Mary's Scarf** — pale blue silk, hand-worked, tied with three
precise knots around Sir Nigel's sword hilt when the Company departed Tilford.
It has been on that hilt through every skirmish in France. Now, removed and
placed in the Fighter's hands, it is warm from the grip. It is damp at one end
where the blood reached it at the castle. The knots are still intact. They must
stay intact. Three knots for three honorable deeds not yet won.

---

### ACT I — *The Castle Gate, Gascony*
*The scarf: knotted on Sir Nigel's sword hilt, pale blue silk, already marked
with ash from the camp-fires but still whole. Location: the approach road to
Château Villefranche, southern France — dust, summer heat, the castle above.*

The castle sits on a rock. Its gate is the only way up. The Company's captain
has ordered a direct assault; Sir Nigel has pointed out, quietly, that a direct
assault on that gate will kill two hundred men for nothing. The discussion is
ongoing and not going well.

Below the gate, a French garrison officer appears on the parapet and shouts a
formal challenge — single combat for the right to parley. Sir Nigel's hand is
on his hilt before the challenge is finished.

**Aylward:** *(to the Fighter, very quietly)* "He's going up there alone. You
know what that means. If he doesn't come back, the scarf comes to us."

**Sir Nigel:** *(already walking forward, not looking back)* "The scarf stays
where it is until I have need to move it."

*The Fighter thinks: three knots. Three deeds not yet won. He walks up the road
alone because that is what he does.*

---

### ACT II — *The Breach, Before Dawn*
*The scarf: on Sir Nigel's hilt, entering the breach with him. Location: the
castle's south wall, forced — rubble, torchlight inside, the sound of iron.*

The parley failed — the garrison's captain refused terms and Sir Nigel's
challenge was accepted, fought, and won in under four minutes. The castle gate
is now open. The Company is going through.

Sir Nigel leads the breach. He is the smallest man in the column and the first
through the gap. The Fighter is his assigned guard — close behind, keeping
the lance-points off his back while he advances.

Inside the wall: a corridor of men, fire, confusion. A crossbow bolt comes from
the gallery above — aimed for the small English lord who is visibly in command —
and Sir Nigel steps aside from it by instinct, still moving forward, still talking
to his men.

**Sir Nigel:** *(conversationally, to no one in particular)* "I believe that
fellow on the gallery has had his chance. Someone deal with him."

*The Fighter thinks: he noticed the shot. He stepped aside from it. He did not
stop talking.*

---

### ACT III — *The Keep, After*
*The scarf: being untied from the sword hilt by Sir Nigel's own hands and placed
into the Fighter's. Location: the castle keep, cleared — a stone room, rush-light,
a surgeon.*

Sir Nigel is sitting on a bench with his left arm held carefully still. A lance
found a gap in his mail at the shoulder. Not mortal — the surgeon says so with
the particular confidence of a man who has seen mortal and this is not it. But
Sir Nigel cannot lift the arm. He cannot hold a sword.

He unties the scarf himself, one-handed, with the careful precision he gives to
everything. He holds it out.

**Sir Nigel:** "The knots must stay as they are. I tied them for three reasons.
One is done. Two remain. Keep it until I have use of it again."

**Alleyne:** *(quietly, nearby)* "He has never given it to anyone before."

**Sir Nigel:** *(already looking at the surgeon)* "He has never needed to."

*The Fighter thinks: three knots. One done. The scarf is lighter than expected.
Honor always is, until you are holding it for someone else.*

---

### ACT IV — *The Mountain Road, Spain*
*The scarf: folded inside the Fighter's coat, against the chest, warm. Location:
the Navarrese passes, three days from Najera — steep road, mule-trains, Castilian
soldiers moving the opposite direction.*

The road through the Pyrenees is contested. Pedro of Castile's allies and Henry
of Trastamara's agents move the same roads from opposite ends, and the Company
is in the middle as a declared English force. It makes them enemies to two sides
simultaneously and friends to a third — the Black Prince's warrant travels with
them, but the warrant only works if you show it to people who respect it.

A Castilian agent on a grey horse intercepts the column at a narrow cut. He says
he has orders to "inspect the Company's carried goods for treaty compliance." He
knows what he is looking for. There are informants in every camp.

His eyes go to the Fighter. He has been told someone in this column carries
something that belongs to an English lord — something that could prove the
Company's contract is personally bound to Sir Nigel, whose name is on Pedro's
enemy list.

*The Fighter thinks: the scarf is inside the coat. The agent cannot see it. He
can see my hands, my face, and whether I am afraid. I am not afraid.*

---

### ACT V — *The Battle of Najera — April 3, 1367*
*The scarf: returned from the Fighter's hands to Sir Nigel's. Location: the
field at Najera, Castile — Spanish plain, dawn, ten thousand men on each side.*

Sir Nigel's shoulder has healed enough. He has tested it each morning for two
weeks — lifting, rotating, gripping the sword — and this morning he says nothing
at breakfast and tightens his mail himself and that is how the Fighter knows.

Before the charge, he comes to the Fighter and holds out his hand.

He takes the scarf. He ties it around his lance — not the hilt, the lance — with
the same three knots Lady Mary made. He does this slowly, precisely, without
looking at what his hands are doing.

**Sir Nigel:** "Two deeds remain. I expect to have them by noon."

**Aylward:** *(to the Fighter, quietly)* "He will."

The charge begins. The longbows go first — three volleys that stop the cavalry
dead — and then the men-at-arms come forward and the field is the usual noise
and press and very small movements that determine very large things. The Fighter
holds the Company's left flank for the forty minutes it takes Sir Nigel to find
his remaining two deeds and win them.

At noon, Sir Nigel has both. The scarf is still on the lance, still knotted,
carrying a thin coat of Castilian dust. He rides back through the Company's
line and holds the lance up once — for Lady Mary, who cannot see it, who is
three countries away doing the accounts.

The scarf has fulfilled its three knots. The vow is complete.

*The Fighter thinks: he did not look at me when he took it back. He did not
need to. That is the whole point of keeping a thing safe.*
