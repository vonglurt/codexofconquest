## Cycle 1 — Pass 1: Full Telling

### Historical and Literary Context

The Nibelungenlied was composed around 1200 in Middle High German, during the height of
the Hohenstaufen imperial court culture — a moment of German courtly literature parallel to
the Arthurian romances being written in France and England. Its author is unknown. It draws
on the same pool of Germanic legend as the Volsunga Saga — the same names, the same murder,
the same Hungarian feast of death — but transforms the material almost completely. Gone is
the cosmic Norse framework: no Odin, no Norns, no Fafnir in the foreground, no
*Ragnarök* as the backdrop. What replaces it is a world of courtly formality, honor protocols,
and the precise choreography of feudal obligation — a world in which everything is ceremony,
and ceremony is the form that destruction wears.

The poem's thirty-nine chapters are called *Aventiuren* — adventures, in the sense of
the court romance. This is deliberate and ironic. The events it describes are not adventures
in the romance sense. They are catastrophes. The genre frame is borrowed; the contents refuse it.

In 1367, the Nibelungenlied is already nearly two centuries old and widely circulated in
German-speaking manuscript culture. It is, in a sense, the German national epic — though
that concept did not yet exist in 1367, the poem's cultural weight was felt. A Fighter in
the game's world who travels through German-adjacent territories would know the story the
way a late medieval European knew the Troy legend: not necessarily the poem, but the events.

The fundamental difference from the Volsunga Saga: in the Norse version, Gudrun (= Kriemhild)
is largely a passive instrument of grief. In the Nibelungenlied, Kriemhild is the engine of
the entire second half of the poem — a woman who spends years engineering the precise
circumstances of her brothers' destruction. The Norse version is a tragedy of fate. The
German version is a tragedy of will.

### The Story in Full

The poem opens in the Burgundian court at Worms on the Rhine. Three brothers rule: Gunther
(the king), Gernot, and Giselher. Their sister is Kriemhild, young and celebrated for her
beauty. She has a dream: she raises a falcon which two eagles tear apart. Her mother Ute
interprets the dream: the falcon is a noble man she will love, and the eagles are those who
will kill him. Kriemhild says she would rather never love a man than suffer such a loss. She
will lose this argument.

Siegfried, son of King Siegmund and Queen Sigelind of the Netherlands, hears of Kriemhild's
beauty and comes to Worms to woo her. He is the greatest warrior alive. Before the story
began — as backstory, not as scene — he killed the dragon Fafnir, bathed in its blood to
make his skin invulnerable, and took the Nibelung treasure hoard. One linden leaf fell on
his back as he bathed. That spot, between the shoulder blades, the size of a leaf, is the
only place a weapon can harm him.

He comes to Worms and is accepted, though with suspicion from Hagen — Gunther's chief
vassal and the most politically intelligent man in the court. Hagen knows exactly who
Siegfried is. He says so. He is overruled.

Siegfried serves Gunther in a war against the Saxons and proves his worth. He is given
access to the court; he sees Kriemhild at a distance, occasionally, with elaborate protocol
separating them. They fall in love through the air between them, across courtyards and
ceremonial passages.

The problem of Brunhild. Gunther wants to win Brunhild, queen of Iceland, who will only
marry a man who can defeat her in three tests of athletic strength — throwing a javelin,
putting a stone, and a long jump. If a man attempts and fails, he is killed. Gunther cannot
survive the tests. Siegfried has a cloak of invisibility — the *Tarnkappe* — taken from the
dwarf Alberich when he won the hoard. Siegfried puts on the cloak and performs the tests
while Gunther goes through the motions beside him. Brunhild believes she has accepted
Gunther. She has actually been defeated by Siegfried.

This deception is the source of everything. Siegfried wins Kriemhild; Gunther wins Brunhild;
and the two marriages begin with a lie inside one of them.

Brunhild is not what she was in Iceland. In the German version, she does not have the cosmic
fire-circle history of the Valkyrie; she is simply a very strong queen who had a rule and
who was deceived into abandoning it. She is suspicious of Siegfried. She feels something is
wrong. She can't name what. She presses Gunther: why does Siegfried serve you as a vassal
when he is clearly a king's son? Gunther evades. The suspicion festers.

The queens' quarrel. Years pass. The couples live at Worms; Siegfried and Kriemhild at
the Netherlands. They visit for a great festival. Brunhild and Kriemhild are both at the
Minster steps and both want to enter first — it is a question of whose husband outranks
the other. Each insists her husband is the greater lord. The argument escalates. Brunhild
says Kriemhild's husband is Gunther's vassal. Kriemhild says Gunther couldn't have won
Brunhild without Siegfried's help.

She shows Brunhild the ring.

Siegfried had taken a gold ring from Brunhild's finger — in the dark, in her chamber,
on the night Gunther was supposed to consummate the marriage and couldn't (Brunhild tied
him to the wall; Siegfried dealt with this problem too, also invisibly). He gave the ring
to Kriemhild. Kriemhild now holds it up on the Minster steps in front of the assembled
court.

Brunhild *"waxed as wan as a dead woman."*

The quarrel is public. Siegfried's deception is public. Brunhild is humiliated before the
court. She weeps. She goes to Gunther and demands justice.

Hagen goes to Brunhild. He does not love Brunhild. He is not doing this for Brunhild.
He is doing this because the Burgundian honor-system has been damaged and the only repair
is blood. He asks Kriemhild, under the pretense of protecting Siegfried in battle, to sew
a cross on his tunic at the linden-leaf spot. She does it. She tells Hagen exactly where
the spot is.

Hagen takes Siegfried hunting. At a spring, while Siegfried stoops to drink, Hagen drives
a spear between his shoulder blades.

Siegfried's death is long. He is not a man who goes quickly. He rises from the ground,
finds his shield, tries to reach Hagen. He cannot. His strength is running out through the
wound. He falls. He speaks. He tells Gunther, who is standing there watching: *your honor is
ruined now.* He speaks to Kriemhild in absence — *be kind to our son.* He dies at the spring
while the hunting birds rise overhead.

Kriemhild's grief is the poem's hinge. From here to the end, the poem is hers. She does
not simply mourn. She calculates. She is twenty years of calculation in one woman's body.

First, she refuses to leave Worms. She stays in the court that killed her husband. She
accepts the hoard — the Nibelung treasure — and distributes it widely, buying loyalty,
building a faction. Hagen watches this and understands. He takes the hoard and throws it
into the Rhine. He tells Gunther where he sank it. They swear never to reveal the location
while any of them lives. The hoard is gone. It has been in the Rhine ever since. No one
will ever retrieve it.

Etzel's messengers come. Etzel — Attila, king of the Huns — is a widower and the most
powerful ruler in the known world. He is sending for Kriemhild. His advisor Rüdeger von
Bechelaren is the messenger, a great and honorable lord. He promises Kriemhild that she
will have twelve kings at her feet in Etzel's country. He does not know what she will do
with this.

She accepts. She rides east. She becomes Queen of the Huns.

Thirteen years pass. Kriemhild rules at Etzel's court. She bears Etzel a son, Ortlieb.
She is the most powerful woman in the world. She waits.

Then she asks Etzel to send an invitation to her brothers.

The invitation is carried by two minstrel-messengers — Wärbel and Swämmelin — to Worms.
Hagen immediately says: do not go. He knows what this is. He tells Gunther directly:
Kriemhild has not forgiven the gold or the husband. This invitation is the shape of
something else.

Gunther and his brothers will not refuse a formal invitation from their sister and their
overlord. Honor requires attendance. They ride east with an army.

They cross the Danube. There is an episode at the river: a ferryman tries to warn them
back, or refuses passage. Hagen kills him and rows the boats himself. Then he does
something extraordinary: he throws a priest overboard — the chaplain — to test a
prophecy that said none of the Burgundians would return alive. The priest cannot swim
but reaches shore and makes it back to Burgundy. The prophecy is confirmed. Hagen knows.
He breaks the boats so there is no turning back.

The journey into Hungary is itself a trial: every host they meet is generous, the
hospitality is perfect, and every gesture of hospitality is part of the trap.

At Etzel's court, the festival begins. Games, feasting, honor. Then Kriemhild approaches
Hagen: she asks him where the hoard is. He tells her he will not tell her while Gunther
lives. She turns and goes.

She asks her Hunnic warriors to kill Hagen. It starts with one retainer trying and
Volker — Hagen's companion, a knight and fiddler — cutting him down. And then the
night-watch on the feast-hall doors while the Burgundians sleep inside, Hagen and Volker
sitting on the steps with weapons, awake, singing to themselves.

Then Kriemhild arranges it so that her young son Ortlieb — Etzel's son, her own child —
is brought in to the feast table and held up before the Burgundians. She is showing the
court that this is Etzel's heir. Hagen looks at the child and says something terrible:
that the boy does not look long-lived. His meaning is understood. Kriemhild has fed her
own child to the feast as the spark.

The slaughter begins. The hall is set on fire. The Burgundians are inside. They stack
the dead and use the bodies as cover. Rüdeger von Bechelaren is given his shield by
Hagen — a great symbolic act; they acknowledge each other as honorable before Rüdeger
is forced to fight them — and then Rüdeger is killed, and Hildebrand kills Rüdeger's
killer. The hall burns and the fighting goes on inside the fire.

At the end, Gunther and Hagen are the last Burgundians alive. They are taken prisoner
separately. Kriemhild goes to Hagen. She asks one more time where the hoard is. He tells
her he swore not to tell while Gunther lived. She has Gunther killed. She brings his head
to Hagen. He says: now no one living knows where the hoard is. He is the last.
He is satisfied.

She takes Siegfried's sword — the blade Balmung — and cuts off Hagen's head herself.

Old Hildebrand looks at this and is appalled. He has watched the whole feast of death,
survived it, and he finds this — a queen cutting down a bound captive — past bearing.
He kills Kriemhild.

Etzel, who has lost everything — his son, his court, his feast — stands and looks at what
his wife and her grief have made.

*"Woe,"* he says, *"that such a hero's life should end at the hand of a woman."*

And the poem ends.

### What the Poem Argues

The Nibelungenlied is not a morality tale. No one in it is simply right or simply wrong.
Hagen is the most morally compromised figure — he betrays a guest, kills his lord's
friend, steals the widow's gold, and orchestrates the suppression of information that
could have prevented everything. He is also, by the end, the poem's most fully realized
figure: a man who knew what was coming at every step, was right about it all, and could
not stop any of it because honor required the response that led to ruin.

Kriemhild is neither victim nor villain. She is a woman whose grief the world has no
structure to accommodate. She cannot fight. She cannot declare war. She cannot indict Hagen
at law. She can only wait, and marry, and invite, and watch. Everything she does is within
the protocol of her role and is entirely designed to destroy. The poem gives her exactly
what she spent twenty years building, and the cost is everything including herself.

The poem's argument, if it has one, is: **the codes by which noble people live —
honor, hospitality, loyalty, obligation — are perfectly designed to be weaponized.** The
same rules that bind people together are the channels through which grief becomes killing.
Kriemhild uses the invitation, the feast, the protocol of hospitality as the delivery
mechanism for something else entirely.

### Chapter Notes — by Aventiure group

**Aventiuren 1–4: The setting, Siegfried's arrival, first impressions** — Kriemhild's
falcon dream opens the poem and gives everything away at once; then Siegfried arrives
at Worms seeking her. The tension between courtly welcome and Hagen's named suspicion is
immediate. Hagen never stops knowing.

**Aventiuren 5–10: The Saxon war, Siegfried's service, mutual watching** — Siegfried
proves his worth militarily. He and Kriemhild see each other in the court's ceremonial
spaces. The love is real and is conducted entirely through distance and protocol.

**Aventiuren 11–14: The wooing of Brunhild** — The Tarnkappe deception. Siegfried in the
cloak, performing the tests while Gunther takes credit. Brunhild's defeat. The double
wedding. The deception is complete and perfect and wrong, and Hagen watches it happen.

**Aventiuren 15–16: Brunhild's suspicion and the Minster quarrel** — Brunhild feels the
gap in her story but cannot identify it. The queens argue on the church steps. The ring
appears. *"She waxed as wan as a dead woman."* The poem's first half breaks on this scene.

**Aventiuren 17–19: Siegfried's death** — Hagen acts. The cross sewn by Kriemhild's hand.
The spring. Siegfried's long dying. The poem lingers on this: his last words, the rising
birds, the fact that it is a beautiful hunting morning.

**Aventiuren 20–25: Kriemhild's mourning and the hoard** — She stays. She distributes. Hagen
watches and recognizes the faction-building. He takes the hoard. Seals it in the Rhine. Now
she is contained and furious and waiting.

**Aventiuren 26–28: Etzel's embassy and Kriemhild's departure east** — Rüdeger's courtly
embassy. Kriemhild's calculation. The years at Etzel's court not shown — only implied.

**Aventiuren 29–32: The invitation and the journey east** — Wärbel and Swämmelin carry the
summons. Hagen's warning. Gunther's honor-bound acceptance. The Danube crossing. The priest
thrown overboard. The boats broken. Hagen's knowledge that this ends one way.

**Aventiuren 33–36: Arrival and the feast of recognition** — Hospitality that is a trap. The
night-watch: Hagen and Volker on the hall steps, swords across their knees, Volker playing
his fiddle, the Hunnic warriors unable to act while the music plays.

**Aventiuren 37–38: The hall fire and the long slaughter** — The Burgundians fight inside
burning walls. Rüdeger's impossible position and his death. The killing of allies. The poem
becomes a body count told in the exact language of courtly honor.

**Aventiure 39: The end** — Gunther beheaded. Hagen decapitated by Kriemhild with Balmung.
Hildebrand kills Kriemhild. Etzel's last line. The poem ends without consolation.

## Cycle 1 — Pass 2: Characters

**Kriemhild** — The poem's most complex figure. In the first half she is loving, passive,
the subject of others' action. In the second half she is the agent of everything. The turn
is not a transformation — it is a revelation. What she became in twenty years of waiting
was already implied in the first half's grief. She gives Hagen the cross on the tunic
*because she trusts him* — this is her most terrible mistake, and the poem doesn't hide that
it is her own hand that put the mark on the spot. By the end she has killed her own child's
father, her brothers, and herself, through the mechanism of a feast invitation. The poem
does not call her wrong. It does not call her right. It watches.

**Hagen von Tronje** — The poem's moral center and moral problem. He is Gunther's chief
vassal, the most intelligent man in the poem, and the source of most of its catastrophe.
He is the only one who understands each situation as it develops. He tells Gunther not to
go; he is overruled. He kills Siegfried not because he hates Siegfried but because the
Burgundian honor-system has been damaged and only blood seals it. He sinks the hoard
because he can see what Kriemhild is building with it. He breaks the boats because he
knows the ferry-crossing is the last chance to turn back. At every step he is the most
rational actor and acts correctly within his code, and his code is the mechanism of ruin.
He is never afraid. At the end, when Kriemhild holds Balmung over him, he says the
location of the hoard dies with him. He is satisfied with this. He wins by losing.

**Siegfried** — He is already formed when the poem begins; the dragon, the hoard, the
cloak are backstory. In the poem itself he is almost too large for the story — the
greatest warrior alive, walking through a court drama that doesn't quite have room for
him. His fatal error is accepting the role Gunther offers him: invisible helper, secret
agent, guest who does the work the host can't. He lives inside a deception and it kills
him. His dying scene is the poem's most careful writing: the spear, the rising, the fall,
the birds.

**Brunhild** — Her role is smaller in the German version than in the Norse. She is a queen
who was deceived into marriage, cannot name the deception, presses the wound until Kriemhild
names it publicly, and then disappears from the poem after Siegfried's death. The poem
doesn't follow her further. Her grief is the trigger but not the engine.

**Rüdeger von Bechelaren** — One of the poem's most beloved figures: a great lord, the
perfect embodiment of feudal honor, who brought Etzel's invitation to Kriemhild, and who
is eventually forced to fight the Burgundians — men he has hosted, men whose daughter
his son has married — because his oath to Etzel requires it. He fights the Burgundians
knowing exactly what he is doing and is killed by them. He is the poem's proof that the
honor codes destroy even their best practitioners.

**Volker the Fiddler** — Hagen's inseparable companion, a knight who plays the fiddle.
The night-watch scene — Hagen and Volker on the hall steps while the Huns debate attacking
them, Volker playing his fiddle until the Huns give up and go to sleep — is one of the
poem's most striking images: violence and music, the same hands.

## Cycle 1 — Pass 3: The Ring on the Church Steps

It is a festival morning at Worms. Both queens are at the Minster, dressed for the
high ceremony, attended by their ladies and their lords.

The quarrel began with precedence: who enters first. This is not a trivial question
in a medieval court. Entering first is a visible statement of rank. Kriemhild has been
saying, all morning, that her husband is the greater lord. Brunhild has been saying
the opposite. Each woman has the support of her ladies, of half the court, of the
logic of her own position.

The argument escalates on the church steps, in public, with the whole court watching
from the square below.

Brunhild says: Siegfried is Gunther's vassal. He came to Worms as a vassal. He serves
under Gunther. Therefore Kriemhild, his wife, stands below Brunhild, Gunther's queen.

Kriemhild says: Siegfried was never Gunther's vassal. And Gunther — she pauses here,
in the deliberate way of someone who has been saving this — Gunther did not win Brunhild
alone.

Brunhild goes very still. The court goes very still.

Kriemhild says: my husband used his cloak of invisibility to perform the three tests.
My husband put Brunhild down.

She holds up the ring. Brunhild's ring. The ring that Siegfried took from Brunhild's
finger in the dark of the bridal chamber and gave to Kriemhild. The ring that proves
it was Siegfried, not Gunther, who was in the room.

The ring is small. It catches the light.

*Brunhild waxed as wan as a dead woman.*

What has just happened is irreversible. The words cannot be unsaid. The ring cannot
be un-shown. In the space of thirty seconds on a church step, Brunhild's marriage has
been publicly revealed as a deception, her defeat acknowledged before the entire court,
and the most embarrassing possible detail — the bridal chamber — made plain.

She does not speak. She cannot speak. The poem describes her color and her silence. She
turns and goes into the Minster without another word.

Kriemhild enters after her. She is first.

That evening, Hagen finds Brunhild in tears. He does not ask what happened. He knows
what happened. He asks what she wants done about it. She tells him that Siegfried must
be punished. She does not use a cleaner word.

Hagen goes to work. He does not need to be told twice.

---

The ring is the pivot-object of the poem. It was taken in a dark room — taken from a
woman who was being held down, by a man she didn't know was there, in service of another
man's deception. It is a stolen object that represents a stolen truth. When it appears
on the church steps, it does not represent Siegfried's love or Kriemhild's marriage. It
represents what was done to Brunhild, held up as proof of Kriemhild's superiority.

This is the moment where the poem acknowledges that everyone's hands are dirty. Kriemhild
uses the ring as a weapon — a factually accurate weapon, but still a weapon. Brunhild's
grief is real. Siegfried's deception is real. The poem does not resolve the question of
who is most at fault. It only shows the ring, held in the light, and then all the deaths
that follow from it.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

**1. The Invisible Third** — Gunther cannot survive Brunhild's three tests; Siegfried
in the *Tarnkappe* cloak performs them beside him. The Fighter must watch the empty space
where Siegfried's invisible presence leaves a wake in the dust — and ensure no one else
notices the impossible footprints before Brunhild accepts Gunther.

**2. The Cross on the Tunic** — Hagen has asked Kriemhild to mark Siegfried's vulnerable
spot for his "protection." She is sewing the cross onto the back of the linen tunic. The
Fighter is in the next room and has just understood what this means. A warning that, if
delivered, changes everything — or doesn't.

**3. The Hoard's Sinking** — Hagen has thrown the Nibelung treasure into the Rhine from
a boat at night. One chest did not sink; it floats against the current, waterlogged,
beginning to come apart. The Fighter must carry it back to Hagen before dawn or find a
different way to silence it.

**4. Kriemhild's Invitation** — Kriemhild hands the sealed invitation scroll and her gold
seal-ring to the Fighter, who will carry it west to Worms. The scroll is gracious. The
scroll is an instrument of death. The Fighter carries both faces of it through three
checkpoints to Gunther's hall.

**5. The Night-Watch** — Hagen and Volker keep the hall steps at Etzel's court, awake
through the dark, while the Huns debate in the yard whether to attack now or wait. The
Fighter must carry a message from inside the sleeping hall to Hagen on the steps without
the Huns seeing the door open.

**6. Rüdeger's Shield** — Before Rüdeger must fight the Burgundians he loves, Hagen
asks him for his shield as a token of their mutual honor. The Fighter must carry the
shield from Rüdeger's hands to Hagen's in full view of both armies — a diplomatic act
of impossible delicacy in a space that is already bleeding.

**7. The Last Oath** — After Gunther is killed, Kriemhild brings his head to Hagen.
He is the last Burgundian alive, bound, about to die. He says: now no one knows where
the hoard is. But the Fighter was on the boat the night Hagen sank it and knows the
exact spot. The Fighter must decide whether this knowledge leaves the room alive.

**Themes I am avoiding:**

"Grief carried in an object" (INV, BHD, GDN) is closely related to Kriemhild's story —
she is consumed by grief — but those quests are about objects that hold the dead as memory.
This quest is about an object that deploys grief as action. The ring on the steps holds
accusation, not mourning.

"Honor vs. shame" (MAD, GDN, BHD) is also adjacent — the whole Nibelungenlied runs on
honor codes. But those quests are about men defending their own honor. This quest is
about the honor codes of *others* being weaponized by a third party who understands them
better than their practitioners.

**New theme introduced by LBC:**
*"The invitation as the weapon: what looks like reconciliation is the shape of vengeance"*
— Kriemhild uses the forms of courtesy — the feast, the welcome, the invitation from a
sister to her brothers — as the delivery mechanism for twenty years of calculated grief.
The Fighter carries an invitation that has two faces and cannot show either one openly.

This is distinct from "corruption wears the face of mercy" (FCO) which is about
institutional corruption wearing a virtuous mask. This is personal grief wearing the
mask of family duty, deployed through the perfect forms of feudal hospitality.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: "The Sealed Summons"

**The Token · A sealed scroll and a gold ring pressed in Kriemhild's wax · two faces ·
carried west from the edge of the world · it looks like a sister's welcome ·
it is a funeral invitation written in the language of courtesy**

---

### Act I — The Commission
*The Sealed Summons · uncarried · in Kriemhild's hands · Etzel's great hall,
a private chamber, morning*

The chamber is hung with gold-stitched cloth. The smell of horse and felt from the
plains outside comes even here, through the walls. Kriemhild sits at the writing table.
She is composed. She has been composed for thirteen years.

She holds up the scroll. She holds up the ring — the gold ring of her seal, pressed
into red wax on the ribbon. She looks at you directly.

*"Carry this to Worms. Give it to my brother Gunther. Tell him his sister invites
him to a festival here in the spring. Tell him she has missed her family. These things
are all true."*

She sets them on the table between you. She does not break contact with your eyes.

She has said: *these things are all true.*

She has not said what else is also true.

*You pick up the scroll. The wax is still warm.*

---

### Act II — The Eastern Road
*The Sealed Summons · in the fighter's coat, against the ribs · still sealed ·
the Hungarian road, a checkpoint gate, torch-smoke and mud*

Etzel's border riders are not expecting a lone courier leaving the court. No one
authorized this passage. The captain of the gate is a diligent man. He wants to know
who sent you, by what authority, carrying what.

The scroll cannot come out. The ring-seal cannot be named.

He has half a mind to send a rider back to Etzel's hall and ask. That will take an hour.
That will prompt questions neither you nor Kriemhild is ready for.

*The scroll is warm through your coat. The road west is three weeks long. The hall at
Worms is waiting.*

---

### Act III — The Danube Crossing
*The Sealed Summons · sealed and dry · under a traveling cloak · the Danube ferry,
grey river, cold morning*

The ferryman rows travelers from east bank to west. He is old, experienced, and
recognizes the ring on the scroll's ribbon — Kriemhild's seal is known to certain
people who live near the river's crossing. He has seen Burgundians refuse to cross east
with her seal on the summons. He refuses to carry it west.

He says nothing specific. He says his boat is full. He says the current is bad today.
He will not meet your eyes.

The alternative is miles upstream or downstream to the next crossing, half a day lost.
Or the water, which is very cold.

*The scroll cannot get wet. The seal cannot be broken.*

---

### Act IV — The Road to Worms
*The Sealed Summons · intact, sealed, approaching its destination · the Rhine valley
road, last miles into Burgundy*

Hagen von Tronje is riding the road outside Worms. He rides alone, reading the road.
He is reading it right now: a traveler coming from the east, on the old courier's road
from the Danube crossing, carrying something under a cloak.

He knows exactly what this is. He has been waiting for it for years.

He does not draw his weapon. He asks, conversationally, where you have come from and
where you are going. He is looking at your coat. He is looking at the shape of the
scroll inside it.

*He knows. He does not yet know that you know he knows.*

### Act V — The Hall at Worms
*The Sealed Summons · last inches from its destination · in the fighter's hands ·
Gunther's hall, evening torchlight, the court assembled*

Gunther's hall at Worms. Torchlight on stone. The court in evening assembly —
Gunther on the high seat, Gernot and Giselher on the bench below, the lords and ladies
in their places. Hagen is at the back of the hall. He got here before you. He is watching.

You walk to the high seat. You hold out the scroll and the ring.

Gunther breaks the seal. He reads. His face opens into something like relief — his
sister, after all these years, inviting him east, a festival, a reconciliation.

Gernot and Giselher lean in. They are already planning the journey.

At the back of the hall, Hagen's face does not change.

*You receive the Burgundian Reply-Token — the wax impression of Gunther's ring,
the acceptance sealed and pressed, the answer that begins everything.*
