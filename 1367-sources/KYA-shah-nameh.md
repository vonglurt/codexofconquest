## Three-Pass Literary Summary

### Part 1 Coverage — Kaiúmers through Kai-Káús (opening)

#### Pass 1 — Overview

The Shah-Nameh is Persia's national epic, written c. 971–1010 AD by Firdusi of Tus. It covers Persian mythic prehistory from the first king through the Arab conquest of Persia (7th century AD), running to approximately 120,000 lines (Atkinson's version is a prose-and-verse condensed translation). The poem's organizing spine is the dynasty of Persian kings and the career of the hero Rustem, who serves successive dynasties for several generations.

The Atkinson translation (1909 revised edition) mixes prose summary and verse translation. The volume also contains introductory material on Persian literature by Richard J.H. Gottheil (Columbia University) covering Firdusi, Omar Khayyam, Sa'di, and Hafiz — positioning the Shah-Nameh as the greatest of Eastern national epics alongside the Iliad and Nibelungenlied.

The central moral structure: kings rise by justice and fall by pride. Every golden age ends in hubris. The world is explicitly described as a passing inn, and the poem returns repeatedly to the vanity of earthly ambition. Violence is constant but framed as destiny (written in the stars) rather than chaos.

#### Pass 2 — Structure (Part 1: early dynasties through Rustem's first campaigns)

**Primordial kings (mythic prehistory):**
- **Kaiúmers**: First king; ruled from mountains. Lost son Saiámuk to a Demon; grandson Húsheng avenged him.
- **Húsheng**: Discovered fire by accidentally striking stone (flint spark killed a serpent). Founded fire-worship. Taught irrigation, bread-making, cookery. First to use animal furs for clothing.
- **Tahúmers (Díw-bund, "Binder of Demons")**: Tamed wild animals. Captured demons and forced them to teach reading and writing as ransom for their lives.
- **Jemshíd**: Greatest of the early kings. Invented armor, silk garments, ships. Established Nú-rúz (New Year) festival. Ruled 700 years of golden peace — then grew proud, declared himself the only king in the world. God withdrew his blessing; his nobles abandoned him. He became a fugitive. Eventually captured in Chin (China) and sawn in half between two planks by the tyrant Zohák.

**The Zohák episode (central myth of tyranny):**
- Zohák was an Arab prince corrupted by Iblís (the Devil) in three stages: (1) convinced to murder his father by digging a pit on the path to the house of prayer; (2) Iblís kissed both his shoulders and two black serpents grew there permanently; (3) Iblís returned as a physician and prescribed feeding the serpents daily with human brains — ensuring Zohák became a perpetual murderer of his own subjects.
- Zohák ruled 1,000 years of tyranny. The cook (a compassionate man) secretly substituted sheep brains for one of the two daily human brains, releasing some prisoners. This allowed the survival of a remnant of humanity.

**Kavah the Blacksmith — the pivot scene:**
- When two of Kavah's children were scheduled for serpent-food, he rose up in the king's court, publicly denounced him, tore the royal blood-register, and threw it underfoot.
- He fixed his leather blacksmith's apron to a javelin-point and raised it as a banner. Crowds followed. He sought Feridún, rallied the people.
- The apron became the Derafsh Kaviani (Banner of Kavah), the sacred standard of Persian kingship — every subsequent king adorned it with gems and venerated it. It is one of the poem's great symbolic objects: a craftsman's work-apron became the emblem of legitimate rule.

**Feridún (the deliverer):**
- His father Abtín was hunted and killed by Zohák. His mother Faránuk hid him and had him nursed by a divine cow (Pur'máieh) on Mount Alberz. Later raised by a dervish on the mountain. Descended at age 16 to seek vengeance.
- Two elder brothers tried to murder him (rolled a boulder down the mountain while he slept) — he stopped it with sorcery, forgave them, promoted them.
- Crossed the Tigris on horseback (no ferryman would help), destroyed Zohák's palace-talisman, freed the imprisoned women (including Jemshíd's sisters Shahrnáz and Arnawáz).
- Found Zohák attempting a night assassination; struck him with the cow-headed mace, but a voice from Heaven commanded: "Do not kill him now — chain him in a mountain cave to die slowly." He obeyed.
- The poem explicitly: "Yet Feridún was not an angel, nor composed of musk or ambergris. By justice and generosity he gained his fame."

**Feridún's three sons and the murder of Irij:**
- Feridún divided his empire three ways: Sílim (Rúm/Kháwer), Túr (Túrán), Irij (Persia — the best part).
- Sílim and Túr conspired from jealousy. Irij voluntarily offered to resign his throne and came to his brothers in peace — was murdered by Túr (struck with a golden chair, then decapitated with a dagger). His head sent to Feridún as a trophy.
- Feridún buried the head in a garden, wept constantly, wore black for the rest of his life.

**Minúchihr (the avenger):**
- Born of Irij's daughter and Pishung. Feridún raised him as the instrument of vengeance. Defeated both Sílim and Túr in battle, killed them. Succeeded to the throne.

**Zál and the Símúrgh:**
- The warrior Sám fathered a son with white hair (Zál). Shamed by the "omen," Sám abandoned the infant on Mount Alberz. The Símúrgh (the divine Griffin whose nest is on Alberz) raised the child with his own young, teaching him language and learning.
- Sám had prophetic dreams of guilt; retrieved Zál from the mountain. The Símúrgh gave Zál a feather: "If you are in danger, burn it and I will come."
- Zál became a renowned warrior and administrator of Zábulistán.

**Rúdábeh and the balcony courtship:**
- Zál fell in love with Rúdábeh, daughter of Mihráb of Kábul (a descendant of Zohák's line — politically dangerous). Her attendants engineered a meeting.
- She let her long hair down from the balcony so Zál could climb up. They pledged mutual love. Both Sám and king Minúchihr eventually approved, on astrologers' prediction that their son would be the world's greatest hero.

**Birth of Rustem:**
- Rúdábeh's labor was dangerous; Zál burned the feather and the Símúrgh appeared. The Símúrgh advised a Caesarean delivery (described obliquely). The child Rustem was enormous — "required the milk of ten nurses." On his first day he looked a year old.
- A silken picture of him on horseback, armed, was sent to Sám in Mázinderán. The old champion wept with joy.

**Rustem's first deeds:**
- Age 8: killed a rampaging white elephant at night with a single mace-blow. His father called it the first proof of his destiny.
- Captured a demon-fort on Mount Sipund by disguising himself and his warriors as a salt-merchant's caravan (the garrison opened the gates to buy salt — in those days salt was precious). Fought all night and killed the chief at dawn.

**Nauder and Afrásiyáb:**
- Minúchihr died after 120 years. His son Nauder quickly became a tyrant; Sám mediated and reformed him temporarily.
- Poshang of Túrán (descendant of Túr) saw his chance and sent his son Afrásiyáb to conquer Persia. Sám died during this period. Nauder was captured and killed by Afrásiyáb.
- Afrásiyáb murdered his own virtuous brother Aghríras in rage when Aghríras tried to ally with Zál.

**Zau and Garshásp (transition kings):**
- Brief reigns. Zál managed affairs; Rustem was becoming the dominant military power.

**Kai-Kobád and the Kaiánian dynasty:**
- Rustem fetched Kai-Kobád from hiding on Mount Alberz (he had dreamed of two white falcons crowning him — Rustem interpreted this as symbolizing himself and Zál).
- In his first battle against Afrásiyáb, Rustem seized Afrásiyáb by his belt and lifted him off his horse — but the golden belt broke, and Afrásiyáb fell free and was rescued. Rustem kept the crown and broken belt as trophies.
- Kai-Kobád made peace, dividing at the Jihún River. Reigned 100 years with justice.

**Kai-Káús (reckless successor):**
- Inherited all his father's advantages and immediately became arrogant. A Demon disguised as a musician appeared at court and sang a rhapsodic description of Mázinderán (demon country to the north) — its perpetual spring, its beautiful women, its enchantments.
- Kai-Káús immediately resolved to conquer Mázinderán, declaring his glory would exceed Jemshíd's.
- Zál rushed from Sístán to the court to dissuade him: "Jemshíd never tried to conquer Mázinderán. Feridún never tried. Minúchihr, Zau, Nauder, Kai-Kobád — none of them tried. It is the place of demon-sorcerers. Swords are useless there."

**Part 1 ends here — mid-story at Zál's plea against the Mázinderán invasion.**

## Storylines

### Storyline A: The Tyrant's Food [CONTINUES]
The serpent-feed mechanism — two human brains per day — is the machinery of Zohák's rule. The cook who substituted sheep brains secretly built a small act of mercy into a system of terror. This storyline tracks objects of resistance embedded in oppressive systems: the single act that saves something.

### Storyline B: The Craftsman's Symbol
Kavah the blacksmith raised his leather apron as the Derafsh Kaviani. The poem is explicit that this is not just a battle-flag but the legitimate symbol of Persian kingship going forward — every king after Kavah decorated and venerated it. What begins as a craftsman's work-tool becomes the archive of legitimacy. This is a completed arc in part 1.

### Storyline C: The Abandoned Child and the Divine Tutor [CONTINUES]
Zál raised by the Símúrgh, educated in language, given a feather of protection. This is the recurring pattern of the Shah-Nameh: the parent who fails (Sám's shame) produces the hero through the intervention of a non-human teacher. The Símúrgh feather becomes the key instrument at Rustem's birth. This arc may repeat in later parts (Rustem's own son Sohráb is in the ToC).

### Storyline D: The Belt That Broke
Rustem grabbed Afrásiyáb by the girdle and had him in his hands — then the golden belt broke. This is the Shah-Nameh's characteristic near-resolution: the climax that slips. The war with Túrán continues for the entire epic because this moment fails. This arc is structural — it drives parts 2+.

### Storyline E: The Demon Musician's Song [CONTINUES]
Kai-Káús is corrupted by a song — a musical description of a beautiful demon-country. This is the same mechanism as the original corruption of Zohák: a charming voice proposes an action that will end in disaster. The poem is tracking a pattern: every generation has a seduction tailored to that king's particular vanity. Kai-Káús's vanity is a desire for unprecedented glory.

### Storyline F: Feridún's Restraint
The voice from Heaven told Feridún not to kill Zohák but to chain him in a mountain cave. The poem gives no full explanation. This is an act of deliberate incompletion — the tyrant is contained but not ended. The cave and the chained tyrant remain in the poem's background (may resurface in later parts — Ferdowsi's text has prophecies about Zohák eventually breaking free at the end of the world).

### Storyline G: The Archive of Kingship [CONTINUES]
The succession of Persian kings is itself the archive — each generation is an increment in the record. The poem is about the preservation of a particular idea of legitimate Persian identity against Arab conquest (Firdusi wrote under Arab/Turkish domination; the poem is an act of cultural reclamation). This meta-theme will sharpen in parts 2–5 when the text deals with Sohráb, the Alexander material, and the final Sassanian kings.

## §KYA-01 — Main Vignette: "The Derafsh Kaviani Fragment"

### Setting and Context (1367)

The Ilkhanid Mongol court at Tabriz had produced the greatest illustrated Shah-Nameh ever made — the "Great Mongol Shah-Nameh" (c. 1330–1340), estimated at 280 folios with over 190 miniature paintings, each the size of a book page. After the fall of the Ilkhanate (1335) and the fragmentation of power in Persia under the Jalayirid Sultans, this manuscript was being dispersed: pages sold or separated to pay debts, fund wars, or as diplomatic gifts.

In 1367, the Jalayirid Sultan Uvays I controls Baghdad and Tabriz. A specific set of folios from the Great Mongol Shah-Nameh — the section depicting Feridún's defeat of Zohák and the raising of the Derafsh Kaviani (the leather apron banner) — has ended up in the hands of a Genoese trader in Trebizond who obtained them from a Tabriz merchant fleeing Jalayirid tax pressure. The trader does not know what they are. A Byzantine scholar in Constantinople (Demetrios Kydones, already used in NWI — or a colleague, say, Manuel Chrysoloras's teacher, John Stobaeus) recognizes their significance from a description and arranges their purchase and transfer to Weimar.

The political sensitivity: the Derafsh Kaviani depictions show the legitimacy of kingship coming from a blacksmith's work-apron, not from a royal bloodline. In the context of competing Jalayirid and Ottoman claims to legitimacy in 1367, these images are politically charged — they suggest that the banner of rightful rule can be raised by a craftsman against a tyrant.

### Part 2 Coverage — Mázinderán Campaign through Gíw's Flight from Túrán

#### Pass 1 — Overview

Part 2 opens with Rustem's Seven Labors (the rescue campaign to free Kai-Káús from the White Demon of Mázinderán), moves through the Sohráb tragedy (Rustem unknowingly kills his own son), the full arc of Saiáwush (the most elegiac story in the epic: a prince whose fatal flaw is keeping his word), and ends mid-sentence with Gíw and Kai-khosráu fleeing Túrán toward Persia. This is the emotional core of the Shah-Nameh — three tragedies in sequence, each escalating: the blinded army, the dead son, the murdered prince.

The poem's moral logic sharpens here: Rustem (who can fight anything) cannot undo the damage done by the kings he serves; the catastrophes that require his intervention are always caused by royal pride or cruelty. Kai-Káús's refusal to give Sohráb the healing medicine is the poem's darkest moment — not demonic evil, just ordinary spite from a king who is tired of feeling humiliated by his champion.

#### Pass 2 — Structure (Part 2)

**Seven Labors of Rustem (rescue of Kai-Káús from Mázinderán):**
- Kai-Káús ignored Zál's warning and invaded Mázinderán; the White Demon's army blinded and captured the entire Persian force including the king.
- Rustem sets out alone (with Rakush) on the seven-stage road to Mázinderán:
  1. Rakush kills a lion while Rustem sleeps; Rustem is annoyed and lectures the horse for fighting without permission.
  2. Desert without water; a sheep leads him to a spring.
  3. A dragon attacks Rakush repeatedly at night; Rustem wakes each time but sees nothing (the dragon is invisible in darkness). Third attack: Rustem finally sees it by moonlight and kills it.
  4. An enchanted sorceress offers food and wine in a beautiful forest glade; she transforms into her true hag form when Rustem speaks God's name — he cuts her in two.
  5. Dark void section / a cornfield; an incident with Aúlád the governor, whom Rustem defeats and captures, binding him to act as guide. (Rustem promises Aúlád the governorship of Mázinderán if he helps — this promise is honored at the end.)
  6. Rustem kills Arzang, the demon governor of Mázinderán.
  7. White Demon's cave battle — the White Demon is enormous, white-furred, described as a mountain. Rustem tears out its liver and heart; the blood from the heart (dripped into Kai-Káús's eyes) cures the king's blindness and restores sight to all captives.

- Return: king of Mázinderán turned to stone by sorcery; Rustem lifts the stone; king revealed, refuses to convert, executed. Aúlád made governor as promised.

**Kai-Káús's Eagle-Throne Flight (hubris escalation):**
- Iblís, in the guise of a young companion, suggests Kai-Káús build a throne borne aloft by trained eagles (eagles attached to the throne's corners by meat on javelins, so the birds fly upward seeking the meat). Kai-Káús has the device built, flies into the air "to see if Heaven could be conquered." The eagles tire; he crash-lands in China. The Chinese emperor sends word to Persia. He is rescued and returned.
- Persian nobles are furious: "Why must a king be spared the consequences of his own foolishness?" The poem answers: he is spared because Rustem is there to spare him, always.

**The Sohráb Tragedy (the epic's central grief):**
- Rustem visits Samengán on a hunt; his horse Rakush is stolen. He goes to the king's house; Tahmíneh (the king's daughter) comes to him at night, they marry by consent. He leaves her the great gold bracelet from his arm: "If your child is a daughter, put this in her hair; if a son, bind it on his arm as his heritage."
- Sohráb is born enormous and strong; at age one he looks three, at three he looks five. He demands to know his father's name; Tahmíneh tells him and he begins training immediately for a campaign to find Rustem.
- Afrásiyáb, learning of this, sends Sohráb to invade Persia — but gives orders that the two must never be told each other's identity. He wants Sohráb to kill Rustem, or Rustem to kill Sohráb; either outcome weakens Persia.
- Sohráb defeats the Persian garrison commander Hujír (takes him prisoner) and the warrior-dame Gúrd-afríd (who disguises herself as a man to fight him; when he unmasks her, she escapes to the fort on horseback and insults him from the walls).
- Kai-Káús sends for Rustem; Rustem delays (feasting with Zál for five days). Kai-Káús rages and sends a threatening letter — Rustem returns insulting the king and nearly quits. Eventually, Tús smooths it over.
- **The deception**: Sohráb repeatedly asks Hujír to identify which warrior is Rustem. Hujír lies each time (to prevent Sohráb from taking his father's side and destroying the Persian army).
- Two days of combat: Day 1 ends in draw (Sohráb throws Rustem but Rustem invokes the "two-falls" custom: "It is the law in Persia that the first fall is not mortal; the kill requires a second throw." This "law" may not be real; Rustem invented it to gain time.) Day 2: Rustem recovers, throws Sohráb, kills him with a dagger.
- Dying Sohráb shows the bracelet on his arm: "My father will avenge me — he is the great Rustem." Rustem cannot speak. He tears the bracelet from Sohráb's arm to prove to himself it is real. He begs Kai-Káús for the healing medicine from the royal treasury (one dose can cure any wound if applied in time). Kai-Káús refuses — he is afraid that father and son together would be too powerful; he lets Sohráb die from spite and fear.
- Sohráb dies. Rustem burns his own tent and all his possessions in grief. Tahmíneh learns and dies of grief within the year.

**The Saiáwush Story (the epic's most extended tragic arc):**
- Kai-Káús takes a forest girl (Súdáveh) as wife. Their son Saiáwush is born; Rustem tutors him for years in all kingly arts. When Saiáwush comes of age and enters court, his stepmother Súdáveh attempts seduction; he refuses. She falsely accuses him of assault. To prove innocence, Saiáwush walks through fire (a line of bonfires 100 meters long) and emerges unhurt. Kai-Káús acknowledges the verdict but does not punish Súdáveh.
- Afrásiyáb prepares a new invasion. Saiáwush leads the Persian army to Balkh and achieves a decisive victory; Afrásiyáb sends Gersíwaz (his brother) with peace terms: one hundred noble hostages and return of all disputed provinces. Saiáwush accepts and sends the terms to Kai-Káús via Rustem.
- Kai-Káús, influenced by rumors and spite, refuses to ratify. He sends the general Tús to continue the war and orders Saiáwush to return with the hostages.
- Saiáwush's crisis: he has sworn an oath to Afrásiyáb to honor the treaty. Breaking the oath would shame him; obeying Kai-Káús would leave him without honor. He chooses a third option — defects to Afrásiyáb's court rather than break his word. He writes to Kai-Káús: "God was my witness at the fire ordeal; God is my witness now. I will not break my oath."
- Afrásiyáb receives him with full honors (gold and incense scattered, his portrait already known); he marries Gúlshaher (called Jaríra), daughter of Pírán; then, at Pírán's suggestion and as political alliance, also marries Ferangís (Afrásiyáb's own daughter). Given sovereignty of Khoten.
- Saiáwush builds a glorious palace at Khoten — covers the walls with portraits of every great king and warrior of both Persia and Túrán (including Rustem, Zál, Kai-Kobád, Afrásiyáb). He is described as not happy: he weeps whenever he thinks of his exile.
- Gúlshaher, left in her father's house, gives birth to a son — Ferúd. (Ferúd's story is a later episode in the epic, not reached yet.)
- **Gersíwaz's betrayal**: Gersíwaz is jealous of Saiáwush's influence. He slanders him to Afrásiyáb — says Saiáwush is gathering an army to overthrow Túrán. Afrásiyáb doesn't believe it but sends Gersíwaz back to Saiáwush with a friendly letter asking him to come. Gersíwaz then secretly tells Saiáwush not to comply immediately, that it would look weak. Saiáwush delays. Gersíwaz reports the delay to Afrásiyáb as proof of treachery.
- Afrásiyáb sends an army; Saiáwush tries to flee but all his Iranians are killed; he is captured on foot. Ferangís pleads for his life in a long poem (invoking Rustem, Kai-Káús, God, and prophecy). Afrásiyáb is briefly moved but not deterred. He orders Gersíwaz to execute Saiáwush: "Do not let his blood touch the earth" (concerned about the blood-curse of innocent blood). Saiáwush prays that a son be born to avenge him. The executioner seizes him by the hair and cuts his head off. A few drops of blood do reach the soil — a tree grows there, "the Saiáwush tree," with wondrous virtues. The blood was carried to Afrásiyáb in a golden dish.
- Ferangís is pregnant. Afrásiyáb orders her killed to prevent any heir. Pírán (the good general) intercedes: "Would you kill your own daughter? Would you kill her unborn child?" Afrásiyáb relents under protest. When the child is born (a son), Pírán tells Afrásiyáb the child was abandoned in the wild. He hides the boy (named Kai-khosráu, per Saiáwush's final request) with peasants on Mount Kalún.
- Afrásiyáb dreams of Kai-khosráu's birth. He discovers Pírán lied. Pírán defends himself; shamed, Afrásiyáb lets it go — he has been tormented with nightmares since Saiáwush's murder and is beginning to repent.
- Kai-khosráu grows up with the peasants, shows extraordinary gifts. Pírán visits him, tells him the truth about his parentage, instructs him to feign madness if brought before Afrásiyáb. The stratagem works: Afrásiyáb is convinced the heir is an idiot and dismisses him.

**Rustem's revenge and Gíw's quest:**
- Rustem arrives at Kai-Káús's court to mourn Saiáwush, blames Súdáveh, kills her on the spot (Kai-Káús is too ashamed to object). Leads the Persian army into Túrán; takes Sarkhá (Afrásiyáb's son) prisoner and executes him the same way Saiáwush was killed (as direct mirror-vengeance). Afrásiyáb flees east. Rustem rules Túrán from the throne for approximately seven years.
- **Gíw's quest**: The elder Gúdarz dreams of Kai-khosráu, who tells him Gíw (Gúdarz's son) is the one destined to bring him back to Persia. Gíw travels alone into Tartary, surviving on wild ass meat, with no guides (he kills each guide he uses to prevent discovery). Finds Kai-khosráu sitting by a fountain — they recognize each other immediately, Kai-khosráu from the portraits in his father's gallery. Ferangís is retrieved; they take Behzád (Saiáwush's horse, kept in Afrásiyáb's stables). Gíw defeats a pursuit force of 300 horsemen single-handedly.

**Part 2 ends mid-sentence** with the three fugitives (Gíw, Kai-khosráu, Ferangís) still in flight toward Persia. The story continues in part 3.

### Part 3 Coverage — Escape Completed through Beginning of Isfendiyár's Seven Labors

#### Pass 1 — Overview

Part 3 opens with the Jihún crossing (Kai-khosráu, Ferangís, and Gíw swim the flooded river on horseback while Afrásiyáb watches helplessly from the far bank), covers the entire reign of Kai-khosráu (coronation through his mysterious vanishing), and ends mid-sentence at the start of Isfendiyár's Seven Labors. This is the most narratively varied section of the epic: it includes the poem's finest comic episode (Byzun and Maníjeh), its darkest instance of father-and-son cruelty (Gushtásp imprisoning Isfendiyár), and its most mystical ending (Kai-khosráu walking into a fountain and disappearing, his warriors dying in a snowstorm).

The Shah-Nameh's central question — what makes a king legitimate? — gets its fullest treatment here. Kai-khosráu is repeatedly contrasted with his grandfather Kai-Káús: where Káús was vain, spiteful, and incapable of admitting error, Khosráu is just, generous, and ultimately transcendent. His disappearance is not presented as death but as voluntary translation — the poem's only king who chooses to leave rather than being removed.

A major historical-religious seam runs through the final section: the introduction of Zoroaster (Zerdusht) and the transition to fire-worship, which the poem treats as a genuine spiritual advance over "pure monotheism" but also as the source of a new kind of religious war (against Arjásp's demons). This places the Gushtásp/Zerdusht episode exactly on the border between myth and history that the Shah-Nameh navigates throughout.

#### Pass 2 — Structure (Part 3)

**Jihún crossing — escape completed:**
- Afrásiyáb instructs all ferrymen on the Jihún to bar the fugitives; the ferrymen demand exorbitant prices (horse, crown, damsel). Gíw refuses all; invokes the precedent of Kavah crossing in armor to bring Feridún. Kai-khosráu, Ferangís, and Gíw plunge their horses into the flood. All three cross safely. Afrásiyáb arrives to see them landing on the Persian bank — can only watch.
- Gíw captures Pírán with his kamund. Ferangís intercedes: "He saved my life and Kai-khosráu's." Gíw has sworn to stain the earth with his blood. Kai-khosráu's compromise: pierce Pírán's ears, let the blood drops fall, then release him. The "humane fraud" satisfies the oath. Pírán is released.

**Coronation dispute:**
- Tús refuses to honor Kai-khosráu (grandson of the enemy Afrásiyáb, via Ferangís) and supports Fríburz (Kai-Káús's surviving son). Gúdarz defends Kai-khosráu with a famous speech that explicitly invokes Kavah the Blacksmith: "Need I blush to be descended from Kavah? He tore up the register of blood, raised the banner of freedom. The empire owes its liberty to a blacksmith."
- Test at the enchanted fortress: Fríburz sends an army; men burned, fortress inaccessible in fire. Kai-khosráu writes the name of God on his javelin point — the fires die out. He takes the fortress. The result decides the succession.

**Ferúd episode (Saiáwush's other son):**
- Saiáwush had two sons: Kai-khosráu (by Ferangís) and Ferúd (by Gúlshaher/Jaríra). Ferúd lives in a fort near Balkh with his mother. Kai-khosráu explicitly orders the army to bypass his fort. Tús defies the order ("I prefer the cultivated road"). Ferúd fights to defend himself; kills the horse of Tús; wounds Byzun. Báhrám kills Ferúd treacherously. Gúlshaher rushes out, clasps her dead son, stabs herself with a dagger. Tús imprisoned. The episode is a direct replay of the Saiáwush tragedy: a son of the same father, killed by the same fault (royal stubbornness), mourned by the same kind of devoted woman.

**Major campaigns:**
- Long war; Rustem defeats Kámús (seized by kamund, dragged and killed); defeats the Khakán of Chín (kamund from elephant); defeats Púladwund (wrestling match — Rustem breaks him on the plain, Púladwund pretends to be dead and escapes; Rustem in pursuit).
- Note: Rustem at the end of the Barzú episode states he is "four hundred years old" and requests rest. This is the poem's first hint that Rustem's era is ending.

**Akwán Díw:**
- A demon disguised as a miraculous wild ass. Rustem hunts it but it vanishes each time. He falls asleep; Akwán carries him suspended on a tuft of earth. Offers him a choice: sea or mountain. Rustem, knowing demons act by contraries, asks for the mountain (wanting the sea). Thrown into the ocean. Fights off crocodiles. Swims to shore. Returns to find Rakush missing, among Afrásiyáb's herd. Steals the entire herd. Meets Akwán Díw again, finally catches him in the kamund, kills him, sends the severed head to Kai-khosráu.

**Byzun and Maníjeh:**
- The poem's extended romantic-comic masterpiece. Byzun, son of Gíw, is sent to destroy wild boars in Armán. Girgín (his companion) grows jealous of his glory and lures him toward Maníjeh's summer garden, describing it in ecstatic terms. Maníjeh is Afrásiyáb's daughter. She sends her nurse to inquire about the beautiful stranger; love is immediate. Byzun is drugged with wine and a narcotic and carried unconscious to Túrán. Afrásiyáb discovers him; Gersíwaz is sent to execute him. Pírán intercedes again (mirroring the Saiáwush intervention). Compromise: Byzun is thrown in a deep pit, bound with chains, with the immense demon-stone (dragged from the ocean by Akwán Díw) placed over the mouth. Maníjeh is stripped of all jewels and clothes and left barefoot near the pit — forbidden any support. A crack in the stone lets Byzun hear her voice; she feeds him bread and water through the fissure.
- Girgín lies to Gíw; Gíw is not deceived, beats him 200 times. Kai-khosráu uses the Cup of Jemshíd (the great seeing-cup that reveals everything in the seven climes) and sees Byzun alive in the pit, Maníjeh sitting on the demon-stone. Rustem goes as a merchant, a thousand warriors disguised as camel-drivers, a thousand camels laden with jewels. Recognizes Maníjeh; conceals his identity initially; sends her a roasted fowl with his own seal-ring inside. Byzun feels the ring, smiles for the first time in his captivity. Guides Rustem to the pit at night via a fire-signal. Rustem kills the son of Akwán Díw (who guards the stone). Lifts the demon-stone with prayer ("God infused strength through his limbs"). Hauls Byzun up by kamund. Attacks Afrásiyáb's palace (Afrásiyáb flees). Maníjeh is later honored with gifts "worthy of her virtue and fidelity."

**Barzú (Sohráb's son — Rustem's unknown grandchild):**
- After his latest defeat, Afrásiyáb encounters a man of "prodigious strength" on the road to China who does not know his father's name. He is recruited as Afrásiyáb's champion, promised a daughter and a kingdom. Barzú nearly kills Rustem in their first combat (wounds his arm). Rustem sends Ferámurz to fight the next day in his own armor and on Rakush. Ferámurz captures Barzú. Barzú escapes from prison (his mother Sháh-rú conceals a file in his food). Rustem encounters them on a hunt and tries to poison Barzú's food — the singing-girl eats it and dies. Barzú reproaches Rustem for this treachery; they wrestle. Rustem is about to cut off his head when Sháh-rú screams "This is the son of Sohráb! He is your grandchild!" She shows Sohráb's brilliant finger-ring. Rustem stops; embraces Barzú; takes him to Sístán and introduces him to Zál.

**Súsen the Sorceress:**
- Afrásiyáb's female agent sets an enchanted inn near Rustem's palace. Tús and Gúdarz become drunk on charmed wine; Gíw, Byzun, and Ferámurz all captured. Only Zál refuses the enchanted food. Rustem arrives from Sístán; kills Pílsam (the Túránian guardian); the captives are freed.

**Death of Pírán:**
- In the campaign under Gúdarz, after two years of war, Pírán is killed in battle. His death is mourned by Afrásiyáb in explicitly funerary terms. The poem treats Pírán as a tragic figure — the "good general" in service of an unjust cause, always interceding to spare lives, always overruled until his own death.

**Death of Afrásiyáb:**
- Defeated, abandoned, hunted. He hides in a cave. A hermit named Húm (of Feridún's line, driven from his home by Afrásiyáb's tyranny) hears him lamenting in Turkish. Tricks him into leaving the cave by calling "Come out, O king, and obtain your wishes" — a spiritual-seeming call. Strikes him on the forehead, binds him. Delivers him to Kai-khosráu. Afrásiyáb and Gersíwaz are beheaded in the same manner as Saiáwush — heads cut off, blood collected in a golden basin.

**Death of Kai-khosráu:**
- After Afrásiyáb's death, Khosráu loses all desire for earthly sovereignty. Increasingly devoted to prayer. Appoints Lohurásp (a descendant of Húsheng) as successor; distributes Zábul to Rustem; gives Khorassan to Tús; distributes gifts to all warriors. Goes on a final journey to a mysterious fountain. Wades in and vanishes — "not a trace was left behind, and not a dimple on the wave." His warriors (Tús, Fríburz, Gíw, and others) sit down to eat nearby; fall asleep; are buried by an overnight snowstorm. All die. Only Gúdarz, who turned back halfway, survives.

**Lohurásp — a transition king:**
- Just and paternal. Four sons: Ardshír, Shydasp (by Kai-Káús's daughter), and Gushtásp, Zarír (by another wife). Gushtásp is brilliant but proud; defies his father; goes to Rúm disguised as a laborer. Kills a ferocious wolf (secretly, for Mabrín) and a dragon (for Ahrun) to win them Lohurásp's two younger daughters. Marries Kitabún (the king's daughter) based on her dream. Recalled to Irán when Lohurásp wants to resign; Lohurásp retires to Balkh for religious life.

**Gushtásp and Zerdusht (Zoroaster):**
- Zerdusht appears as a historical religious figure — the "Guber" (Zoroastrian priest) who explains the fire-worship doctrines. Gushtásp converts; the fire-worship tree planted before his house gives wisdom to those who eat its leaves. Zerdusht claims to have ascended to heaven on a ladder; to have received the Zendavesta from above; to have seen the seven celestial mansions.
- Religious war: Arjásp (king of Chín and Má-chín, of demon-army composition) demands Gushtásp return to his "ancient religion." Gushtásp refuses. Zarír leads the Persian army; is killed by the demon Bai-derafsh. Isfendiyár (young, brilliant) kills Bai-derafsh and turns the battle. Arjásp flees. Isfendiyár crowned; proceeds to spread the Zoroastrian faith by conquest of Rúm, India, and Arabia.
- Gurzam (a jealous courtier) slanders Isfendiyár to Gushtásp, claiming the prince plans rebellion. Gushtásp, drunk for three days on wine, orders Isfendiyár imprisoned in a mountain tower and chained to four iron pillars. He remains there until Arjásp invades again (killing Lohurásp at Balkh and burning the Zendavesta). Only then does Gushtásp release Isfendiyár.
- Isfendiyár defeats Arjásp's second invasion; the warrior Kurugsar is captured and converted to the Persian cause. Part 3 ends as the Seven Labors of Isfendiyár (the Heft-Khan) begin.

## §Part 4 — KYA-shah-nameh.part4of5.txt (lines 1–449 Shah-Nameh narrative; 452–583 footnotes; 589+ Rubaiyat)

### Pass 1 — Overview

Part 4 completes the Shah-Nameh narrative. It opens with Isfendiyár's Heft-Khan (Seven Labors), the capture of the Brazen Fortress, and then the climactic confrontation between Isfendiyár and Rustem — the two great champions each bound by prophecy to destroy the other. After Isfendiyár's death the epic pivots swiftly through the final Persian dynasties and ends with Sikander (Alexander the Great), whom Firdusi casts as a half-Persian prince. From line 452 to 583 are scholarly footnotes by Atkinson. From line 589 onward the KYA volume transitions entirely to **The Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam** — a separate work by a different author, not part of the Shah-Nameh. The Shah-Nameh portion of KYA is therefore complete with Part 4.

### Pass 2 — Structure

**Isfendiyár's Heft-Khan (Seven Labors)**
Isfendiyár, sent by Gushtásp to free Luhrásp's hostage daughters from the Brazen Fortress held by Arjásp, undertakes seven sequential trials on the road: (1) two fierce wolves; (2) two lions; (3) a dragon overcome using a wheeled cart fitted with sword-blades (Isfendiyár rides inside while the dragon swallows the cart and is cut apart from within); (4) an enchantress whose true form is a hideous hag; (5) a Símúrgh attacked with flame-tipped arrows; (6) crossing a raging flood; (7) a snowstorm. He reaches the Brazen Fortress in disguise as a merchant named "Kherád," hides 160 warriors in merchandise chests, infiltrates the citadel, arms his men at night, kills Arjásp, frees the daughters and Lohurásp's corpse.

**Gushtásp's Machination — Isfendiyár vs. Rustem**
Returning victorious, Isfendiyár expects the throne. Gushtásp, advised by the treacherous Gurázm, instead sends Isfendiyár to chain Rustem and bring him to court — a mission calculated to kill one or both. The confrontation plays out in three days of speeches, feasting, and battle. Rustem and Isfendiyár are equals; both are severely wounded on day one. That night Zál burns a Símúrgh feather. The Símúrgh heals Rustem's wounds and reveals the one vulnerability: Isfendiyár is invulnerable everywhere except the eyes (granted by his divine armor quest). She shows Rustem a specific Kazú-tree and instructs him to cut a forked arrow from it. On day two the Kazú-arrow blinds Isfendiyár; he dies slowly, commending his son Bahman to Rustem's care and naming Gushtásp as the true killer.

**Death of Rustem (Shughad's Pit)**
Rustem is killed by treachery. His half-brother Shughad, aided by the king of Kábul, digs seven concealed pits on a hunting road, each lined with sword-blades and spear-points. Rustem rides into the first pit; his horse Rakush follows. Mortally wounded on the spear-blades, Rustem still draws his bow and shoots Shughad through a plane-tree he hides behind. Ferámurz recovers Rustem's body and bones. Bahman later takes revenge on Zál and Ferámurz.

**Húmaí and Dáráb**
Gushtásp's daughter Húmaí, pregnant by Dáráb (or by the king in some versions), cannot acknowledge the child. She places the baby in a gilded box and sets it on the Euphrates. A washerman finds the box and raises the boy Dáráb. Dáráb grows up, rejoins the court, and becomes king. His son Dárá picks up the line to Alexander.

**Sikander (Alexander the Great)**
Firdusi frames Alexander as a half-Persian: Dáráb briefly married a Macedonian princess (Náhíd), sent her home when he found her breath disagreeable, and she bore his son Sikander (Alexander) without telling anyone. Sikander thus fights and defeats his own half-brother Dárá. Before the war Sikander infiltrates Dárá's court disguised as his own "envoy," memorizes the court, and secretly takes four golden goblets from Dárá's table. Three great battles. Dárá is killed by two of his own ministers; Sikander holds the dying king, weeps, and sends for justice. Sikander conquers India using iron horse-shaped fire-machines against war elephants. He finds the prophetic trees (one speaks at sunrise in Persian, one at sunset in Greek) that tell him he will not return home alive. He builds the Wall of Gog and Magog between two mountains. He dies at Kashán.

**Firdusi's Invocation and Satire on Mahmúd**
After the Shah-Nameh narrative ends, Firdusi appends his own Invocation (a brief exaltation of the poem's completion) and then his famous Satire on Sultan Mahmúd — a bitter poem complaining that after thirty years of labor Mahmúd paid him in silver coins instead of the promised gold, and wishing the sultan various misfortunes. The Satire is a celebrated piece of Persian literary history in its own right.
