# BEY — Mandeville's Travels — attributed to Sir John Mandeville
## Source: BEY-mandeville.txt (c.1357 — original Old French; English translation used here)
## Note on File: ~472 KB — size gate applies; drawn from memory. Written c. 1357. Claims authorship by Sir John Mandeville, English knight of St. Albans; the author's historical identity is uncertain or fictional. The book is a travel memoir compiled substantially from earlier travelers (Friar Odoric of Pordenone, William of Boldensele, Plano Carpini, the Letter of Alexander) and presented in the first person as eyewitness account. Written only 10 years before the game's 1367 setting — the most contemporary source in this pipeline.

---

## Cycle 1 — Angle: *The Sober Draft — What the compiler knew before he added the wonders*

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

Mandeville's Travels is the most-read travel book of the late medieval period and one of the least trustworthy as personal account. It was written around 1357, probably in French, probably in Liège (the book says the author retired there for medical treatment and wrote the account from memory), and the author claims to be an English knight named John Mandeville of St. Albans who left England in 1322 and spent thirty-three years traveling the East before illness forced his return. There is no contemporary evidence that this man existed.

What the book actually is: a careful and intelligent compilation of earlier travel literature, personal testimony, and invented material, assembled into a first-person travel memoir of extraordinary readability. The author was clearly educated, probably a cleric, and read widely in the travel literature available to him. He took Friar Odoric of Pordenone's account of his travels in Asia (c. 1330) and used it extensively, sometimes verbatim. He used William of Boldensele's account of the Holy Land and Egypt (c. 1336). He used the Letter of Alexander (a medieval compilation of fantastic Eastern geography attributed to Alexander the Great). He used encyclopedic and geographical sources like Vincent of Beauvais. He assembled these into a readable first-person account and added enough personal detail — the name John Mandeville, the St. Albans origin, the 1322 departure date, the account of his medical condition in Liège — to make it seem like memoir.

The deception, if it is one, is extremely accomplished. The book has two distinct registers: the Holy Land and Near East section, which is primarily reliable geographic and diplomatic description borrowed from contemporary travelers who actually went there; and the Eastern section (India, China, and beyond), which becomes increasingly fantastical as it moves further from verifiable sources.

**The Holy Land Section (Chapters 1–15):** The route from Europe to the Holy Land is described in practical detail: ports of embarkation, overland routes through Anatolia, the sea crossing to Cyprus, the coasts of Asia Minor, the approaches to Jerusalem. The description of Constantinople is accurate for the period — the Hagia Sophia, the column of Constantine, the double walls, the Venetian quarter. The description of Alexandria and Cairo is similarly reliable, drawing from Boldensele's account of the 1330s. Jerusalem and the Holy Places are described with the emphasis of a pilgrim guide: the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, the dimensions of the Tomb, the route of the Via Dolorosa. The Saracen sultan of Egypt is described as a reasonable man who has conversations with European visitors about theology and governance — whether any such conversation occurred, or whether it is a literary device for presenting a comparison of Christian and Islamic governance, is unclear.

The siege of Jerusalem and the Crusader history are woven into the geographic description. Mandeville is interested in what places looked like and in the human infrastructure around them — the pilgrim hostels, the market customs, the costs of passage — as well as in the theological significance of each location.

**The Eastern Section (Chapters 16–34):** As the narrative moves east of known Crusader and merchant travel, the reliability decreases and the wonders increase. Persia is plausible; India becomes the source of spices and precious stones and also of dog-headed men (the Cynocephali), a race that was ancient in Greek geographical tradition and appears in Pliny and in the Letter of Alexander. The island of Dondin is inhabited by people who eat their own dead rather than let them be eaten by worms — Mandeville presents this as reasonable practice, which is one of his recurring moves: to present customs that European readers would find shocking as internally logical within their own context.

Further east: the kingdom of the Great Khan (the Mongol empire), described using material from Plano Carpini's 13th-century account; the island of Lamori where people go naked and hold all property in common; the island of Dondin; Cathay (China); a country where people have no heads and faces in their chests (the Blemmyae, another ancient tradition); the Valley Perilous, a haunted magnetic valley; the diamond mountains; the kingdom of Prester John.

Prester John occupies the last section of the book. He is a mythical Christian king beyond the lands of the Saracens — sometimes identified with the Mongol ruler, sometimes with the Ethiopian emperor, sometimes as a purely imaginary figure. Mandeville's account of Prester John's kingdom is the book's most elaborate and most fantastical, combining the paradise garden tradition with the political fantasy of a Christian ruler who could outflank Islam from the east. Prester John has been the subject of European diplomatic and military interest since the 12th century; letters purportedly from him circulated in European courts; the Portuguese later tried systematically to find him in Africa. He does not exist.

**The Compilation Method:** The author's selection and arrangement of his sources is not merely plagiaristic — he exercises genuine editorial judgment. He cuts material that doesn't serve his narrative, adds bridging passages, adjusts the tone to maintain the memoir register, and in several places explicitly notes that he heard a story but cannot verify it. The authorial persona is careful and self-aware; when he doesn't know something, he says so; when he reports a wonder he has not seen himself, he attributes it ("I have been told that..."). The persona of a responsible witness is built into the compilation to make the compilation credible.

**The 1367 Context:** The Travels was completed approximately 1357. In 1367, it is a contemporary document — ten years old, being widely copied and read across Europe, still being translated from French into Latin, English, German, and Flemish. The debate about its reliability has not yet hardened into the scholarly consensus that it is substantially fictitious. In 1367, a reader would encounter it as a recent travel account by a living or recently deceased author. The distinction between "reliable sections" and "fantastical sections" is not yet clearly drawn in European scholarship.

#### Chapter Notes

- **Chapters 1–7 (Routes to Jerusalem)**: Practical pilgrim guide material; reliable geography; Boldensele and direct travel literature.
- **Chapters 8–15 (Jerusalem and the Near East)**: Accurate to contemporary knowledge; pilgrim emphasis; the sultan dialogue as literary device.
- **Chapters 16–22 (Persia, India, the first islands)**: Transition zone; increasingly unreliable as the route moves beyond merchant knowledge.
- **Chapters 23–29 (The Far East, the Great Khan)**: Odoric material, Plano Carpini material; selective and rearranged; some accurate observation mixed with compilation.
- **Chapters 30–34 (Prester John, the Paradise Garden)**: The most fantastic section; paradise geography and political fantasy; the least traceable to actual sources.

### Pass 2 — Characters

**"Mandeville"** — The most interesting character in the book is the author persona. He is cautious in his claims, consistent in his register, occasionally self-deprecating, and occasionally pompous. He says he has met the sultan; he says he was offered a great position in the sultan's court but declined for reasons of Christian faith; he says he once saw a tree that produces lambs in place of fruit (the Vegetable Lamb of Tartary — a real medieval legend about cotton) and carefully notes that he cannot explain how this is possible. The persona is not cynical; the author seems to have believed most of what he wrote, in the sense that a man who compiles existing literature tends to believe his sources even when they are wrong.

**The Sultan of Egypt** — Appears in the early section as a model of enlightened rule. The sultan-Mandeville dialogue is probably invented but presents an interesting mirror: the sultan's questions reveal what a reasonably intelligent Islamic ruler might ask about European Christianity; Mandeville's answers are those of a thoughtful layman rather than a polemicist. The sultan's conclusion — that Christians do not live up to their own principles — is presented without irony.

**Prester John** — The absent center of the book's last section. He is the object of European desire — the powerful Christian king who does not exist — and Mandeville's description of his kingdom is the book's most elaborate invention. Everything Prester John has is the best of everything: his realm is the richest, his court the most magnificent, his faith the purest. He has rivers of precious stones and a table made of amethyst to discourage drunkenness. He is a fantasy of what Christendom could be if it were a success rather than a set of competing principalities.

**The Dog-Headed Men** — The Cynocephali represent Mandeville's treatment of the monstrous races. He presents them not as monsters but as peoples with their own customs, their own logic, their own theology (they worship the ox, the most useful animal). The move is characteristic: what looks like a monster from outside has an internally consistent culture. This is probably the most intellectually interesting move in the Eastern section, and it recurs throughout — Mandeville consistently contextualizes the "wonders" rather than simply reporting them as exotic.

---

### Pass 3 — The Pivotal Scene: The Sultan's Question

In the Jerusalem section, Mandeville describes being received by the sultan of Egypt (whether Mamluk or Ayyubid is historically uncertain in the account). The sultan has read a report on the customs of Christians compiled by his scribes and wants to ask the visitor about what he has found.

He says: I have read about your faith. I see that you have all the best commandments — love your neighbors, do good, give to the poor, treat others as you would be treated. I have read your scriptures. And then I see how your people actually live — the wars between Christian princes, the corruption of the clergy, the poor not given to. And I think: you have the best instructions I have read from any religion, and you follow them the least. We follow ours less well than we should, but the gap between our instructions and our practice is not as large as yours.

Mandeville's persona says: I could not answer. I was ashamed.

The sultan gives him a safe conduct through his territory out of sympathy rather than contempt. He says he is treating the traveler well because the commandments say so, not because the traveler has earned it.

This scene is the closest the book comes to genuine moral argument. It is also almost certainly invented — the sultan's speech is too perfect, too pointed, too rhetorically well-formed to be a traveler's recalled conversation. But it is presented as true, and the author's shame is presented as genuine. It is the book's version of what the journey found: the traveler went east to see holy places and found the mirror. The most honest observation in a book full of compiled others is the single invented scene where the author is told what he already knew.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **"The Sober Draft"** — A Liège monastery has a manuscript that appears to be an earlier version of Mandeville's Travels, without the dog-headed men, headless people, and Prester John material. The sober draft contains the reliable geographic and diplomatic sections and ends at the border of reliable travel knowledge, with a note that says: beyond this, I have only what others have told me. The standard text adds two hundred pages of compiled wonders. Fighter escorts the sober draft from Liège to Weimar before a Dominican inquisitor can assess whether it constitutes evidence of deliberate deception.

2. **"The Sultan's Safe Conduct"** — Mandeville describes being given a safe conduct through Egyptian territory by the sultan. If such a document existed, it would be a formal diplomatic instrument issued by the Mamluk chancery to a European pilgrim. A Mamluk archive in Cairo has a collection of such safe conducts from the 1320s–1340s. One of them is issued to a European knight whose name is given in an Arabic transliteration that could be read as a version of "Mandeville." Fighter escorts the collection.

3. **"The Odoric Annotations"** — Friar Odoric of Pordenone's account of his Eastern travels (c. 1330) is one of Mandeville's main sources. An Italian monastery has a copy of Odoric's text with annotations in a different hand, marked where the text was used in Mandeville's Travels — the annotator was identifying the compilation in real time. Fighter escorts the annotated copy.

4. **"The Prester John Letter"** — One of the medieval letters purportedly from Prester John to European kings, written in approximately the 12th century, survives in a German monastery archive. In 1367, it is being debated whether Prester John is the Ethiopian emperor (recently contacted by a Portuguese ship) or a fictional figure. The letter is evidence for neither position — it is a fiction that has taken on the weight of primary source. Fighter escorts it.

5. **"The Cotton-Lamb Record"** — The "Vegetable Lamb of Tartary" (cotton plants described as producing lambs) appears in Mandeville. A merchant's account book from a Genoese trading house in Caffa (Crimea) from the 1330s contains an entry for "cotton wool" with a marginal note explaining to an apparently puzzled correspondent that the product does not actually come from lambs. The note is the earliest debunking of the legend. Fighter escorts the account book.

6. **"Boldensele's Original"** — William of Boldensele's account of his travels in Egypt and the Holy Land (c. 1336) is one of Mandeville's primary sources for the reliable sections. The original manuscript of Boldensele's account — as opposed to the many copies made after Mandeville's use of it made it famous — survives in a Rhineland monastery. Fighter escorts it to a neutral archive before it is displaced by the Mandeville-derived copies.

7. **"The Missing Chapter"** — Versions of Mandeville's Travels in different language traditions contain different material; the English version has passages not in the French, and the Latin has passages not in either. A German monastery has a manuscript of the Latin version that contains an additional chapter not found in any other known manuscript, describing a conversation with a Jewish scholar in Jerusalem about the Old Testament geography of the Eastern routes. Fighter escorts the manuscript.

## Theme Check

**Existing themes to avoid**: all previously listed themes (see latest quest-map.md Theme Threads Active list).

**BEY theme chosen**: *The compiled record is not a lie — the assembler of many witnesses is himself a witness to what they all saw; what makes the standard text dishonest is not the compilation but the wonders added after, when the evidence ran out; the sober draft is truer than the finished book because it stops where the knowledge stops.*

This is distinct from all above. The nearest neighbors are "authorship as claim" (DQX) and "the wound as witness" (LIS — damage as proof). Neither covers: the compiler-as-witness; the honesty of incompletion (the draft that stops at the limit of evidence rather than filling the rest with invention); the distinction between the reliable geography and the fantastic supplement as the document's most diagnostic feature.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Sober Draft*

### The Token

*The Liège Manuscript — a manuscript in Old French, approximately 1356, containing a travel account describing routes from Europe to the Holy Land, Egypt, Constantinople, Persia, and the borders of India. The account ends at the Malabar coast with a note: "Beyond this I have only what others told me and I will not set down as my own what I have not seen myself." The handwriting matches descriptions of Mandeville's manuscript; the Liège monastery has a tradition that the author lodged there before his death. This version stops a hundred pages before the standard text ends. The hundred missing pages are where the dog-headed men live.*

---

**ACT I**

*The Liège Manuscript — on a reading stand in the monastery library — at the Monastery of Saint-Laurent, Liège, morning.*

Brother Gilles has been the monastery's librarian for twenty-two years. He received the manuscript in the library's care when a man described as a traveled English knight made a small donation and left a box of papers, of which the manuscript was the only legible and complete item. That was in 1362. Since then, the standard Mandeville text has spread across Europe — translated into English, German, Latin, Flemish — and Gilles has compared it to the manuscript. He knows the difference. He has told three people about it. Two of them wanted to destroy it (the Dominican who thought it showed Mandeville had invented his credentials) and one wanted to publish it (a humanist in Cologne who wanted to expose the standard text as the embellishment it partly is).

He has sent word to a contact in Weimar asking for someone trustworthy to carry the manuscript.

The Dominican chapter in Liège has been told about the manuscript by one of the two who wanted to destroy it and is sending a representative to assess it. The representative will arrive in three days.

*— Three days is the measure of everything. The Dominican's question will be: did Mandeville lie? The answer the manuscript gives is: he stopped lying at the Malabar coast.*

> **Skill Check: Insight DC 12.** Understand what Brother Gilles is asking the Fighter to carry: not evidence of deception but evidence of honesty — the draft that stopped at the limit of evidence is more truthful than the finished text, and both are about to be collapsed into "Mandeville lied" by a Dominican who wants a simpler story.

**Fail:** Gilles hesitates. He wants to write a covering letter to the Archivus explaining the comparison between the two texts. He needs a few hours. The Fighter waits in the guesthouse; the Dominican representative arrives at the gate at dusk. Gilles hands over the manuscript and the covering letter at the same time — the Dominican is still at the gate being received by the abbot.

**Pass:** Gilles reads the Fighter's understanding of the situation and relaxes. He takes the manuscript from the reading stand himself, wraps it in an oilcloth cover with practiced hands, and says: "Tell the Archivus it stops at the Malabar coast because that's where Mandeville's sources stopped. That's what honest looks like."

**TOKEN GRANTED: The Liège Manuscript** — Old French travel account in a monastery-made oilcloth wrapping, stopping at the Malabar coast; the draft that refused the wonders.

---

**ACT II**

*The Liège Manuscript — in the pack — on the Rhine road south of Liège, late morning.*

A bookseller named Jakob operates between Liège and Cologne, dealing in manuscripts, printed broadsheets, and copies of recently popular texts. He has heard, through Gilles's leak, that a Mandeville variant exists. He has been trying to get a look at it for three months. He intercepts the Fighter on the road with the air of a man who has been slightly too obvious about waiting.

He does not want to acquire it — he is a dealer, not a collector. He wants to commission a copy. He says he will take only one week; the Fighter can stay in Cologne at his expense while he has it transcribed; the original goes to Weimar and he sells his copy to the Cologne humanists. The copy will make the comparison between the draft and the standard text public, which is what the humanist in Cologne already wanted.

He has the logic of a man in the printing business: publication = preservation. The copy spreading across the Rhineland will ensure the draft's existence even if the original is lost or seized.

*— He is right that the copy would spread the knowledge. He is wrong that this is the same as preserving the evidence. The physical manuscript is the evidence; copies of the text are arguments. Gilles gave the Fighter a piece of evidence, not an argument.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 12.** Explain to Jakob that a copy of the text and the original manuscript are two different things — the physical document is the proof of priority; a copy, however accurate, is a claim. The Weimar archive can authorize copies after authentication; a bookseller's copy is a copy of an unauthenticated text.

**Fail:** Jakob is not satisfied but doesn't want to cause trouble on the road. He says he will be in Weimar in six months and expects to see the transcription authorization waiting. He steps aside.

**Pass:** He accepts the distinction. He is a practical man; authentication before copying is how the certified copies become legally usable. He steps aside and gives the Fighter a Rhine road map with the better waystation inns marked.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT III**

*The Liège Manuscript — in the pack — in the Ardennes highlands, two days south of Liège.*

The Dominican representative who arrived at Saint-Laurent has confirmed through the abbot that the manuscript left that morning. He has sent two of his escort riders ahead on the faster road to intercept. They are ahead of the Fighter and waiting at the road junction where the Cologne road splits from the Rhine road.

They are not violent men — they are escort riders doing what they were told. They want the manuscript returned to Saint-Laurent for assessment by the Dominican chapter. They say this is the chapter's authority over materials in a monastery under their supervision. They are not wrong that this authority exists; they are wrong that it extends to a manuscript already in transit under private commission.

The junction is wide enough that they cannot simply block the road, but there are two of them and one Fighter.

*— They believe their authority is clear. It isn't, quite. But arguing canon law on a road junction is not how this ends.*

> **Combat: 2 Dominican Escort Riders** — AC 13, HP 20 each (mounted, experienced escorts; they yield at 50% HP if offered a face-saving exit). The horses are an advantage on open ground; the road junction has hedgerows on both sides that negate cavalry advantage if the Fighter moves into them.

After combat, the road south is clear. The Ardennes begin to drop toward the Rhine valley.

> **Skill Check: Perception DC 10.** Check the oilcloth wrapping — the manuscript took a knock when the pack went down during the fight.

**Pass or Fail:** The oilcloth held either way. The manuscript is intact.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT IV**

*The Liège Manuscript — in the pack — at the chapter house of a Rhineland abbey, one night south of Mainz.*

The abbot of this abbey has heard about the Dominican interest and is a Dominican ally. He has been told to receive the manuscript if the Fighter passes through and hold it for collection. He does not attempt to seize the manuscript outright — he is too careful for that — but he presents a canonical hospitality argument: a traveler carrying a contested religious document through his territory owes the local church a disclosure of the document's content and destination, which he as a chapter superior can assess for doctrinal implications.

He is not wrong that such an obligation exists, technically. He is using it as a procedural delay to allow the Dominican representative to catch up.

*— He wants the document assessed. What he actually fears is that the Malabar stopping point, if authenticated and published, will confirm that the standard Mandeville text was embellished after the reliable sections were written — which implicates the Church's circulation of those embellishments as travel literature.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 13.** Argue that the doctrinal assessment of a travel document is not within the chapter's authority without a formal ecclesiastical inquiry, and that the Weimar archive has a recognized process for receiving documents under contention — the appropriate channel for a contested religious document is the neutral archive, not a chapter house that has been notified in advance by one of the parties. The abbot understands procedural argument.

**Fail:** He exercises the hospitality hold for one night. The Dominican representative does not arrive in time. He releases the Fighter in the morning with a written note of the hold and its release, which the Fighter must carry to Weimar as documentation of the chain of custody.

**Pass:** The abbot recognizes the procedural argument — it is the same one he would use himself in a different context. He releases without the hold and does not require a receipt. "God speed," he says, which in this context means: I hope we're on the right side of this.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT V**

*The Liège Manuscript — in the pack — at the Weimar archive intake desk, Archivus Sweelinck, afternoon.*

The Archivus unwraps the oilcloth and looks at the manuscript. He opens it at several places. He opens the standard Mandeville text he has in the archive's reference section and compares passages. He reads the last page of the Liège Manuscript — the note about the Malabar coast and the refusal to add what others told him — and sits back.

"This version stops where the evidence stopped." He is not asking. He is reading aloud what the manuscript says.

He opens the intake form.

"The standard text continues for another hundred pages with dog-headed men and the kingdom of Prester John." He looks up. "Is this a draft or is this the completed work?"

*— The question is not rhetorical. The classification depends on the answer. A "draft" is categorized differently from a "completed work" — a draft is a step toward something, a completed work stands on its own. Gilles said: it stops at the Malabar coast because that's where his sources stopped. That's what honest looks like. It's not a draft toward the embellished version — it's the book Mandeville should have published.*

> **Skill Check: History DC 12.** Determine from internal evidence whether the Liège Manuscript reads as an incomplete draft or as a concluded work: the Malabar coast note is framed as a concluding decision ("I will not set down as my own what I have not seen myself"), not as a placeholder ("more to be added"). It is a completed work that refused expansion, not an unfinished draft that was later completed with invention.

**Fail:** The Archivus files it as "Draft — Mandeville's Travels, earlier version, Liège provenance." Accurate but undersells the document's significance.

**Pass:** The Fighter names the distinction: the note is a conclusion, not a placeholder. The author wrote the ending he intended to write. The standard text's extra hundred pages are not this text completed — they are this text replaced.

Sweelinck writes: *Mandeville's Travels — Liège Manuscript — completed earlier text; concludes at the Malabar coast by authorial decision; note at conclusion distinguishes first-hand evidence from reported material; classified as primary text, not draft. Standard text (embellished version) constitutes a separate, later work.*

He closes the ledger.

"Two books, then." He sets the manuscript on the intake shelf. "The reliable one and the famous one." A pause. "The reliable one is shorter."

**TOKEN TAKEN: The Liège Manuscript**

The manuscript rests on the intake shelf classified as a completed primary text. The standard Mandeville is cross-referenced as a separate work. The Dominican representative will arrive in Weimar in two weeks; the archive's intake record will show the document received and classified before his formal objection is lodged.

## Cycle 2 — Supplementary 1367 AD Cycle

**Angle:** *The Sultan's Commission — what Mandeville carried that no pilgrim had ever carried*

**Date processed:** 2026-05-31

**What Cycle 1 missed:** Cycle 1 focused on the structural question of the text: the compilation versus the invention, the draft that stopped at the edge of knowledge. What it did not examine is the specific historical *credential* at the heart of the reliable section — the fact that Mandeville claims to have received from the Sultan of Egypt not a pilgrim's signet (which anyone who served him received) but a full diplomatic commission under the Sultan's great seal, and that he claims to have earned this credential by serving in the Sultan's army against the Bedouins. In 1367, ten years after the book was written, the Alexandrian Crusade of 1365 has collapsed Mamluk-Christian relations to their lowest point in two generations. A book circulating as a pilgrim guide to Egypt contains, buried in Chapter XI, the record of a Christian who voluntarily served the Mamluk Sultan as a soldier and was rewarded with a diplomatic commission that gave him access to the Temple of the Lord itself. This is what Cycle 1 missed: the political content of the one document the book actually describes in detail.

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling (1367 angle: The Sultan's Letters)

Mandeville's description of his time in Egypt contains two extraordinary claims that Cycle 1 did not examine. The first: in Chapter VI, describing the Sultan's court at Babylon (Cairo), Mandeville states "I ought right well to know it; for I dwelled with him as soldier in his wars a great while against the Bedouins. And he would have married me full highly to a great prince's daughter, if I would have forsaken my law and my belief; but I thank God, I had no will to do it." This is not a pilgrim's relation. It is a mercenary's memoir. The author claims to have served in the Mamluk army, to have been offered military preferment including a dynastic marriage, and to have declined on religious grounds while remaining in the Sultan's good graces.

The second claim, in Chapter XI: "I came in there and in other places there I would, for I had letters of the soldan with his great seal, and commonly other men have but his signet. In the which letters he commanded, of his special grace, to all his subjects, to let me see all the places, and to inform me pleinly all the mysteries of every place, and to conduct me from city to city, if it were need, and buxomly to receive me and my company, and for to obey to all my requests reasonable if they were not greatly against the royal power and dignity of the soldan or of his law."

The distinction the text draws is explicit and important: the signet, which ordinary supplicants received — those "that ask him grace, such as have served him" — was borne on a spear before the bearer and received with reverence. But the letters with the great seal were a different order of credential. They were a commission document: comprehensive, addressed to all the Sultan's subjects, binding them to active cooperation rather than mere passage. The locals "kneel down" when the letters are presented, "put them on their heads," and "offer them to do all that the bearer asketh."

No pilgrim received this. The text implies that Mandeville's military service was the basis for this exceptional credential. He served, he refused conversion, and he was given, in return, a document that made him effectively an officer of the Sultan's court throughout the Mamluk domains.

In 1367, this matters in ways that would have been invisible in 1357 when the book was written. Peter I of Cyprus conducted the Alexandrian Crusade in October 1365, sacking the city before withdrawing. The Mamluk response — trade embargo, diplomatic rupture, closure of the Egyptian pilgrim routes — has made any memory of Mamluk-Christian cooperation politically complicated. Mandeville's book is still circulating as a pilgrim guide, but the routes it describes through Egypt are now closed. The letters from the Sultan that gave Mandeville access to the Temple of the Lord would, if authentic, be:

- For merchants and Venetian diplomats: evidence that the Mamluk Sultanate once granted comprehensive diplomatic access to a Christian who served their army — a precedent for negotiation
- For crusade propagandists: an embarrassment, because they prove a Christian voluntarily took service under a Muslim ruler
- For Mamluk agents: an embarrassment, because they prove a Sultan was willing to hand full access to a Christian spy who then wrote everything down
- For the Weimar archive: a primary document of extraordinary rarity — a Mamluk diplomatic commission to a European named in the Arabic transliteration

**Cyprus in 1367:** Famagusta (which Mandeville specifically names as "one of the principal havens of the sea that is in the world, and there arrive Christian men and Saracens and men of all nations") is, in 1367, the most complicated port in the Mediterranean. Following the 1365 crusade, Cyprus under Peter I is diplomatically isolated from the Mamluks. Venetian and Genoese merchants are attempting to preserve their Egyptian trading interests despite the crusade. Famagusta is where all these interests collide. The harbor is simultaneously the last European port before the Mamluk-closed eastern Mediterranean and the place where Mamluk agents, Venetian diplomats, Genoese factors, and crusade recruiters all operate.

A document of the type Mandeville describes — a Mamluk Sultan's commission letters under the great seal, granting a European Christian comprehensive access — would be extraordinarily valuable in Famagusta in 1367, and simultaneously dangerous to anyone who held it openly.

### Pass 2 — Slower (Characters and Document Details)

**The Sultan of Egypt (historical context, 1320s–1340s):** The Sultan Mandeville claims to have served was most likely al-Nasir Muhammad ibn Qalawun (reigned 1310–1341), who was the dominant Mamluk ruler of the period Mandeville claims to have traveled (1322–1355). Al-Nasir Muhammad was known for his sophisticated foreign policy, his use of European visitors as intelligence sources, and his elaborate court ceremonial including the extensive seal and signet system that Mandeville describes. His commission letters would bear his specific honorific titles in Arabic.

**Mandeville's claim (as fiction and as fact):** Whether Mandeville actually served the Sultan or invented the claim for authority is irrelevant to the 1367 stakes. What matters is that the claim, printed in thousands of copies circulating across Europe, identifies the author as someone who earned a specific credential. If any document exists that could be authenticated as the letters described in Chapter XI — a Mamluk diplomatic commission in Arabic under the great seal, issued to a European Christian bearing a name transliterable as "Mandeville" — it would either authenticate the entire Mandeville account or create a forgery market of extraordinary scale.

**The Mamluk intelligence network:** In Chapter XV, the Sultan describes how he maintains knowledge of Christian courts: he sends agents posing as merchants "of precious stones, of cloths of gold and of other things" into Christian lands. He had, at the time of Mandeville's visit, four men who spoke French "right well" and knew Christian countries "as well as they had been of the same country." This detail — the Sultan's French-speaking intelligence network — is, in 1367, a live concern. The same infrastructure described in the 1350s book is presumably still operating, and Mandeville's description of it is essentially a published admission that Christian courts have been penetrated.

**The physical document:** A Mamluk commission letter under the great seal would be a horizontal strip of paper or papyrus (the Mamluk chancery used a distinctive horizontal format), written in chancery Arabic, bearing the Sultan's name in honorific titles at the head and his tughra (seal) at the end. The seal would be large and impressed in red or gold. The letter would name the recipient, describe the commission, and list specific authorities commanded to comply. In 1367, such a document from the 1320s–1340s would be thirty to forty years old: yellowed, fragile, authentic-looking in ways that a forger would have difficulty replicating exactly.

### Pass 3 — Elaborate Scene: Benedetto Cena Opens the Box

It is an afternoon in October 1367, in the back room of Benedetto Cena's factor house in Famagusta. The harbor sounds come through the stone wall: rope-creak, gull-cry, Genoese and Cypriot Greek and Arabic layered together. Benedetto has the cedar box on his counting table. He has had it for three weeks, purchased from an Armenian dealer named Sahag who said it came from a Coptic monastery outside Cairo, dispersed following the 1365 crisis.

The box is old. The cedar has gone grey-yellow. The seal on the clasp is intact — it is not the great seal but a notary's wax seal with Sahag's own mark, authenticating that the contents have not been disturbed since he acquired them. He bought the seal of the contents from the previous owner; he cannot authenticate the contents themselves.

He opens it.

Inside: three sheets of Mamluk-format paper, horizontal strips, each approximately eight inches by thirty-two. The first sheet bears a dense Arabic text in chancery calligraphy; at the top right corner are honorific titles in large letters. At the bottom left, a red seal impression, large as a man's palm. The second sheet is similar but shorter — possibly a covering instruction to a specific official. The third is smaller, appears to be a receipt or register entry.

Benedetto cannot read Arabic. He knows what Mamluk diplomatic paper looks like — he has handled Mamluk trade permits. He knows this is not a trade permit. The format is different. The seal is larger.

He carefully refolds the papers. He closes the box. He does not seal it again. He puts it in the corner behind his spare ledgers. He writes one letter: to his cousin at the Venetian Senate's diplomatic archive, asking whether an expert in Mamluk chancery documents could examine something he has acquired that may be "of historical rather than commercial interest." He does not describe what it is.

He waits. Three days later, his letter has not yet left Famagusta, because the courier he hired has been delayed by a harbor inspection — inspections have become frequent since the October crisis. And Benedetto has received, through an intermediary, an inquiry from a man named Ibrim who would like to discuss a "paperwork matter."

He sends word through a third party that he is not available for unscheduled meetings.

He hires someone trustworthy to carry the box.

*This is where the Fighter enters.*

### Part 3 Detailed Content (BEY-mandeville.part3of3.txt, 270 lines)

**Chapter continuations (Valley of Fiends onward):** The text picks up mid-sentence — Mandeville finishing his account of passing through the Perilous Valley (bodies lying everywhere, apparently whole by fiend-work; Mandeville and his party were cast down many times by winds and thunders but passed safely). Beyond the valley: islands of 28-30 foot giants who eat raw flesh; an island with the "cadeberiz" custom (professional paid deflowerers for wedding nights, justified by ancient fear of serpents in maidens' bodies); an island that weeps at births and rejoices at deaths, burning the dead and any widow who loves her husband; an island whose king is always elected, never a father, with perfectly just laws.

**Chapter XXXII — The Isle of Bragman and Alexander's Letter:** The Bragmans are a people of natural virtue — no thieves, murderers, poor, or beggars; they fast all days; they believe in God of Nature without Christianity. When Alexander came to conquer them, they sent him a letter: *"What may be enough to that man to whom all the world is insufficient? Thou shalt find nothing in us that may cause thee to war against us. For we have no riches, ne none we covet... Our treasure is accord and peace."* Alexander was so struck by this letter that he left them alone and "sent them sureties that they should not be afeard of him." Alexander also encountered the naked philosophers of Gynosophe, who asked why he worked to put all the world under his subjection when he would die anyway; he was confounded. Also: the isle of Pytan, whose people live only by the smell of wild apples and die if the scent leaves them.

**Why Emperor of India is called Prester John:** An early emperor came to a church in Egypt on Whitsun Saturday; seeing priestly orders made, he asked what those men were; when told "priests," he declared he would henceforth be called priest, and take the name of the first priest to leave the church. That man's name was John. "And so ever-more sithens, he is clept Prester John."

**Chapter XXXIII — Terrestrial Paradise and the Pismire Gold:** The isle of Taprobane (Sri Lanka) has two summers and two winters. Beyond: isles of pure gold and silver (Orille and Argyte). In Taprobane: hills of gold guarded by giant pismires (ants as large as hounds) who fill any empty vessel placed near them; folk steal the gold by placing vessels on mares, sending them to graze near the hills while ants fill the vessels, then recalling the mares with their foals — the mares return loaded. The Trees of the Sun and Moon warned Alexander of his death; they lie beyond dark deserts guarded by dragons; unreachable. Terrestrial Paradise: the highest place on earth, touching the moon's circle (the Flood couldn't reach it); a wall of indeterminate material covered with moss; one gate of fire no mortal can enter. From its center: a well feeding the four rivers — Pison (Ganges), Gison (Nile), Tigris, Euphrates. Mandeville says he cannot speak of Paradise properly because he was not there.

**Chapter XXXIV — Rybothe and the Rich Man:** The isle of Rybothe (Tibet): at a great man's funeral, the body is carried to a hill; the prelate decapitates the head, placed on a gold/silver platter for the son; priests smite the body in pieces; vultures and ravens eat the flesh — this is celebrated as "angels of God come to fetch him to Paradise"; the son preserves the skull as a drinking cup and drinks from it all his life in his father's memory. A rich man in another country: served by fifty virgin damsels who sing and cut his meat (he cannot do this himself because his nails are so long they cover his entire hands — the mark of noblesse). Women bind their feet from birth to keep them small — also noblesse. Mandeville concludes his geographical survey: all diverse peoples believe in God of Nature by natural law; idols represent this universal impulse.

**Conclusion — Mandeville's self-description and papal certification:** He returned to Rome; confessed to the Pope of all he had done "in company amongst so many a diverse folk of diverse sect and belief"; showed his book to the Pope; it was examined by the Pope's council against the *Mappa Mundi* and certified true. He is John Mandeville, knight, who departed in 1322 and writes in 1356 "in the thirty-fourth year that I departed from our countries." He suffers from gout and rests. Final Latin blessing.
