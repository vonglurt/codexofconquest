## Cycle 1 — Angle: *The Method as Meaning — What the comparison produces that neither biography contains alone*

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

Plutarch was a Greek from Chaeronea in Boeotia, a priest of Apollo at Delphi, a philosopher in the broad Platonic tradition, and a biographer who invented — or perfected — a form that no one has improved on. He wrote the *Lives* in his middle and late career, probably between about 95 and 115 AD, under the Trajanic and Hadrianic Roman Empire. He was writing in Greek about Greeks and Romans alike, as a member of a Greek culture that had been politically subordinate to Rome for two centuries and culturally superior to it in the same period. This double position — Greek mind inside Roman power — is the condition the *Lives* are written from, and it explains the comparative method.

The method: Plutarch selects a Greek and a Roman who share a defining characteristic — both were lawgivers, or both were great generals, or both were demagogues, or both betrayed their cities — and writes their lives in sequence, then appends a comparison chapter in which he draws out the parallel and typically renders a judgment. The judgment is never simple: Plutarch is not writing to declare winners. He is writing to say: here are two cases of the same human problem in two different cultures. See what each culture did with the same raw material.

The *Lives* are not histories in the sense that Thucydides or Polybius wrote histories — systematic, politically analyzed, sourced with care. Plutarch is explicitly a biographer and moralist; he is interested in character, in the small anecdote that reveals character, in the moment when a man's private nature and his public role come into collision. He is the first to articulate what has become a commonplace: that a man's table conversation reveals more of his character than his major battles. He chooses anecdotes ruthlessly, discarding events that do not illuminate and preserving events that historians might ignore because they seem too small.

**The Greek Lives:** The oldest Greek lives — Theseus, Lycurgus, Solon, Themistocles — deal with the founding era of Greek civilization. Theseus is treated with affectionate skepticism: Plutarch knows the mythological material is unreliable but treats the myths as moral data anyway. Lycurgus, the Spartan lawgiver, is the most didactic life in the collection — Plutarch is genuinely interested in what produces the Spartan system, and his account of it is the most complete ancient description we have. Solon, paired with the Roman Publicola, is the life of the lawmaker as moderate — the man who could have made himself tyrant, chose not to, and then had to leave Athens to let his laws take hold because his presence would have undermined them.

The classical-era lives divide into the brilliant and the catastrophic. Themistocles is brilliant — the man who saw that Greece's future was naval before anyone else did, built the fleet that won Salamis, and then overplayed his position so badly that he ended his life at the Persian court he had saved Greece from. Pericles is the supreme administrator of Athenian democracy: long-sighted, patient, the architect of the Acropolis and the careful strategist who designed the Peloponnesian War strategy and died in the plague that followed. Alcibiades is the catastrophe — beautiful, brilliant, charismatic, loyal to no one, the man whose defection to Sparta hurt Athens more than any Spartan general had. Plutarch's Alcibiades is one of the most psychologically acute portraits in ancient literature: a man whose gifts were indistinguishable from his vices.

Nicias and Demosthenes (the general, not the orator) died together in Sicily after the catastrophic expedition. Their lives are among Plutarch's most tragic — two men of genuine merit destroyed by an enterprise they both opposed and both ended up commanding. The Sicilian Expedition is the pivot of the entire Peloponnesian War and Plutarch treats it as such.

Pelopidas and Epaminondas were the Theban leaders who briefly shattered Spartan military supremacy at Leuctra (371 BC). Epaminondas, oddly, is not a subject of one of the surviving Lives — he is mentioned in other Lives and was apparently paired with Scipio in a Life that is lost. What survives of Plutarch's treatment of Theban supremacy is in Pelopidas. The loss of the Epaminondas Life is the most significant lacuna in the surviving corpus.

Demosthenes, paired with Cicero, is the orator as national conscience — the man who spent decades warning Athens about Philip of Macedon, was occasionally right and often not listened to, and died rather than be captured after Athens's final failure. Plutarch makes the comparison with Cicero explicit: both men were self-made, both were orators first and politicians second, both died badly in political collapses they had spent their careers trying to prevent.

**The Roman Lives:** The Roman lives begin with Romulus, paired with Theseus — the founding myths of the two cities compared, their violence (Romulus killing Remus; Theseus abandoning Ariadne) held up alongside their achievements. Numa Pompilius, the second Roman king, paired with Lycurgus — the lawgiver by consensus against the lawgiver by force.

The middle republic lives — Publicola, Coriolanus, Camillus — track the development of Roman civic virtue through crisis. Coriolanus is the proudest man in the *Lives*, the man whose virtue is indistinguishable from his contempt for everyone who didn't share it, and whose fall comes from refusing to understand that a leader requires a relationship to those led, not just to the idea of being their superior.

Fabius Maximus and Scipio Africanus represent the two poles of Roman strategy: patience and aggression. Fabius earned the title "Delayer" by refusing Hannibal direct battle; it worked but was politically unpopular. Scipio won the war by doing what Fabius wouldn't — crossing to Africa and forcing a decisive engagement. Both are right about different phases of the same war.

Cato the Elder is one of Plutarch's great characters: the reactionary Roman in full, the man who opposed Greek philosophy on principle and wrote his own son's education in Latin, who built Roman moralism into an institutional force, who spent the end of his life on the floor of the Senate demanding that Carthage be destroyed until the day it was. He and Aristides are paired; the comparison is brisk and cuts both ways.

The late republican lives — Marius, Sulla, Lucullus, Pompey, Crassus, Caesar, Cato the Younger, Brutus, Antony — are the most read section of the *Lives* because they cover the most dramatic period of ancient history. Plutarch knew Cicero's speeches and letters, had access to Caesar's *Gallic Wars*, and drew on sources now lost; his portraits of these figures are the primary channel through which the late Republic entered European imagination.

Sulla is Plutarch's most unsettling life — a man of genuine talent who committed atrocities at a scale that made the later dictators seem modest, retired to write his memoirs, and died of natural causes. The comparison with Lysander (the Spartan who won the Peloponnesian War and promptly overreached) is one of the collection's most devastating.

Caesar is not given the largest life — that distinction belongs to Pompey — but it is the pivot of the Roman sequence. Plutarch's Caesar is the most capable man in the *Lives* and the one about whom the moral question is most permanently open: was this conquest, or did the Republic fail on its own and Caesar simply occupy the space?

Cato the Younger, paired with Phocion, is the study in principled intransigence. Both men were right about what was coming and refused to survive into the world they couldn't prevent. Cato's death at Utica — reading the *Phaedo*, going to sleep, waking at some point to check that his sword had been returned, and using it — is one of the most philosophically self-conscious deaths in ancient literature. Plutarch treats it with complete respect.

Brutus and Dion are paired — the tyrannicide as study in motive and consequence. Both killed men they loved; both believed they were serving a larger good; both died without seeing that good achieved.

Antony is the most novelistic of the late Lives — the man who might have been what Caesar was and wasn't, who ended badly in Egypt with the woman who is conspicuously not given a Life of her own.

**The Comparisons:** The comparison chapters, placed after each paired set, are where Plutarch is most himself. They are short — sometimes only a page — and often begin by acknowledging that the comparison is incomplete or unfair. He will say: in courage, Demosthenes and Cicero are equally distinguished, but in different registers; in vanity, Cicero's is more offensive because his was more verbal; in the manner of death, both were untimely, but the manner of Cicero's was less heroic. He awards no trophy; he draws no conclusion the Lives themselves don't support. What the comparison chapter does is force the reader to hold both Lives simultaneously — to see each man as a response to the other.

**What is Lost:** The Epaminondas-Scipio pair is the most significant loss. The lives of Epaminondas that survive in fragments through other sources suggest Plutarch admired him more than almost any other Greek general. What survives is a paragraph in the Pelopidas saying that Epaminondas would have taken more space than he can give him here.

#### Section Notes

- **Greek Founding Lives (Theseus–Solon)**: The mythological material treated as moral data; Plutarch's position is that even invented stories about great men reveal the culture's values.
- **Classical Greek Lives (Themistocles–Demosthenes)**: The rise and fall of Athenian democracy, told through characters. Each life implicitly comments on the others.
- **Theban Lives (Pelopidas)**: The brief period of Theban supremacy; the loss of the Epaminondas Life is a wound in the corpus.
- **Roman Founding and Republic Lives (Romulus–Fabius)**: The development of Roman civic virtue; Coriolanus as the cautionary case study.
- **Late Roman Republic (Marius–Antony)**: The most-read section; the destruction of the Republic as a sequence of character studies.
- **The Comparisons**: The places where Plutarch's method becomes most visible; where the form performs its argument.

### Pass 2 — Characters

**Plutarch himself** — The most important character in the *Lives* is their author. He appears in the prefaces and dedicatory letters; he makes choices on every page; his presence is everywhere in the anecdote selection, the moral framing, the moments when he pauses to note that he cannot verify something but is including it because it reveals character. He is a Greek under Roman rule who is not angry about it — who has made peace with the political situation and found in biography a method for preserving what the Greeks produced that Rome could not produce and Rome produced that Greece could not. His equanimity is not complacency; it is a philosophical achievement.

**Alcibiades** — The most compelling individual in the Greek Lives. Plutarch describes his beauty (extraordinary), his voice (capable of mimicking any accent, which he used for theatrical effect), his dog (he cut off the tail of a beautiful and expensive dog to give the Athenians something to talk about instead of his actual behavior). He is the man who made Athens win battles it should have lost and lose the war it could have won. Plutarch's moral judgment on him is almost affectionate: this is what happens when gifts have no center.

**Coriolanus** — The study in virtue-as-contempt. A Roman Alcibiades, almost — also brilliant, also self-defeating — but where Alcibiades had no loyalty, Coriolanus had excessive loyalty to an idea. He could not believe that good soldiers should also manage bad politicians. He was right about the politicians and wrong about the consequence of being right that way.

**Cato the Elder** — The Roman reactionary as force of nature. Plutarch treats him with half-exasperated respect: this man was right about the corruption of Roman morals and wrong about how to prevent it, and his solution (suppress it through disapproval and public shaming) did not work and could not work, but was internally consistent from first to last.

**Caesar** — The pivot. The man who was too good at everything to be stopped until it was too late. Plutarch notes that many of Caesar's critics were also envious of him, which complicates the moral picture without resolving it.

**Cato the Younger** — The study in integrity without accommodation. He read the *Phaedo* twice the night before he died. He was the most principled man in Roman public life and the most politically ineffective, which Plutarch finds tragic rather than ironic.

**The Method Itself** — Plutarch's comparative method is a character, in a sense. It appears in the prefaces, in the transitions between Lives, in the comparison chapters. It argues: no one is fully understood alone; every life is commentary on another life; the meaning emerges in the gap between the two.

### Pass 3 — The Pivotal Scene: The Comparison of Alexander and Caesar

Plutarch's comparison chapter for Alexander and Caesar is the most-discussed in the collection, partly because the Lives themselves are the most famous and partly because the comparison is structurally the most interesting: it compares two men who are in some sense genuinely parallel — both conquerors, both political revolutionaries, both died young or were killed before their projects were complete — and then refuses to rank them.

The comparison says, approximately: in courage, both were equal and beyond ordinary measure. In fortune, Alexander had more of it — the Persian Empire was already in decay when he attacked, while Caesar had to build everything. In the manner of victory, Alexander's were more spectacular but less durable; Caesar's less spectacular but better organized. In treatment of defeated enemies, Caesar was more clement — this is a real point and Plutarch makes it directly; Alexander killed Philotas, killed Cleitus, destroyed Thebes; Caesar, generally, spared. In the matter of drink, Alexander was worse — this is also a real point; his most serious failures of judgment occurred when drunk. In literary gifts, both were superior, but Caesar wrote better prose.

What the comparison chapter doesn't do is declare a verdict. It ends by noting that both men were still in process — neither had fully become what they were becoming — when their lives were cut short. The comparison is therefore permanently open, because neither man reached the full arc Plutarch would have needed to judge completely.

The deepest thing the comparison says is structural: Alexander and Caesar are not being compared to each other. They are both being compared to an ideal of greatness that neither achieved and both approached from different angles. The comparison is not "which was better" but "what does greatness look like when it approaches from the Greek side versus the Roman side, and what does the gap between those two approaches reveal about the ideal itself?"

The reader who reads both Lives and both comparisons ends up knowing something that neither Alexander nor Caesar knew: what it looked like from the outside to be them, placed next to each other, by someone who admired and judged both.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **"The Third Comparison"** — A Byzantine scholar of the 11th century, copying the Alexander/Caesar pair in Thessaloniki, added a long marginal annotation comparing both men to Basil II — the Byzantine Emperor who spent forty years methodically destroying his enemies and died in his sleep after his greatest victory. The marginalia is theologically and politically dangerous: it implies the Byzantine emperors are inheritors of Alexander rather than Augustus. The manuscript survives in a Thessaloniki monastery under threat from Ottoman pressure in 1367. Fighter escorts it.

2. **"Alcibiades's Letter to Tissaphernes"** — Alcibiades, in Persian exile, wrote to Tissaphernes outlining the case for Persian non-intervention in the Peloponnesian War (keeping both sides exhausted). The letter is in the Persian diplomatic archive tradition; a 3rd-century BC copy on papyrus survives in an Egyptian monastery. The letter is the most explicit surviving statement of Alcibiades's strategic thinking outside Thucydides's paraphrase. Fighter escorts it.

3. **"The Solon Tablet"** — Solon had his laws written on wooden tablets (kyrbeis) and set up in the Athenian Agora. The tablets rotted. A marble copy was made, also lost. A late Roman copy of a Hellenistic transcription of several laws survived in a Spartan archive. A Venetian collector has acquired it and is sailing for Venice. Fighter escorts an authenticated rubbing to a neutral archive before the stone is lost at sea.

4. **"Tiro's Notes"** — Cicero's secretary Marcus Tullius Tiro invented the first systematic shorthand notation (Tironian notes) to record Cicero's speeches as they were delivered. A wax notebook of Tiro's shorthand drafts for the second Philippic was preserved in a monastery near Arpinum (Cicero's hometown). The shorthand is not fully legible — a scholar who can read it would find variant passages from the published speech. Fighter escorts it from Arpinum to a neutral archive.

5. **"The Nicias Correspondence"** — Nicias wrote dispatches to Athens during the Sicilian Expedition that survive in excerpt in Thucydides, but Plutarch quotes material that is not in Thucydides, suggesting he had access to fuller copies. A complete Nician dispatch, covering the critical summer of 413 BC, survives in a Byzantine monastery as a text in a volume otherwise containing geographic descriptions. Fighter escorts the volume.

6. **"The Agrarian Commission Record"** — Tiberius Gracchus's land commission (133 BC) produced land-redistribution records: who received what allotment, where, and under what legal description. A fragmentary copy of one year's commission decisions survives in a Roman archive. In 1367, the land it describes is in Campania and is still arable; the document has legal implications for current landowners who trace their ownership to Sullan confiscations. Fighter escorts it before it can be confiscated or destroyed.

7. **"The Missing Preface"** — Plutarch's preface to the Epaminondas/Scipio pair is quoted in fragments by later writers but the Lives themselves are lost. A monastic scholar in 1367 has found a manuscript that contains what appears to be the preface — Plutarch's stated reason for pairing these two men — without the Lives following it. The preface alone tells you what Plutarch thought the comparison was about. Fighter escorts the fragment from a Greek island monastery to Weimar.

**MLA theme chosen**: *The comparison produces what neither biography contains alone; meaning lives in the gap between two lives, not in either life separately; the archivist who receives both manuscripts is the only one who can see the whole.*

This is distinct from all above. The nearest neighbor is "posthumous truth" (LGW — a dead woman's words carried through silence) and "authorship as claim" (DQX — the manuscript belongs to its author). Neither covers: the formal method of comparison as the text's argument; the idea that two documents shelved together produce a third document that neither alone contains; the archive as the place where comparison becomes possible at all.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Third Comparison*

### The Token

*The Basil Margin — a Byzantine manuscript containing Plutarch's Lives of Alexander and Caesar, 11th century, Thessaloniki scriptorium. The leather binding is worn at the corners. In the margins of the comparison chapter, a different hand has written a dense annotation in smaller script, arguing that Basil II of Byzantium is more precisely parallel to Alexander than Caesar is, on fifteen specific grounds. The annotation is unsigned and covers six pages of margin. Someone knew what they were writing and knew not to put their name to it.*

---

**ACT I**

*The Basil Margin — on a reading stand in a monastery library — at the Monastery of the Latomos, Thessaloniki, morning.*

The monastery is in the upper city. From the library window, the harbor is visible and, beyond the harbor, the sea. The Ottoman expansion has been pressing the coast for a generation; the monastery has been sending manuscripts west for fifteen years, quietly, to monasteries in Venetian-held territory. The head librarian, a monk named Father Simeon, has been choosing which manuscripts to move and in which order. He has left the Basil Margin manuscript until now because it is problematic.

He explains the problem to the Fighter: the marginalia argues that the Byzantine imperial line descends more properly from Alexander than from the Roman emperors — that the Greek dimension of the imperial tradition is the legitimate core and the Roman dimension is an overlay. This argument, in 1367, is not heretical in the strict theological sense. But in the political sense, it would be read as a challenge to every claim the Latin West has made about the inheritance of Rome. The wrong people reading it could cause real trouble; the right people reading it, in the right place, would find it valuable.

Simeon wants it in Weimar. He does not want it in Venice or Rome.

The monastery's gates are guarded — not against the Ottomans, who are not yet at the walls, but against a Serbian noble's administrator who has been conducting "inventories" of monastic property in the city. The administrator passed through two days ago and will return in one week for a second review. The Fighter must leave with the manuscript before the second review registers it.

*— The administrator is not looking for this book specifically. He is looking for anything valuable. The manuscript in his hands becomes a political document; in the archive, it becomes a scholarly one.*

> **Skill Check: Stealth DC 12.** Exit the monastery through the garden gate, which opens onto a residential lane rather than the main road, while Simeon creates a distraction in the library to explain any noise. The administrator's men do casual watch on the main entrance.

**Fail:** A guard at the lane gate is awake and notices the Fighter. He does not stop them — he is not sure enough to challenge — but he reports to the administrator's office. A rider will be sent to the harbor to check passenger manifests. The Fighter must board a westbound ship within three hours.

**Pass:** The lane is empty. The garden gate opens with a key Simeon has cut specially. The Fighter is in the residential lane before the morning prayer bell rings inside.

**TOKEN GRANTED: The Basil Margin** — worn leather binding, pages dense with 11th-century Greek, the marginal annotation in a second, smaller hand running through the comparison chapter like a parallel argument in the book's own skin.

---

**ACT II**

*The Basil Margin — in the pack — at Thessaloniki harbor, two hours later.*

A Venetian merchant named Alvise Morosini has been acquiring manuscripts from the Thessaloniki monasteries for six months. He has agents at the harbor watching for known book-carriers. He is not hostile; he considers manuscript acquisition a service to civilization. He has a house in Venice with a proper library and a trained copyist. He makes a generous offer on the spot.

His problem is not bad intent. His problem is that the marginalia in the Basil Margin is the kind of argument that, published from a Venetian house, gets immediately classified as a political document rather than a scholarly one — the papal curia has people in Venice who read everything that comes through Morosini's library. A neutral archive handles it as a scholarly object; the Venetian book trade handles it as a political commodity.

*— He genuinely believes the manuscript would be better preserved in Venice. He is wrong about what "better preserved" means for this particular text.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 12.** Explain to Morosini that the marginalia's value depends on it being received in a context that can read it as scholarship rather than politics — and that Weimar has the classification system for this, where Venice would require its content to be managed for political implication before it could be studied.

**Fail:** Morosini does not accept this argument and files a formal letter of acquisition claim with the harbor master — not theft, just a legal paper trail that complicates the Fighter's departure. The Fighter must sail on an earlier ship than planned, before the harbor master processes the letter.

**Pass:** Morosini thinks about the Venetian curia. He knows exactly who Plutarch's Lives reads as, in Rome. He closes his wallet. "Tell the archivist I want to see the transcription when it's available." He steps back. The morning ship for the Adriatic is boarding.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT III**

*The Basil Margin — in the pack — on the Adriatic road north of Brindisi, three days west.*

The road from the Adriatic coast north through Ancona and into the foothills is a pilgrim and merchant route. A group of Serbian merchants traveling in the opposite direction — south toward Brindisi for the return crossing — recognizes the Fighter from Thessaloniki. They recognized a scholar's messenger leaving the Latomos monastery in the early morning three days before they themselves departed. They are not agents of the administrator; they are simply men who travel this road and have noticed that book-carriers from Thessaloniki tend to carry things worth having. There are four of them.

They do not negotiate.

*— They're not trying to steal the book specifically. They're trying to find out whether what they've been following for three days is worth the effort. The fastest way to make them stop is to make the effort seem larger than the reward.*

> **Combat: 4 Serbian Merchants turned opportunists** — AC 11, HP 14 each. Armed with knives and one short sword among them. Travelers, not soldiers; they fight to threshold and look for an exit when it costs them.

After the combat, the road ahead runs through a small market town where the local magistrate's officer keeps a gate record. The Fighter's pack will be noted if the officer is doing his job.

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 10.** Present the manuscript at the gate as a scholar's package being delivered under scholarly commission. The officer is not rigorous but needs a plausible description.

**Pass or Fail:** The gate is cleared either way — a fail costs a delay and a small payment for the officer's notation process.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT IV**

*The Basil Margin — in the pack — at the house of Father Benedetto, a Dominican scholar, Ancona.*

Father Benedetto has been studying Plutarch for twenty years. He knows the Lives in their Latin translation, has access to three partial Greek manuscripts, and has been told by a contact in Thessaloniki that a copy of the Alexander/Caesar Lives with unusual marginalia is in transit. He has sent a letter to Weimar asking for the archive to delay the document's intake until he has had a chance to read it. He is waiting in Ancona because Ancona is on the road.

He is not trying to stop the document reaching Weimar. He wants three days with it before it is sealed in an intake box. He says this directly and honestly.

His problem: the marginalia was written by a Byzantine scholar commenting on Byzantine imperial legitimacy. A Dominican friar in Ancona reading it will read it through a specific theological and political lens that will produce a specific kind of annotation in his own notebooks, which will circulate in Dominican circles, which will reach the curia before the document reaches Weimar. The marginalia will be politically classified before it is scholastically received. The sequence matters.

*— He is not wrong that the document is important. He is wrong that his reading it first is neutral.*

> **Skill Check: Insight DC 13.** Understand that Benedetto's request is genuinely academic but structurally political — not because he intends it to be, but because the institution he moves through will make it so; and that the correct response is not to refuse him but to offer him the right sequence: Weimar receives and classifies first, then Dominican scholarship has full access.

**Fail:** Benedetto makes a formal request for a one-night loan of the manuscript on the authority of his Order's scholarly commission. The Fighter must leave Ancona without spending the night, before his letter to Weimar's intake officer can catch up.

**Pass:** The Fighter names the sequence problem: reception first, scholarship second. Benedetto sits with this. He is a scholar first and a Dominican second, and the framing — that he would get better access through proper intake than through a preliminary reading that contaminates the reception — appeals to the scholar. He withdraws the request and writes a second letter to Weimar, this one requesting a formal consultation after intake. The Fighter leaves with the manuscript and the goodwill.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT V**

*The Basil Margin — in the pack — at the Weimar archive intake desk, Archivus Sweelinck, afternoon.*

The Archivus looks at the manuscript. He opens it at the comparison chapter with the care of a man who has opened a great many manuscripts and knows which ones are delicate. He reads the marginal annotation without speaking, moving through it slowly. When he finishes, he closes the manuscript and sits with his hands flat on his knees.

"The marginalia argues that Basil II is more precisely parallel to Alexander than Caesar is." He pauses. "This is either a theological provocation, a historical argument, or both simultaneously." He opens the intake form. "The manuscript is Byzantine Greek with an 11th-century hand for the main text and a secondary hand that may be contemporary or may be later. The annotation is scholarly in form and politically combustible in content."

He looks up.

"I need to know which category governs. Not both — that would be a holding entry, which means it sits in the provisional queue for six months while someone decides." He meets the Fighter's eyes. "The text of the Lives is a historical document. The marginalia is a scholarly argument appended to a historical document. Does the marginalia change the category of the document it's written in, or does the document's category govern the marginalia?"

*— He knows the answer. He is asking because the intake record requires the decision to be made in the presence of the person who carried it, as a form of chain of custody for the decision itself.*

> **Skill Check: History DC 12.** Recall that Plutarch's own comparison chapters are appended to the Lives in exactly this way — secondary text commenting on primary text — and are treated as part of the same document, categorized with it. The marginalia is a third comparison appended to Plutarch's second comparison. The governing category is the document's outermost layer: historical record with appended scholarly argument, both received together.

**Fail:** The Archivus files it provisionally under Comparative History — Byzantine, with a note that the marginalia requires separate assessment. It will be correctly classified in three months.

**Pass:** The Fighter says: the marginalia is a third comparison appended to Plutarch's second comparison. The document's classification is whatever Plutarch's comparison chapters are classified as — and those have never been filed separately from the Lives. The governing layer is the outermost one. All three comparisons go in together.

Sweelinck is still for a moment. Then he writes, carefully: *Comparative Biography — Plutarch (Lives of Alexander and Caesar) with appended comparisons, including annotation. Complete set: original comparison by Plutarch, secondary marginalia by unknown Byzantine scholar, received together as one document.*

He closes the ledger.

"Three comparisons." He looks at the manuscript on the intake shelf. "And whoever wrote the marginalia didn't know they were writing the third."

**TOKEN TAKEN: The Basil Margin**

The manuscript rests on the intake shelf of the Weimar archive, classified as a complete comparative document in three layers. Benedetto's second letter, requesting formal consultation after intake, will arrive in six days.

## Cycle 2 — Angle: *The Missing Preface — The Argument That Outlived Its Evidence*

**What this angle reveals that cycle 1 missed**: Cycle 1 showed two complete Lives being shelved together, producing a third text in the gap between them — the archive as the machine that makes comparison possible. Cycle 2 shows the inverse: a preface without its Lives. Plutarch's stated reason for pairing Epaminondas and Scipio — his argument about what their comparison reveals — survives while the Lives themselves are gone. A preface to absent texts is not an incomplete document; it is the method statement in its purest form, unqualified by the evidence it was written to introduce. What Plutarch thought the comparison was about is clearer now than it would have been if the Lives had survived, because the surviving text cannot be argued against.

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Missing Preface*

#### The Fragment

*The Epaminondas Preface Fragment — a vellum folio, rolled and sealed with monastery wax — Plutarch's stated argument for pairing Epaminondas and Scipio: what the comparison was designed to reveal, what quality in each man made them parallel, what the gap between their two approaches would show about the idea of great generalship. The Lives that were supposed to follow it are not here. They have not been here since at least the ninth century. The preface ends with a sentence about what the reader will find — and the reader will not find it. Held for four years in the Naxos ducal monastery library, in a theological collection where it was not being looked for.*

---

**ACT I**

*The Epaminondas Preface Fragment — on a reading stand in the monastery library — Naxos ducal monastery, morning.*

Brother Grigorios found it four years ago. He was cataloguing a late antique cosmography collection and found the preface bound loose at the back of a volume it had nothing to do with — same-era parchment, same scriptorium hand, clearly displaced. He read it twice and understood what it was: the preface to the two lost Lives. He has been deciding what to do with it since.

His hesitation is not cowardice. He is asking: what is a preface without its text? Is it a fragment or a self-sufficient document? He believes it is self-sufficient. He is waiting for someone who will understand why.

*— He wants to know that you see what it is before he releases it. Not its historical value. What it means that the argument survives without the evidence.*

> **Dialogue DC 12.** Tell Brother Grigorios what it means that the preface outlived the Lives — why the absence of the supporting text is not a loss but a clarification; why the argument is more visible now than it would have been if Plutarch had finished.

**Fail:** Grigorios sets his hands flat on the rolled folio. "You see a fragment. Come back when you can tell me why it is complete." He is not unkind. He has been waiting four years; he can wait another day.

**Pass:** He lifts the folio from the stand and sets it on the table between you. "The Lives would have qualified it. Now nothing qualifies it. He said exactly what he thought, and we are the only readers left." He applies the monastery wax seal to the exterior with a notation: *single-folio preface, Plutarch collection, verified by Grigorios, monk, Naxos ducal monastery.* He slides it across.

**TOKEN GRANTED: Epaminondas Preface Fragment** — vellum folio, rolled, monastery wax seal with Grigorios's verification notation; Plutarch's stated argument for pairing Epaminondas and Scipio.

---

**ACT II**

*The Epaminondas Preface Fragment — in the Fighter's satchel — Naxos harbor embarkation dock, midday.*

Ser Luca is the Venetian ducal factor for Naxos. He is the kind of man who knows the value of manuscripts without being able to read them, which is the most dangerous kind. He has been watching departures from the island's monastery network for two years. He intercepts the Fighter at the embarkation point with a light hand on the arm and a pleasant expression.

"Cultural export review. The duchy has an interest in materials from Naxos's historical collections." He gestures at the satchel with the ease of a man who has done this before and knows when someone will comply. He is not threatening. He is a gate.

*— He has two categories ready: commercial manuscript cargo (requires hold and inspection) and personal religious documents in clerical transit (requires only a notation). He will file it under whichever category you give him.*

> **Dialogue DC 12.** Frame the document correctly — it is Plutarch, not Scripture, but it belongs to a monk's personal scholarly collection in transit, not to a commercial cargo. The factor knows the difference. He will log it as you describe it if your description is coherent.

**Fail:** Ser Luca logs it as "library manuscript, commercial cargo, hold for inspection pending ducal review." Three days. The Fighter waits at the harbor inn while the review is requested and does not arrive.

**Pass:** Ser Luca considers, writes his notation: *personal scholarly documents, clerical carrier transit, religious-cultural category.* He attaches his transit slip to the rolled folio's exterior. "You'll need that slip at Ragusa if anyone asks." He steps back. The ship is boarding.

**TOKEN STATE 2**: Venetian ducal transit slip attached to exterior — *personal scholarly documents, clerical carrier transit, religious-cultural category* — freeing the document from commercial inspection authority.

---

**ACT III**

*The Epaminondas Preface Fragment — rolled folio with monastery seal and transit slip — Ragusa waystation inn, evening.*

Friar Benedetto is a Dominican scholar doing advance work for Venetian cultural acquisitions. He has been at the Ragusa waystation for a week, watching arrivals from the Greek island routes. He sees the transit slip. He reads it. He correctly identifies the document's origin: Naxos monastery collection, one folio, clerical transit.

He sits across from the Fighter at the inn table with the expression of a man who has been looking for exactly this.

"A preface without its text is an incomplete document. Technically, under the archive classification protocols, it belongs in a pending-authentication file until the associated primary texts can be confirmed as either present elsewhere or definitively lost." He opens his hands. "I am not proposing to take it. I am proposing that you allow me to log it as pending, which keeps it accessible for scholarly review."

*He is not lying. He is right about the classification protocol. He is wrong about what the document is.*

> **Skill Check: History DC 13.** Establish that Plutarch's preface to the lost Lives is not an incomplete document — it is a primary methodological text; the Lives were illustrations of an argument, not the argument itself; what survives is the argument, which is self-sufficient; a preface to absent Lives is not a secondary text pending its primary work, it is the primary work.

**Fail:** Friar Benedetto applies his pending-authentication notation to the transit slip. The document is not taken, but the notation creates a complication at every future archive intake. The Fighter argues again at dawn with the fuller citation; Benedetto concedes by the second bell.

**Pass:** Friar Benedetto is quiet for a moment. He is a genuine scholar. He recognizes when he has been outargued correctly. "I will note it as authenticated self-sufficient primary document." He adds his endorsement below Ser Luca's transit notation: *primary methodological text — method statement, self-sufficient, no primary text required; Plutarch's Lives, Epaminondas/Scipio pair; authenticated Friar Benedetto OSP, Ragusa.*

**TOKEN STATE 3**: Dominican scholar's authentication endorsement added — *primary methodological text, self-sufficient* — establishing the document's independent status under archive classification.

---

**ACT IV**

*The Epaminondas Preface Fragment — rolled folio with three notations — road north from Ragusa toward the overland route, night.*

Ser Luca reconsidered his transit slip classification. He sent two men north on fast horses two hours after the ship arrived. They have been waiting on the road.

*— They do not know what the document says. They know what it's worth.*

> **Combat: 2 opponents — AC 12, HP 22 each.** They want the satchel. They will break off if reduced below half health.

**Fail:** They get the satchel. The Fighter pursues. Ragusa's civic guard will record the theft if the Fighter reports it — a formal complaint against Venetian agents in a Venetian-dependent city is a delicate claim, but the guard will take the report.

**Pass:** Both down or broken. The road north is clear. The folio is intact.

**TOKEN STATE 4**: Carried through Venetian agents' ambush. Three notations undamaged.

---

**ACT V**

*The Epaminondas Preface Fragment — rolled folio, three notations — Weimar archive, the desk of Archivus Sweelinck, morning.*

Sweelinck reads the folio carefully. He knows the Lives. He knows the Epaminondas/Scipio pair is lost. He reads Plutarch's stated argument — what the comparison was designed to reveal — and sets the folio down with the expression of a man who has just understood something he has been about to understand for years.

"The preface tells you what Plutarch thought the two men had in common." He looks up. "The Lives were going to demonstrate it. Without them, I can only take his word for it." A pause. "That is the most honest thing I have read in this archive in twenty years." He picks up his pen. "But I have no category for a preface without a text."

*— He has the right problem. He needs to know what the category is.*

> **Skill Check: History DC 11.** Tell Sweelinck the category: this is not a secondary document pending a primary text; it is a primary methodological document — Plutarch's explicit statement of his comparative method, applied to a specific comparison. The Lives were the evidence. The preface is the argument. File under: Primary Methodological Documents — Comparative Method Statement; the Lives, if found, would be filed as supporting material.

**Fail:** Sweelinck files it under "Lost Works Fragments — Plutarch" and marks it pending. He consults his senior cataloguers. The correct framing arrives in an hour; he reopens the intake.

**Pass:** Sweelinck writes a new category header at the bottom of the methodological documents folio: *Primary Methodological Documents — Comparative Method Statement; document type: argument standing independent of its illustrations.* He files the folio. He looks at it in the box.

"A preface to two men who were never written down." He closes the box. "The gap in the middle is not a loss. It's the proof."

**TOKEN TAKEN: Epaminondas Preface Fragment**

The folio enters the Weimar archive under a new category. The monastery seal, the Venetian transit slip, and the Dominican authentication endorsement are indexed as chain-of-custody documentation. The argument Plutarch wrote is the only surviving statement of what he thought Epaminondas and Scipio shared.

## MLA-04 — Tiro's Notes (Life of Cicero)
**Cycle 3**

*(Theme differentiation: Avoiding MLA-01's "comparison produces what neither biography contains alone," MLA-02's "preface that outlived its evidence." Avoiding FCO-14's "alternate ending that survived in a margin." New theme: the shorthand draft is faster than deliberation and therefore more honest — the wax catches the moment before revision; the variant passages are not rejected alternatives but earlier selves of the argument; the archive receives the draft as primary evidence of what the orator thought before he decided what to say.)*

---

*Tiro's Wax Shorthand Notebook · a compact diptych of beeswax tablets, dense Tironian notes across eight pages, three large passages marked with a different stylus indicating revision sessions; a 10th-century monastery hand in the margin: "variants recorded here, text diverges from known published version in three places" · in Abbot Anselmo's document chest · Benedictine monastery near Arpinum, Campania*

---

### Act I — Before the Florentines Arrive

A delegation from the Platonic Academy in Florence sent a courier yesterday. Messer Giacomo Salviati wants the notebook for a commentary project on Cicero's late rhetoric. He has not arrived yet. The abbot has his letter.

Abbot Anselmo is not obligated to Florence. He is uncertain. The document has been in this monastery's care for four hundred years; releasing it to anyone is a decision he will be explaining for the next four hundred. The Florentines have a claim of scholarly urgency. The archive has a claim of permanent preservation.

The argument available: a commentary project uses the document. It marks it, copies it, cites it, and thereby changes what the document is by anchoring it in its first scholarly use. A neutral archive preserves it unchanged for all future commentary projects, including any the Florentines might eventually write. The first use should not define the document; the archive is precisely the institution that holds it before any use defines it.

*The abbot looked at Salviati's letter for a long time.* Then he folded it and put it in a different pile — the pile of things he had already decided about.

He wrote the Release Authorization with the monastery seal and the date, and he added a line the Fighter had not asked for: "released for permanent preservation, not for commentary or annotation."

You receive the Monastic Release Authorization. You receive Tiro's Wax Shorthand Notebook.

### Act II — Messer Falcone

*Tiro's Wax Shorthand Notebook · in the Fighter's satchel with the Release Authorization · at the Naples harbor freight office*

He is not threatening. He is commercial.

The Fighter refused.

Falcone nodded as if this was the expected answer. He wrote a Non-Copy Statement on the back of his own business card — standard commercial language, his seal pressed into the wax — and slid it across the desk. *Professional courtesy,* he said. *It proves the document was not touched here.*

### Act III — Maestro Uccelli

*Tiro's Wax Shorthand Notebook · in the satchel with two documents · in the corridor outside Maestro Uccelli's classroom, Bologna studium*

*The courier was waiting for a response.*

### Act V — What the Draft Is

*Tiro's Wax Shorthand Notebook · still sealed in the satchel, all four documents present · at the Archivus Sweelinck's intake desk, Weimar*

The Archivus opened the notebook carefully and read through the Tironian notes with a stylus he did not use, tracing the column without touching the wax. He read the three revision passages. He read the 10th-century marginal identification.

He said: *This is a draft. Draft materials are preparatory. Preparatory materials are classified as secondary to the finished work.*

He was reading the classification protocol, not making an argument.

The finished work is the published Philippic, which has been in circulation for fourteen hundred years. Every scholar who needs to understand the Philippic has access to the published text. No scholar has access to what Cicero thought before he revised. The revision marks are data — each marked passage is evidence that the earlier version existed and was rejected, which means the earlier version is the primary record of what was considered. The published speech is the end of a process. The draft is the process itself.

*The Archivus set down the stylus.*

A draft that predates revision is not secondary to the finished work. It is primary evidence of the thought that the finished work was designed to conceal. The archive should classify it accordingly — not as preparation, but as testimony.

He wrote slowly at the top of a new intake sheet, in the hand of a man who is creating a category rather than filling one: *Primary Drafts — Pre-Revision Documents: materials recording thinking prior to its public articulation. First entry: Tiro's shorthand notes for the second Philippic.*

He looked at the three revision marks in the wax. *He was a better editor than he wanted people to know.*

You receive the Primary Draft Classification Record.

## MLA-06 — The Agrarian Commission Record (Life of Tiberius Gracchus)
**Cycle 5**

*(Theme differentiation: Avoiding MLA cycles 1-4. Avoiding CID cycle 2's provisional obligation, BRU cycles' amendment before seal, LIL's mercy before order. New theme: the historical document that is still a live legal instrument — the commission records show allotments predating Sullan confiscations; carrying it to a neutral archive is the only act that neutralizes the legal threat; the archive that receives it classifies it as history and thereby suspends its evidentiary function; the landowners know this and will stop the carrier before it can be filed.)*

---

*The Tiberian Allotment Record · a wax tablet set, three tablets hinged with bronze rings stamped with the commission's mark; official format listing forty-seven Campanian allotments with grantee names and measurements; a second-century hand at the bottom of the third tablet lists the same land under Sullan grant titles, annotated "comparison: prior grants documented here, superseded under Lex Cornelia 81 BC" · in the restricted section of the Roman civic archive, Ser Bartolomeo's custody*

---

### Act I — The Injunction

Avvocato Mastino filed the preemptive injunction three weeks ago. It prohibits transfer of the tablets on the grounds that they are material evidence in a pending proceeding. The proceeding has not been filed. The injunction is against the transfer, not against a named defendant.

Ser Bartolomeo, the archivist, can release the tablets to a neutral non-Italian archive under the provisions that govern historical documents. He is uncertain whether the injunction's pending-proceeding language converts the tablets from historical to active-evidence status before a case is actually filed.

The argument available: the injunction is procedurally premature. A pending proceeding requires a filed case with a named defendant. The landowners have filed an injunction against the transfer — which is not the same as filing a case. No case exists. The injunction's legal basis is circular: it prevents the transfer that would make the document historical, to prevent the document from being classified as historical, to preserve its evidentiary function in a proceeding that has not been filed.

*Ser Bartolomeo read the injunction again.*

He signed the Transfer Authorization. Then he added a note: "Released as historical document per archival protocol, destination non-Italian neutral archive; any subsequent legal claim must be filed in the archive's jurisdiction, not ours."

You receive the Archive Transfer Authorization. You receive the Tiberian Allotment Record.

---

### Act II — The Gate

*The Tiberian Allotment Record · in the Fighter's satchel with the Transfer Authorization · at the south gate of Rome, midday*

Avvocato Mastino is standing on the road outside the gate with a writ and two men. He is professionally dressed. He says, without raising his voice: *That document cannot leave Roman jurisdiction while a proceeding is pending.*

The Fighter said: no proceeding is pending. An injunction against transfer is not a proceeding. A proceeding requires a case. A case requires a defendant. A defendant requires a named party. No name has been filed. The injunction is a claim against a document, not a legal action against a person; it has no jurisdiction over a carrier.

*Mastino looked at the Transfer Authorization.*

He could not argue with the logic on the road. He could argue with it in court, later, and he would. But the argument required a venue, and the venue was not this gate.

*He stepped aside.*

You receive the Jurisdictional Rebuttal Statement.

---

### Act III — The Second Injunction

*The Tiberian Allotment Record · in the satchel with two documents · on the road north, Tuscan foothills*

Two riders caught up in the afternoon. They carried a Campanian court order — a second injunction, different jurisdiction, same purpose: hold the carrier three days pending review.

The problem: the Fighter is in Tuscan territory. Campanian jurisdiction does not extend here. The riders know this. They are relying on the Fighter not knowing it.

The argument must be stated clearly and in terms the riders can report back: the Campanian court's jurisdiction ends at the border. The border is behind them. The fighters are now in Tuscan territory. If they want to delay the carrier, they need a Tuscan court order, not a Campanian one. They are welcome to ride back and get one. The carrier will not be here when they return.

*The riders looked at each other.*

One of them pulled out a map, verified the border location, and made a sound that was not quite an acknowledgment. They wrote a formal acknowledgment that the Campanian jurisdiction claim had been made and declined on territorial grounds, and rode back south.

You receive the Campanian Jurisdictional Acknowledgment.

---

### Act IV — Maestro Ricci

*The Tiberian Allotment Record · in the satchel with three documents · in Maestro Ricci's study, Bologna studium*

Maestro Giacomo Ricci is one of the five best legal scholars in Italy. He has been retained by the Campanian landowner consortium to write a legal opinion on whether Italian courts can issue a recall order for a document held in a German archive. The opinion is thorough. It concludes that the jurisdictional question is unsettled and that the archive's neutrality is contestable. He offers it to the Fighter as scholarly information.

The opinion is designed to delay. Not by its conclusions — which are careful, not certain — but by its existence: once the opinion is filed with the Italian courts, a review process begins that lasts three to four months. A document in transit is subject to recall during review.

*Maestro Ricci is a professional. His professionalism is funded.*

The question is not whether his opinion is legally correct — it might be. The question is whether it represents an independent scholarly conclusion or a paid position. The law professor who is retained by one side of a dispute to write an opinion on that dispute is not providing scholarship; he is providing advocacy in scholarly form.

*The Fighter noted in writing who retained Maestro Ricci, and when, and what the fee was, which was available as public record from the studium's contract register.*

The note does not dispute the legal opinion. It documents its genesis. A funded opinion is admissible evidence; it is simply weaker than an independent one. The Weimar archive would note both.

You receive the Funding Source Notation.

---

### Act V — The Archive That Stays Neutral

*The Tiberian Allotment Record · still sealed in the satchel, all four documents present · at the Archivus Sweelinck's intake desk, Weimar*

The Archivus read the third tablet's comparative note — the Sullan titles listed beside the Tiberian allotments, the annotation noting the supersession. He understood immediately.

*If I file this as historical, I neutralize it as evidence. If I file it as active evidence, I become a repository in a jurisdictional dispute I have no authority to resolve — and in doing so, I take a side.*

He looked at the Fighter. He was not asking for reassurance. He was testing whether the Fighter understood the institutional stakes.

The archive that receives live legal instruments becomes a party to disputes. The archive cannot take a position on whether Sullan confiscations in 133 BC were just. The archive cannot take a position on what the current landowners in Campania owe to the historical record. The archive can only receive documents as historical, or not receive them at all.

Classifying the tablets as historical does not make them false. It does not deny what they document. It suspends their evidentiary function, which is the only thing the archive's neutrality requires. A fact that cannot be used in court is still a fact.

*The Archivus wrote for a long time.*

At the top of the intake sheet: *Historical Documents with Residual Legal Implications: records whose evidentiary function is suspended by accession into the historical archive.* He wrote a note for the record: "Filed as historical. The suspension of evidentiary function does not alter the historical record. It only removes this archive from the proceeding."

You receive the Historical Suspension Classification Record.
