# LHR — Beowulf
*Source read: full text (4,262 lines, 166 KB). Translator: Francis Barton Gummere.*

---

## Four Sentences

Beowulf is the oldest major poem in the English language, composed by an anonymous Christian poet drawing on older Germanic oral tradition sometime between 700 and 1000 AD, and set among the seafaring tribes of 6th-century Scandinavia. Its author is unknown but ranks among the great voices of Western literature by sheer force of survival — one manuscript, one chance, one poem that outlasted an entire civilization. The work follows the Geat warrior Beowulf through three monster-slaying trials separated by fifty years: the ravening Grendel who plagues a Danish mead-hall, Grendel's mother who strikes in revenge from her underwater lair, and finally a fire-breathing dragon who awakes because a single golden cup was stolen from a cursed hoard. What makes it immortal is not the monsters but the grief underneath them — the poem is a long elegy for a world of hall-joy and loyalty that is always already ending, where even the greatest king dies leaving his people exposed, and where the treasure he wins for them is buried again, useless, in the earth.

## Long Summary — Heroes, Decisions, Romance, Human Condition

### The World

Denmark, ~530 AD. King Hrothgar of the Scyldings has built Heorot, the greatest mead-hall in the world — gold-bright, gabled, ringing with song. For twelve years, the hall sits empty at night because Grendel, a shadow-creature descended from Cain, comes each dark to murder thirty thanes at a time, eat them, and vanish. Hrothgar is helpless. He has prayed, offered gold, tried peace: nothing. The hall smells of blood. The best men sleep elsewhere.

### Beowulf

He is a Geat — a young warrior from across the sea, nephew to King Hygelac. He has thirty men's grip-strength in one hand. He has wrestled nicors in the winter ocean for five nights and killed nine of them. He is not yet king; he is a thane looking for glory. When he hears of Grendel, he equips a ship and sails to Denmark uninvited.

His defining decision is made before the fight: he strips off his armor and puts away his sword. Grendel uses no weapons; to use a weapon would be unworthy. He will meet the monster on equal terms — muscle to muscle, grip to grip. This is not strategy. It is code. The choice costs him everything and wins him everything.

He grips Grendel's arm in the dark. The monster has never felt a grip like this. He tears Grendel's arm from its shoulder socket. Grendel crawls to his fen and dies. The arm hangs in the hall like a trophy. All of Denmark celebrates.

Then Grendel's mother comes.

### Grendel's Mother

She is described as "less grim than a man in war" — the poet almost apologizes for her, then shows her tearing the king's dearest counselor from his bed and dragging him into the dark. She is grief made physical. Her son was her whole world. Now she acts.

Beowulf's second decision: he dives alone into the monster-mere — a lightless lake full of sea-serpents and worm-things, burning at the bottom, deep beneath the cliff-face. He goes without hesitation. "Sorrow not, sage. Friends are better avenged than mourned."

Underwater, his sword Hrunting fails — it cannot bite the monster's hide. He throws it away. He wrestles her, falls, nearly dies. Then he sees, mounted on the wall of her underwater hall, an ancient sword forged by giants — so huge that no man should be able to wield it. He wrenches it free. Kills her. The blade dissolves in her blood until only the jewelled hilt remains. He brings back her head and the hilt. The weapon that saved him could not be kept.

### Hrothgar's Sermon

This is the emotional heart of the poem. After Beowulf's second victory, King Hrothgar — old, wise, loving — calls Beowulf to his side and gives him the most extraordinary speech. He warns him: even the greatest warrior, once pride takes root, begins to ruin. He describes a man who has everything — land, hall, gold, fame — and then slides into cruelty and selfishness because no one can tell him no. He is not talking about Beowulf. He is talking about every powerful man who ever forgot that Wyrd (fate) will find him too. "The flower of thy might lasts now a while: but erelong it shall be that sickness or sword thy strength shall minish."

Beowulf listens. Whether he truly hears it is the question the poem leaves open.

### Wealhtheow — The Queen

She is the poem's romance. Not erotic but deeply tender. She moves through the hall carrying the mead-cup from man to man, greeting the strangers, gracious and watchful. She gives Beowulf her finest necklace — the greatest collar in the world since the Brisings' necklace. She speaks carefully to her husband Hrothgar, and her subtext is plain: she fears Hrothulf, the nephew, will seize the throne from her sons when Hrothgar dies. She is a woman who has built her life around a hall that she knows will not outlast her husband, and she is asking Beowulf — a stranger — to someday protect her children.

"Men say to me, as son thou wishest yon hero to hold. Be glad with thy Geats; of those gifts be mindful. Preserve thy strength, and these striplings here counsel in kindness: requital be mine."

She asks a price for her necklace. It is a mother's love coded in court speech.

### The Dragon — Fifty Years Later

Beowulf becomes king of the Geats. Fifty winters pass. He rules wisely, fights well, gives rings. Then a slave steals a single golden cup from an ancient barrow — the hoard of a man who buried his whole dead tribe's wealth and then died alone. A dragon has been sleeping on that gold for three hundred years. It wakes. It burns everything it can reach, including Beowulf's own hall.

The old king makes his third decision. He will go alone (with eleven companions for witness, not for aid). He commands them to wait outside the cave. He is seventy years old and knows this is his end — he says so. "I would fain bestow on son of mine this gear of war, were given me now that any heir should after me come of my proper blood." He has no heir.

He fights the dragon and his sword shatters. The dragon bites his neck. He is dying.

### Wiglaf — The One Who Stayed

All eleven companions run. One does not: Wiglaf, youngest of his thanes, the last of the Waegmunding line, who remembers his oath of loyalty. He runs toward the fire and speaks — perhaps the most perfect five lines in all Old English verse:

*"I remember the time, when mead we took, what promise we made to this prince of ours in the banquet-hall, to our breaker-of-rings, for gear of combat to give him requital."*

He stands in the dragonfire. His wooden shield burns. He covers Beowulf. Together they kill the dragon. But the poison is already in Beowulf's blood.

Beowulf dies asking Wiglaf to bring him one piece of gold from the hoard so he can see it before his eyes close — so he can know he bought his people something. Wiglaf does. Beowulf thanks God. Then he gives Wiglaf his gold collar and his name.

After Beowulf dies, Wiglaf turns to the ten men who fled and delivers Beowulf's verdict on them in three sentences: "Death is better for every earl than a life of shame."

The poem ends at the pyre on the headland above the sea. The gold is buried again with Beowulf. Useless, as it always was. The mourning-woman sings her grief. The riders circle the barrow twelve times. End.

### The Human Condition

The poem is about the impossibility of holding on. The mead-hall is the center of all joy and it keeps getting burned. Every alliance is temporary. Every great man dies. Every treasure is either stolen or buried. Even Beowulf's victory over the dragon cannot save the Geats — the herald's speech at the end explicitly predicts that without their protector, the Swedes and Franks will come and there will be no one to stop them. The poem knows this. The poem grieves this. The poem does not offer consolation except the fact of Wiglaf — that once, one man did not run.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Shore-Warden's Choice** — A lone Scylding guard is posted to watch for raiding ships, but the approaching vessel carries armed warriors. He must decide on the spot: challenge them or let them pass? A diplomacy or intimidation skill-check determines whether Beowulf's party lands in peace or conflict.

2. **Unferth's Shame** — The king's champion Unferth publicly challenges the player (questioning their past) before they can speak to Hrothgar. Winning or surviving the flyting (verbal duel) is a prerequisite for the main quest's start. Losing means proving yourself by another route.

3. **Wealhtheow's Token** — The queen privately pulls the player aside and asks them to observe who speaks with her nephew Hrothulf during the feast — reporting back is a quest that reveals whether the throne is safe after Hrothgar dies. A pure observation-and-report quest. The player is the queen's spy for one night.

4. **One Night in Heorot** — The player must survive the night in the empty hall. No weapons allowed by Beowulf's decree. When Grendel enters and kills the companion asleep nearest the door, the player must hold their position and wait for Beowulf's grip to lock on — or break and run. Courage check + unarmed combat.

5. **The Mere of Monsters — Escort and Vigil** — Escort Hrothgar and his column through the monster-haunted moor to the mere's edge. Guard the shore while Beowulf dives. Fight off the nicors that surface to investigate the blood. Survive until Beowulf emerges. Best match for the escort-quest format.

6. **Wiglaf's Moment** — The player is one of eleven companions outside the barrow. The dragon erupts. All eleven companions break and run — the player must make a Courage DC or follow them. If they stand, they fight beside Wiglaf and help kill the dragon. If they run, they must face Wiglaf's judgment after.

7. **The Cursed Hoard** — A prequel: find the barrow before the dragon. Inside, a hundred years of history sealed in gold — and a curse on anyone who removes it. Taking even one coin triggers a chain: dragon awakes, village burns, the player must lead the survivors to safety before the dragon follows the hoard-piece they carry.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Mere of Monsters*

*Storyline 5 — Escort Quest. The player is a Fighter hired to escort Hrothgar's column from Heorot to the monster-mere and guard the shore while Beowulf dives.*

**THE TOKEN: A MEAD-HORN** — Wealhtheow's own drinking horn, of red ox-horn banded in gold. She has filled it with dark mead and pressed it into the Fighter's hands before departure. "Bring it to my husband at the water's edge. Let him drink before the hero dives. Do not spill a drop." The horn is full. It is warm from her hands.

---

### ACT I — *The Hall at Dusk*
*The horn: full, warm, held in the Fighter's right hand. Location: Heorot's eastern door.*

The hall is emptying of warriors. They file out into torchlight, cloaked against the moorland wind. Beowulf walks among them in his iron mail — the ring-links singing softly. He stops to take the sword Hrunting from Unferth, who holds it out in both hands like a man offering something that burns him.

**Unferth:** "It has never failed any man who gripped it in battle. Take it."

**Beowulf:** *(without looking at him)* "I'll return it."

Hrothgar passes last, leaning on the arm of a thane. He sees the horn in the Fighter's hands and his eyes soften. He does not speak. He nods once.

*The Fighter thinks: the queen filled this herself. Whatever else she is, she is afraid.*

---

### ACT II — *The Moorland Road*
*The horn: full, tilted carefully against the jostling. Location: the dark moor, two miles from Heorot.*

They march in single file over the wolf-cliffs, down into the fen-paths. No one speaks. The moon is wrong — too orange, too low. Twice the line halts; both times a sound from the reeds that resolves into nothing. Beowulf does not halt. He walks ahead of everyone, alone, mail ringing.

The Fighter holds the horn with two hands now. The mead sloshes near the lip. Somewhere in the dark ahead, something large moves. The line tightens.

**A thane, whispering:** "There are things in this moor that Grendel kept away. Now Grendel's dead."

**Another thane, not whispering:** "Then we should move faster."

*The Fighter thinks: the horn is still full. Keep it full.*

---

### ACT III — *The Edge of the Mere*
*The horn: passed from Fighter to Hrothgar. Location: the lip of the monster-lake, gray stone, dead trees.*

The lake is black. Its surface does not move the way water should. Something beneath it — not a current — makes it breathe. On the far bank, half in water, something large is coiled and still. One of Hrothgar's archers puts a bolt through it. The water thrashes and is quiet.

Hrothgar stops at the edge. He holds out his hand. The Fighter gives him the horn.

The old king turns his face toward the water and drinks. Not all of it — half. He stands there a long time. The half-empty horn goes back to the Fighter.

Beowulf removes his helmet, his mail, his sword-belt — Hrunting last. He sets it on a flat rock beside a thane named Aeschere's mark in the stone. Then he says, quietly, as if to himself:

**Beowulf:** "Sorrow not, sage. Friends are better avenged than mourned."

He walks into the water. He does not look back. The black mere swallows him.

*The Fighter thinks: he walked in smiling. I will not tell the queen that.*

---

### ACT IV — *The Long Watch*
*The horn: half-full, gripped, cooling fast. Location: the mere's edge, alone.*

The thanes wait through the first hour. Then the second. Then the water goes red from below — a deep red, like embers — and the shore-guard's horn sounds, and everyone draws their weapon for nothing, because nothing comes up.

At the ninth hour, Hrothgar's men begin to leave. Not running — just walking, faces down, the slow retreat of men who have decided someone is dead. One by one they go. The Fighter stays.

The shore is silent. The mere is silent. The half-full horn sits between the Fighter's hands, cooling to the temperature of stone.

Then, far down in the red-lit dark — something moves upward.

*The Fighter thinks: she told me not to spill a drop. I have not.*

---

### ACT V — *What Comes Back*
*The horn: emptied, given. Location: the mere's edge, then Heorot's feast-hall.*

Beowulf surfaces alone, carrying Grendel's severed head by the hair. Four men will be needed to carry it on a spear-pole back to the hall — but first he surfaces, and he is laughing, and his mail is eaten through in one place, and his face is cut, and none of it matters.

He wades to shore. The Fighter is there. He looks at the horn.

**Beowulf:** "Is that from Wealhtheow?"

The Fighter holds it out. He takes it. Drinks what remains in one motion. Sets the empty horn on the flat rock beside Hrunting, still sitting where he left it.

**Beowulf:** "Tell her it was good mead."

At Heorot that night, he returns Hrunting to Unferth with careful words of praise — the sword was not at fault, he says; the fault lay elsewhere. Unferth takes it without speaking. His hands are steady.

Wealhtheow stands at the far end of the hall and watches the Fighter return the empty horn. She nods once, the way Hrothgar did. She turns away.

The quest is finished. The debt to the water is paid.

*The Fighter thinks: I kept it full until she told me to give it. That is all a soldier ever has to do.*
