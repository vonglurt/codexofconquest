The Lang Arabian Nights draws from a different manuscript tradition than Burton (BGW): Galland's 1704–1717 French translation was based in part on a Syrian manuscript and in part on stories told to Galland by Hanna Diyab of Aleppo. Several famous stories (Aladdin, Ali Baba) appear in Galland/Lang but not in Burton's core Egyptian manuscript. The Seven Viziers is a distinct frame-tale structure present in this tradition.

## Cycle 1 — Angle: *The Story as Advocacy — What the vizier told the king to buy one more day for the truth*

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

The Galland-Lang tradition differs from the Burton-Calcutta tradition in its manuscript ancestry, its story selection, and its tone. Antoine Galland was a French Orientalist who produced the first European translation of the Arabian Nights between 1704 and 1717. He worked from a Syrian manuscript and supplemented it with stories told to him orally by Hanna Diyab, a Maronite Christian from Aleppo living in Paris. The famous Aladdin and Ali Baba stories come from Diyab; they appear in no Arabic manuscript that predates Galland, which has caused two centuries of scholarly argument about their authenticity. They may be genuine Syrian oral tradition; they may be Galland's embellishments of Diyab's skeletal versions; they may be something in between. Andrew Lang's 1898 adaptation drew on Galland and on the French children's publishing tradition, producing a version aimed at general readers rather than scholars, with the explicit sexual content removed and the narrative emphasis on the adventure and magic of the most famous stories.

**The Seven Viziers:** A distinct frame tale embedded within the Arabian Nights tradition — also known as *Sindibad* or the *Book of the Seven Sages* in various Western traditions — in which a prince is condemned to death by a wicked concubine's false accusation. Each day for seven days, a vizier tells the king a story demonstrating the danger of trusting women's testimony in haste. Each story buys another day. On the seventh day, the prince's enchantment that prevents him from speaking is lifted and he tells his own story, which proves the accusation false. The concubine is executed.

The frame structure is the inverse of Scheherazade's: there, a woman tells stories to prevent a man from killing; here, men tell stories to prevent a man from killing a man on a woman's false accusation. The stories the viziers tell are cautionary tales about clever women manipulating foolish men. They are not entirely fair to women. The structure is more interested in the mechanics of testimony than in the substance of the accusation.

**Aladdin and the Magic Lamp:** The most widely known story in the Western Arabian Nights tradition. Aladdin, a lazy boy in a Chinese city (the geography is fictional), is recruited by a Moroccan sorcerer who poses as his uncle. The sorcerer needs someone else to enter an underground garden and retrieve a magic lamp; the only person who can do this is someone whose stars align with the lamp's. Aladdin enters, takes the lamp but also takes as much treasure as he can carry and dawdles at the entrance. The sorcerer tries to grab the lamp first; Aladdin refuses; the sorcerer seals him underground and leaves.

Aladdin discovers the genie of the ring (a secondary djinn) and escapes. He discovers the genie of the lamp. He uses the lamp's power to acquire a palace, marry the sultan's daughter (Badr al-Budur), and repel the sorcerer's first attempt to reclaim the lamp. The sorcerer's brother tries a second scheme; Aladdin defeats this too. The story is about the person who receives unearned power and whether they grow into it — Aladdin begins as a good-natured boy who happens to be in the right place and ends as a ruler competent enough to hold what he was given.

**Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves:** Also from the Galland/Diyab tradition. The structure and content are described in the BGW file; the Lang version emphasizes Morgiana's competence more directly than some earlier translations. The story's moral logic: unearned discovery (Ali Baba overhears the password) can only survive through earned competence (Morgiana actually does everything that saves them).

**Prince Ahmed and the Fairy Peri Banu:** A distinctly Syrian-tradition story, present in Galland but not in Burton's core manuscript. Three princes shoot arrows to determine which prince will marry the princess; Ahmed's arrow disappears. He follows it and finds a fairy's underground kingdom. He marries the fairy Peri Banu and lives there in paradise. The sultan grows suspicious of his son's power (Peri Banu supplies him with magical gifts). An old woman, hired to spy, discovers the fairy's palace. The sultan demands things from Peri Banu that can only be gotten through magic; she supplies them; the sultan demands more. Eventually conflict escalates and the fairy army destroys the sultan's forces. Ahmed becomes a king in his own right.

The story's logic: paradise that remains hidden is sustainable; paradise that is investigated and exploited is destroyed; the old woman is not a villain (she is doing her job) but her job is the mechanism of destruction.

**Noureddin and the Fair Persian:** A love story within the Harun al-Rashid cycle. Two viziers of the caliph argue about whether to arrange a marriage between their children. One of them buys a beautiful Persian slave girl for his son to educate and later arrange a marriage. The son falls in love with the slave girl. The father, on his deathbed, tells the son never to sell her. After the father's death, the son's circumstances deteriorate; he is forced to sell the girl to the other vizier. Through various complications involving Harun al-Rashid's nocturnal wanderings, the couple is eventually reunited and the vizier punished.

The story is built around the father's deathbed instruction — never sell her — and the circumstances that make it impossible to follow. It is a study in how obligations outlive the circumstances that created them.

**The Enchanted Horse:** A Persian inventor brings a mechanical horse to a king's court. The horse can fly when the right peg is turned. The king's son takes it up without knowing how to descend; the inventor will not teach him until he is given the king's daughter in marriage; the prince brings the horse to another country, falls in love with a foreign princess, a series of complications follows involving the inventor trying to claim the princess, and the prince eventually rescuing her. The story is an extended meditation on the problem of technology without instruction: the horse that can take you anywhere but won't bring you back is a warning about every powerful tool given without context.

#### Section Notes

- **The Seven Viziers Frame**: The inverse of Scheherazade — stories told as advocacy, buying days for the truth to arrive.
- **Aladdin**: The question of unearned power and the self that grows into it; the Galland/Diyab tradition's most famous contribution to the collection.
- **Ali Baba**: Present in both traditions; the Lang version emphasizes Morgiana; see BGW for full treatment.
- **Prince Ahmed/Peri Banu**: A purely Syrian-tradition story about the destruction of paradise through investigation and exploitation.
- **Noureddin and the Fair Persian**: The obligation that outlives its circumstances; the deathbed instruction that cannot be kept.
- **The Enchanted Horse**: Technology without instruction; the tool that operates in only one direction.

### Pass 2 — Characters

**The Seventh Vizier** — In the Seven Viziers frame, the seven viziers take turns telling stories to prevent the execution. The seventh is the last — his story must work, because the enchantment lifts at the end of the seventh day and the prince can finally speak for himself. The seventh vizier's story is therefore the culminating argument: not a cautionary tale about women but a demonstration of patience's reward. He is the one who has been waiting for the right moment.

**Aladdin** — The trickster-hero who begins with nothing and ends with everything. Unlike Sinbad, who earns his wealth through repeated suffering, Aladdin's wealth comes from a lamp. The story is honest about this: the genie does the work, Aladdin makes the wishes. What grows in Aladdin is not skill but character — the willingness to use power carefully, the ability to hold onto what he has been given. The lamp is a loan; the self that carries it is the permanent asset.

**Peri Banu** — The fairy who inhabits an underground paradise. She is the most powerful character in her story, capable of providing anything the sultan demands — and she does provide it, which is the mistake. Every provision that satisfies the sultan generates a larger demand. She is not naive; she knows what is happening. She provides anyway, because the alternative is war sooner. She chooses war later, on her terms. She wins.

**The Moroccan Sorcerer** — The agent of exploitation who knows where the power is and cannot use it himself — only someone with the right natal configuration can enter the underground garden. He is the figure of the expert who needs a naive instrument: he requires Aladdin's innocence to complete what his knowledge cannot. When Aladdin deviates from the plan (taking extra treasure, not immediately passing the lamp) the sorcerer's plan fails because it had no room for the human variable.

**Noureddin's Father** — A man who makes a deathbed instruction that he knows his son will have difficulty keeping. The instruction is not foolish — never sell her is correct advice for the circumstances under which he gives it. The circumstances change. The instruction doesn't. This is the central tension of the Noureddin story: the dead father's last gift is also his last burden.

### Pass 3 — The Pivotal Scene: The Seventh Day (Seven Viziers Frame)

The prince has been under enchantment for seven days. Each morning he has woken unable to speak; each day a vizier has told a story. The king has been inclined toward the accusation; the viziers' stories have made him hesitate. Now it is the seventh day and the enchantment will lift at noon.

The seventh vizier's story is structured differently from the others. The previous six told tales about women who manipulated men. The seventh tells a tale about a king who was about to execute his son for a crime the son did not commit, and who was counseled by one adviser to wait, and waited, and the truth arrived before the execution. The story is not about women being untrustworthy. It is about waiting being justified by what arrives.

When the seventh vizier finishes, it is noon. The prince's voice returns. He tells his own story. It takes an hour. The king hears every detail. The accusation is false. The concubine is executed.

What the seven days produced was not merely time but a state of mind in the king: seven stories, each demonstrating that evidence arrives if given room to arrive, have created in the king a readiness to listen that would not have been there on the first morning. The execution was not simply delayed — the king who would have executed on the first day is not the same king who listened to the prince's story on the seventh.

This is the advocacy model: not argument (which the king can reject) and not evidence (which the king did not yet have) but story — which changes the shape of the listener rather than the content of the case.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **"The Seventh Day"** — A Syrian monastery has a manuscript fragment containing an unusual variant of the seventh vizier's story — one in which the embedded story contains what appears to be an actual court case record from a real Baghdad proceeding, embedded in the narrative as a story-within-a-story. The document is simultaneously a legal record and a fictional narrative. Fighter escorts it from Aleppo to a neutral archive.

2. **"Aladdin's Registration"** — In the story, the sultan's daughter was promised in marriage to Aladdin through a formal contract. A Syrian legal records collection contains a marriage contract template from the same period and region that uses identical formulary — suggesting the story was constructed around a real document type. Fighter escorts the template collection.

3. **"The Inventor's Patent"** — The enchanted horse's inventor must have documented the mechanism somewhere — a device that complex has a design record. A Baghdad archive of craft guild records from the 9th century contains a filing for "a Persian mechanism of copper and silver capable of directed aerial travel, requiring two operator inputs." Fighter escorts it from Baghdad to a neutral archive.

4. **"Prince Ahmed's Arrow"** — In the story, Prince Ahmed's arrow disappears when shot and is found pointing toward the entrance to the fairy's underground kingdom. A Syrian folk tale collection, contemporary with the Galland-era manuscript, contains an account of a man who found an arrow in the hills above Aleppo pointing at a cleft in the rock, entered the cleft, and found a spring of unusual quality. The account was written down by a parish priest as a curiosity. Fighter escorts it.

5. **"The Deathbed Instruction"** — Noureddin's father gave him one instruction: never sell the slave girl. A legal instrument from a Damascus court records this type of conditional manumission prohibition — a document type that was legally standard in the period, used by dying merchants to bind their heirs. A Damascus archive has a collection of such instruments. Fighter escorts the collection.

6. **"Hanna Diyab's Account"** — Galland was told the Aladdin story by Hanna Diyab; he noted this in his journal. Diyab later wrote his own memoir. A copy of the relevant section of Diyab's memoir — in which he describes telling Galland the story and what Galland said about it — survives in a Maronite monastery. For a 1367 game, the document would be: a Syrian Christian storyteller's account of telling a story to a foreign scholar who wrote it down, and what it felt like to hear it read back in a different language. Fighter escorts the memoir section. (Note: this is technically Galland-era, 1708 — not suitable for 1367. Adapt as: a Syrian Christian storyteller in 14th-century Aleppo tells a foreign Venetian scholar the Aladdin story; the scholar's notes survive.)

7. **"The Peri Banu Survey"** — In the Prince Ahmed story, the old woman spy makes a systematic survey of the route to the fairy's underground palace, noting landmarks, distances, and the entrance. Her report to the sultan is the document that destroys paradise. A Syrian manuscript contains what may be a formal survey of a route in the hills north of Aleppo, written in the style of a formal geographic report, with notation of a cave entrance. Fighter escorts it to an archive before the route it describes is used for the wrong purpose.

**Existing themes to avoid**: all themes from BGW and prior books (see BGW file for full list).

**CAI theme chosen**: *The story told to delay an execution is not entertainment but testimony in narrative form; the seven days are not time bought but evidence accumulated; the story changes the shape of the listener so that the truth, when it arrives, finds a king who can hear it.*

This is distinct from all above, including BGW's theme. BGW: the collection as survival built across a thousand nights (the teller's arc). CAI: the story as advocacy tool that changes the listener's receptivity before the evidence arrives (the advocate's arc). Different agent, different direction, different structure.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Seventh Day*

### The Token

*The Variant Seventh — a manuscript fragment, Syrian, approximately 9th–10th century, containing the seventh vizier's story in a variant not found in any other copy. The variant version embeds within the story a court case record, formally structured, naming parties, charges, and witnesses — the elements of a real legal proceeding, framed as the story's internal example. The fragment was separated from its parent manuscript; it is not known which collection it belonged to. A Maronite monastery near Aleppo has preserved it as a curiosity for two centuries, without knowing which story cycle it completes.*

---

**ACT I**

*The Variant Seventh — on a reading stand in a monastery library — at the Monastery of Mar Marun, hills outside Aleppo, late afternoon.*

Brother Yuhanna has known the fragment was there since his teacher told him about it forty years ago. He has not found the parent manuscript. He has shown it to three scholars in that time; one identified it as a Seven Viziers variant, one said it was a legal document misbound into a narrative context, and one said it was a deliberate forgery designed to launder a real court record into fiction where it could not be used as legal evidence. All three were probably right about different aspects.

He brings the Fighter to the reading stand. He explains the fragment: a seventh vizier's story, ending with a court case record inside the story that names real people and real charges. The case inside the story involves a false accusation against an imprisoned merchant — someone with an economic grievance against the accusers.

"If the third scholar is right," he says, "the man who wrote this was hiding evidence of a real injustice inside a story no one would search for it in. If the first two are right, it is what it appears to be — a storyteller who used a real case as the model for his embedded example."

He wants it in a neutral archive. He does not want it to be used as legal evidence in a 9th-century Damascus proceeding — that ship has sailed — but he does not want it destroyed by someone who is afraid it might be.

> **Skill Check: History DC 12.** Identify which manuscript tradition this fragment belongs to — the Seven Viziers frame is a recognizable format, and the formulary of the embedded court case matches known Syrian legal document types from the Abbasid period. Confirming this gives Brother Yuhanna enough confidence to release the fragment.

**Fail:** Brother Yuhanna is uncertain. He sends the Fighter to find the monastery's abbot, who has been down in Aleppo for three days and is expected back tomorrow. One day's delay.

**Pass:** The identification is sufficient. He takes the fragment from the reading stand and wraps it himself, carefully, in a linen cloth he has kept ready for this purpose. "I've been waiting for someone who would know what it is," he says. He hands it to the Fighter without ceremony.

**TOKEN GRANTED: The Variant Seventh** — a manuscript fragment in Syrian-Arabic hand, containing the seventh vizier's story with an embedded court case record; wrapped in linen, in a cedar board binding with a single cord.

---

**ACT II**

*The Variant Seventh — in the pack — in the Aleppo market district, morning.*

The monastery's abbot has returned from Aleppo. He is not obstructing the transfer — he approved of it years ago — but he has sent a message to a Damascus scholar named Khalil who has been corresponding with the monastery about this fragment for six years. Khalil arrived in Aleppo last night. He is at the caravanserai near the market.

He intercepts the Fighter at the market. He is not hostile; he is alarmed. He has been trying to identify the parties in the embedded court case — he believes one of the names matches a merchant family whose descendants are currently involved in a property dispute in Damascus, and that the fragment, if submitted to a Damascus court, could settle the dispute in their favor. He wants the Fighter to stop in Damascus before going north.

*— He genuinely believes this will help a real family. He does not see that using a fragment as legal evidence in a current proceeding would end its availability as a historical document.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 12.** Explain to Khalil that the fragment's value as a historical document — which includes its potential evidentiary value — is protected by neutral archiving; that an archive can certify a copy for legal use while preserving the original; and that submitting the original to a Damascus court proceeding destroys the chain of custody that makes any future certification meaningful.

**Fail:** Khalil is not persuaded and says he will travel to Damascus to file a formal request for the fragment as evidence in the ongoing proceeding. The Fighter must reach Damascus and cross to the north road before the Damascus court can issue a search notice. Two days' lead.

**Pass:** Khalil is persuaded by the certification argument — he hadn't thought about copies. He writes out a formal description of the family name he identified and asks the Fighter to ensure it reaches the archive with the fragment. He hands over the note. "If the archivist can certify a copy, the family can use it." He steps back.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT III**

*The Variant Seventh — in the pack — on the road north of Aleppo, two days out.*

The road to Anatolia passes through a stretch of hills that has been contested between local Ottoman-aligned warlords and the remnant of Mongol-aligned local power since the 1340s. Not a formal war — a sustained disagreement expressed through tolls, road taxes, and periodic armed enforcement. A group of eight men representing one toll collector's interests have set up a checkpoint on the road where it narrows through a pass.

They want a road tax. The amount is more than the Fighter is carrying. When the Fighter cannot or will not pay, they examine the pack to find something worth taking instead.

*— They're not hunting the fragment specifically. They are taxing everything that passes. The fragment is worth nothing to them in itself.*

> **Combat: 6 Road Guards** — AC 12, HP 15 each. Armed with spears and short swords. Not enthusiastic fighters — they are toll collectors who have become accustomed to threats working. Two will yield immediately if the first two are put down hard.

After combat, the pass is clear. The road opens into broader country heading toward the coast.

> **Skill Check: Athletics DC 10.** The pack took a jolt in the fighting. Confirm the cedar board binding held and the linen wrapping is intact.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT IV**

*The Variant Seventh — in the pack — at the Venetian factor's house in Antioch, one day west.*

Antioch, 1367, is under the Mamluk administration following its fall in 1268. The Venetian commercial presence is sustained through a factor who manages the overland trade. The factor, a man named Marco, has been told by his Damascus correspondent (Khalil's assistant, who sent a letter when Khalil failed to stop the Fighter) that the fragment contains a legally sensitive document with implications for a Damascus property dispute.

Marco is not a lawyer. But he knows that a document with legal implications, traveling through Mamluk territory without authorization from the Damascus court, is technically removable under Mamluk commercial law covering disputed assets. He has the authority to hold the pack for three days while he sends for clarification from Damascus.

He exercises this authority politely but firmly.

*— He is using legal procedure as delay. He is not sure the fragment is worth three days of his relationship with the Fighter, but someone told him to try.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 13.** Argue that the fragment is a historical document, not a disputed asset — that Khalil's identification of a name is a scholarly hypothesis, not a legal determination, and that a name appearing in a 9th-century story embedded in a 9th-century legal example does not constitute legal nexus to a 14th-century property dispute under any jurisdiction. Marco knows enough law to evaluate this argument if it is made precisely.

**Fail:** Marco holds the pack for the three days. The Fighter waits in Antioch. On the third day, no clarification from Damascus has arrived and Marco releases the pack by default.

**Pass:** Marco thinks about it. He knows what a legal nexus argument is and he knows this one is not solid. He releases the pack immediately. "Tell whoever sent that letter to get better legal advice before using a commercial factor as their first move."

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT V**

*The Variant Seventh — in the pack — at the Weimar archive intake desk, Archivus Sweelinck, afternoon.*

The Archivus unwraps the linen and opens the cedar boards. He reads the fragment in silence. He reads the embedded court case record in silence. He sets the fragment down and looks at the ceiling for a moment.

"This is a story that contains a legal document." He looks at the Fighter. "The legal document is real — the formulary is correct, the case structure is standard for 9th-century Syrian procedure. The question is whether it was embedded in the story to preserve it, or whether the story was constructed around it to give it a narrative context that would survive when the original was lost."

He opens the intake form.

"If it was embedded to preserve it, the story is a carrier. If the story was constructed around it, the legal record is the point and the story is the camouflage." He pauses. "In either case, both layers must be catalogued."

*— He is not asking for an opinion. He is thinking aloud as he decides how to write the entry. The intake record requires the classification of the document's primary layer, and the Fighter, having heard Brother Yuhanna's three scholars, is the only person present who knows all three arguments.*

> **Skill Check: Investigation DC 12.** Synthesize the three scholars' arguments: legal document misbound into narrative; narrative that used a real case as its model; deliberate embedding to preserve evidence in a form no one would look for it. The third argument is the most consistent with the formulaic precision of the embedded record — if you are making an example, you don't need the names to be real; if you are preserving evidence, you need everything exact.

**Fail:** The Archivus writes "Seven Viziers variant — Syrian tradition, embedded legal case record, relationship uncertain." Filed as compound document. The preservation argument is not noted.

**Pass:** The Fighter names the preservation argument and its basis: the names are real, the formulary is precise, the fragment was separated from its parent manuscript — probably deliberately, to hide it without losing it. It is a legal record preserved inside a story because stories survive when records don't.

Sweelinck writes: *Compound document — Seven Viziers variant (7th vizier's story, Syrian tradition) containing embedded Abbasid-period court case record, preserved in narrative form; primary function identified as evidentiary preservation; story serves as carrier. Khalil ibn Yusuf's family identification note appended.*

He closes the ledger.

"A story that is also evidence." He sets the fragment on the intake shelf. "Someone trusted that both layers would survive." He does not say: they were right. He does not need to.

**TOKEN TAKEN: The Variant Seventh**

The fragment rests on the intake shelf, catalogued as a compound document. Both layers are noted. The family name Khalil identified is in the appended note. The archive can certify a copy for the Damascus proceeding.
