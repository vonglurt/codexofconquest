**Original:** Sanskrit Mahabharata, c. 13th–14th century BCE composition; ~90,000 couplets in original; Dutt condenses to ~2,000 English couplets

## §Part 1 — Overview (both parts)

Dutt's condensed verse Mahabharata presents the core narrative of the great Indian epic: the rivalry of the Pandava and Kaurava branches of the Kuru royal family, culminating in the eighteen-day war at Kurukshetra. Dutt explicitly compares the work to the Iliad — Yudhishthir as Agamemnon, Bhima as Ajax, Arjun as Achilles, Karna as Hector, Krishna as Ulysses. The translation is not complete but selects and translates the key narrative passages, linking them with prose summaries of what is skipped. The battle books (VIII–X) are severely condensed.

The distinctive tragic element of the Mahabharata — absent from the Iliad — is that almost every major character knows or is told their fate in advance and proceeds anyway. Karna is told he is Pritha's son (Arjun's brother) yet fights on. Bhishma tells Duryodhan the war is lost yet continues. Savitri is told her chosen husband will die in one year yet marries him. This "fate-known-in-advance" quality gives the epic an unusual tone of doomed nobility.

## §Part 2 — Structure

### Book I — Astra Darsana (The Tournament)
The princes of Kuru are trained by the Brahman warrior Drona. At a tournament, the unknown warrior Karna appears and matches Arjun's every feat. Kripa demands Karna declare his lineage (commoners cannot challenge princes); Duryodhan immediately crowns Karna king of Anga to give him royal rank. Karna and Duryodhan seal a friendship that will last to their deaths. The crowd divides: "Arjun is the victor!" "Karna won!" Pritha secretly weeps, recognizing Karna as the son she abandoned. The rivalry between Arjun and Karna is established as the spine of the epic.

### Book II — Swayamvara (The Bride's Choice)
After surviving assassination attempts by Duryodhan, the Pandavas live in disguise as Brahmans. Princess Draupadi of Panchala holds a swayamvara: she will marry whoever strings the iron bow and shoots a moving target. Every crowned king fails. Arjun, disguised as a Brahman, strings the bow and wins. Angry kings attack; Bhima tears up a tree and fights them off. Krishna recognizes the brothers. Draupadi becomes wife to all five Pandavas by their mother's inadvertent command. Marriage of Arjun's son Abhimanyu to Matsya's princess is foreshadowed.

### Book III — Rajasuya (The Imperial Sacrifice)
Yudhishthir, now king of Indraprastha (near Delhi), performs the Rajasuya — the imperial coronation sacrifice at which all kings bring tribute. Bhishma designates Krishna as the first-honored guest. Sisupala, king of Chedi, delivers a furious speech against honoring the "uncrowned Vrishni chieftain." Krishna recites Sisupala's crimes, then throws his discus and kills him. His soul enters Krishna. The sacrifice concludes with Yudhishthir confirmed as emperor. Duryodhan returns consumed by jealousy.

### Book IV — Dyuta (The Fatal Dice)
Sakuni, prince of Gandhara and expert at false dice, challenges Yudhishthir, who cannot decline without dishonor. Game after game Yudhishthir loses — wealth, kingdom, brothers, himself, and finally Draupadi. Duryodhan's brother Duhsasan drags Draupadi into the council hall by her hair. She makes her plaint before silent elders. Bhima vows to break Duhsasan's thigh in battle and drink his blood. Krishna later supplies Draupadi's sari miraculously so she cannot be stripped. Dhrita-rashtra releases them but Sakuni forces a second game: the losers must spend twelve years in forest exile and one year in concealment — if discovered, another twelve years. The Pandavas go into exile. Draupadi vows not to bind her hair until she has washed it in Duhsasan's blood.

### Book V — Pativrata-Mahatmya (Woman's Love)
In the forest years, the sage Markandeya tells Yudhishthir the tale of Savitri and Satyavan. Princess Savitri of Madra chooses as her husband the forest-hermit prince Satyavan — despite the sage Narad warning that Satyavan is fated to die in exactly one year. "Only once a maiden chooseth," she says. The year passes. On the appointed day Savitri follows Satyavan into the forest. He collapses with a headache; she holds his head in her lap. The dark-crowned god Yama arrives and draws out the tiny soul. Savitri follows. Yama offers her three boons rather than keep arguing, each time saying "but the dead do not return" — yet she phrases her boons so cunningly (restoration of her father-in-law's sight; restoration of his kingdom; children to be born to Satyavan and herself) that the last boon logically requires Satyavan's return to life. Yama yields. Satyavan wakes. They return home to find everything Savitri requested has been granted. The story is celebrated across India as a festival.

### Book VI — Go-Harana (Cattle-Lifting)
In the year of concealment the Pandavas serve King Virata of Matsya in disguise. Yudhishthir as dice-player, Bhima as cook, Arjun as the eunuch Brihannala (teaching dance), the twins as horse- and cattle-keepers, Draupadi as waiting-woman. The Trigartas raid from the south while Duryodhan steals Virata's cattle from the north. Prince Uttara, left to defend, needs a charioteer — Arjun volunteers as Brihannala. They retrieve the Pandavas' hidden weapons from a sami tree (wrapped to look like corpses). Arjun reveals himself to Uttara, then routs the Kuru forces including Bhishma, Drona, and Karna. Arjun arranges the marriage of his son Abhimanyu to Virata's daughter.

### Book VII — Udyoga (The Preparation)
At the wedding council, Krishna advocates for peace — let both sides keep their kingdoms. Valadeva counsels submission; Satyaki demands war. Drupad (Draupadi's father) prepares for war while sending envoys. Krishna himself goes to Hastina as ambassador; his great speech to Dhrita-rashtra (a set piece of tragic grandeur) begs for a partition of the kingdom. Each counselor — Bhishma, Drona, Vidura — urges Duryodhan to yield. Duryodhan refuses: "Spot that a needle's point can cover — not to them be given." Krishna is physically seized (briefly) by Duryodhan; Krishna simply shows his cosmic form and departs. War is certain.

### Book VIII — Bhishma-Badha (Fall of Bhishma)
Bhishma, ancient patriarch of both houses, leads the Kuru forces for ten days. He is invulnerable and drives the Pandavas into flight repeatedly. Arjun is reluctant to fight his beloved grandsire. The Pandavas visit Bhishma by night; he tells them his one weakness: he will not fight a woman-born warrior. Krishna identifies Sikhandin (born female, transformed male) and places him in the van. On the tenth day Bhishma drops his arms before Sikhandin and falls. He lies on a bed of arrows while the war continues around him. Before dying he tells Karna the truth of his birth: "Arjun is thy brother, Karna, end this sad fraternal war." Karna refuses.

### Book IX — Drona-Badha (Fall of Drona)
Drona commands for five days, threatening to capture Yudhishthir. He forms the chakravyuha (wheel formation) which only Abhimanyu knows how to enter — but not how to exit. Abhimanyu enters alone, fighting magnificently, until his bow breaks, his horses die, his car collapses. He fights on with a wheel-hub as a club. Seven champions surround him and kill him. Arjun learns of his son's death that evening; his vow to kill Jayadratha (who blocked the Pandavas from entering the chakravyuha) by sunset tomorrow or perish on a funeral pyre drives Book IX's climax. With Krishna's help (a false eclipse to make Karna think sunset had come), Arjun kills Jayadratha at the last moment before true sunset. Drona is killed by a trick: Bhima kills an elephant named Aswa-thaman, and Yudhishthir (the only one who cannot lie) is made to say "Aswa-thaman is dead" — Drona, believing his son dead, lays down his arms and is killed by Dhrishta-dyumna.

### Book X — Karna-Badha (Fall of Karna)
Karna leads the Kuru forces for two days. The climactic duel between Karna and Arjun is the crowning event of the Epic. They are nearly equal; Karna's divine bow Vijaya matches Arjun's Gandiva. Mid-battle Karna's chariot wheel sinks in the soft earth. He asks Arjun to pause — citing the warrior's code against attacking a helpless man. Krishna answers: did you think of the warrior's code when Draupadi was dragged by her hair? When Abhimanyu was hunted by seven men? Arjun fires. Karna falls. Salya leads the final day; Yudhishthir kills him. Bhima breaks Duryodhan's thigh (fulfilling his vow). Aswa-thaman (Drona's son) perpetrates a midnight massacre of the Pandava camp — Dhrishta-dyumna, Sikhandin, and Draupadi's five sons are killed. Duryodhan hears the news and dies content.

### Book XI �� Sraddha (Funeral Rites)
The blind king Dhrita-rashtra leads the Kuru women to the battlefield. Gandhari's lament over Duryodhan is among the great passages of the epic. The dead of both sides are cremated. Pritha reveals to her sons that Karna was their eldest brother — "Sun-like trod the spacious earth." Yudhishthir laments: "All were ours, O noble Karna! with thee by thy brother's side... this carnage of the Kurus these sad eyes had never seen." Oblations are made to Karna as eldest brother.

### Book XII — Aswa-Medha (Sacrifice of the Horse)
Yudhishthir's guilt over the war's carnage leads Vyasa to recommend the Aswa-medha (horse sacrifice). A horse is released to wander under Arjun's escort; wherever it is detained, Arjun fights and wins. The horse returns; the sacrifice is performed with enormous feasting and Brahman gift-giving. Yudhishthir attempts to give Vyasa his entire kingdom; Vyasa returns it, asking for Brahman gifts instead. The sin of the war is expiated.

### Conclusion
Dhrita-rashtra, Gandhari, and Pritha retire to a forest and die in a forest fire. Krishna's city Dwarka is swallowed by the sea. The Pandavas and Draupadi set out for the Himalayas to die: Draupadi falls first, then Sahadeva, Nakula, Arjun, Bhima. Yudhishthir alone reaches heaven bodily, where Indra introduces him to all the slain warriors and family now living as celestials.

### Translator's Epilogue
Romesh Dutt's scholarly essay on the Mahabharata's composition, growth, and comparison with the Iliad. Explains his translation method (selected verse passages linked by prose notes, ~2,000 couplets from 90,000). Includes Sanskrit glossary. Date: 13 August 1898, University College London.

## §HTY-01 Full Vignette Spec — "Savitri's Three Bargains"

### Premise
The scholar Vardan (Armenian, fifties, ink-stained fingers, speaks Persian and Greek) has spent three years writing a commentary called "The Three Bargains" — a logical analysis of how Savitri's boons to Yama (Death) were constructed so that the final one necessarily required her husband's return to life, without ever directly asking for it. He argues this is the most precise example of argumentation in all world literature. He has finished a fair copy in Persian and Greek. He needs it transported to a Jewish logician named Haim in Constantinople who studies the same tradition. Vardan cannot travel (gout). He will pay for the escort with a gold piece and a reference letter.

The Fighter delivers the manuscript. No one else knows this is the cargo (it looks like a scholar's bundle of notes).

### Act Structure (5 acts, skill check DC 12 each, TOKEN granted Act 1 taken Act 5)

**Act 1 — The Commission** (Tabriz)  
Vardan's study. Shelves of manuscripts. He gives the Fighter a leather satchel containing the manuscript bundle, a sealed letter for Haim, and a second sealed letter for a contact in Trebizond who will arrange the crossing. He says: "If you are stopped by anyone who asks what this is — it is my private correspondence with a physician in Bursa. Nothing scholarly, nothing political." He is a little nervous. TOKEN granted. Skill check: does the Fighter notice that Vardan is more frightened than he lets on? (Insight/Perception) — on success, a man has been watching the house.

**Act 2 — The Road to Trebizond**  
Two days out of Tabriz, a tollman on a mountain pass stops the Fighter and demands to inspect the satchel. He has a list of contraband — "foreign manuscripts spreading heresies." He can read enough to identify the title page. Skill check: Persuasion or Deception — talk past the tollman, or pay a bribe (3 silver, reduces final pay), or find an alternate path (adds half a day). On failure: the tollman confiscates the satchel and the Fighter must retrieve it from the tollhouse that night (a second skill check, Stealth or Sleight of Hand).

**Act 3 — Trebizond**  
Vardan's contact is a spice merchant named Zoravar. He reads the letter and says the next ship to Constantinople leaves in three days. He offers the Fighter lodging in his warehouse. That night a woman knocks on the warehouse door — she says she is Vardan's niece, that Vardan sent her, and that he wants the manuscript back because he fears for its safety. Skill check: Insight — is she genuine? On success: she is lying (she is a hired agent; the watcher from Act 1 sent her). On failure: the Fighter hands over the manuscript and must recover it before the ship leaves (track to a rooming-house, another check).

**Act 4 — The Sea Crossing**  
On the ship, a Genoese merchant in the cabin becomes curious about the Fighter's cargo. He is friendly and offers wine. He mentions he collects rare manuscripts and names a price that is more than Vardan promised in total pay. Skill check: Persuasion or Wisdom Save (resisting temptation). On fail: the Fighter is tempted; if they sell, the quest ends here with a mixed outcome (good coin, no completion). On success: politely declined, Genoese accepts it with grace; he then mentions casually that a man in Trebizond had been asking about manuscripts destined for a Jewish scholar in Constantinople — and described the Fighter's appearance.

**Act 5 — Delivery to Haim** (Constantinople)  
Haim's house is in the Jewish quarter near the Golden Horn. He is elderly, bird-like, and immediately recognizes the manuscript's value — he reads the first page standing in his doorway and lets out a short laugh of pleasure. He pays Vardan's promised fee plus a small bonus. As the Fighter turns to leave, Haim says: "Tell Vardan — the argument works. But only if the reader believes Yama was bound by his own word. In our tradition, Death is not." TOKEN taken. Quest complete.

### Mechanics
- 5 acts, DC 12 skill checks, Fighter as silent escort
- TOKEN: a small clay seal Vardan pressed into the satchel's wax closure — if the Fighter has it at Act 5, Haim remarks on it and adds the bonus
- Cargo: manuscript bundle (medium, fragile, weight 1); does not need to stay dry but must not be opened by unauthorized hands
- Gold reward: 1 gold piece (Vardan) + 4 silver (Haim's bonus) on success; 1 gold piece - 3 silver bribe if paid in Act 2; 0 if sold to Genoese

## §HTY-02 — Vignette Cycle 2: "Draupadi's Plaint"

### Cycle vs. Cycle — What This Angle Reveals

Cycle 1 ("Savitri's Three Bargains") took the angle of the argument that worked: Savitri loved with such precision that she framed each boon so that Death was logically bound by his own word and could not refuse the last without denying the first. The argument succeeded because it gave Death nothing he did not already own.

Cycle 2 takes the inverse: the argument nobody would receive. Draupadi was dragged by the hair into the council hall and asked one question before the assembled elders: was she a slave? Could a man stake his wife after having already lost himself at dice, having already become a slave himself? The elders were silent. No one spoke. The question was not refused on grounds of law — it was refused on grounds of comfort. What Draupadi left behind was the shape of the unanswered question, and that shape became the form other women used to ask the same question before other courts.

### TOKEN: "The Plaint Fragment"

A single unbound folio — an Arabic translation of a Pahlavi commentary on the Sabha Parva (Assembly Book) episode — preserving in indirect paraphrase the moment of Draupadi's question: *"She asked before the assembly: by what right is she called a slave? The assembly was silent. No answer is recorded in the tradition."* A later hand has added in the margin: *"The question was not refused on grounds of law. It was refused on grounds of comfort."*

The folio is folded into four, sealed with a plain wax seal, no name written on the outside. Inside: the Sanskrit narrative episode condensed, the philosophical commentary, and the scholiast's margin-note.

### Route

`NIS` (Nishapur — private house of Zara, widow of a Persian scholar) → `TBZ` (Tabriz — Fatima's household, the woman who will use the form) → `WM` (Weimar archive)

### Five-Act Vignette Play

**Present tense. Second person. The Plaint Fragment is the grammatical subject of all TOKEN stage directions.**

---

**ACT I — "The Widow Who Kept It Without Knowing" (Nishapur)**

Zara is in her sixties. Her husband was a scholar who collected Pahlavi-Arabic translations for thirty years. He died twelve years ago. She has been distributing his manuscripts to people who can use them; most are technical commentaries, dry, necessary for specialists. This one she kept last because the margin-note disturbed her.

She opens the folio on the table. You read the margin-note. *The Plaint Fragment lies flat on the table, its fold-crease sharp after decades.*

"I don't know where it should go," she says. "I know it shouldn't stay here."

She names the woman in Tabriz: "Her name is Fatima. She survived a Mongol court decision that took everything. She has been looking for a way to ask her question before a court that cannot pretend it has never heard it before." She pauses. "This is the shape of the question she needs."

*Dialogue DC 12 — Insight: Zara is testing whether you understand what "shape of a question" means — whether you know why a form that was already refused once is more powerful in a second court than a form that has never been tested.*

*On success:* She folds the folio back into its four creases and gives it to you without further explanation. "Take it to Fatima first. Then to the archive." *The Plaint Fragment is in your hands, folded, plain wax seal intact.*

*On fail:* She gives it to you anyway, but adds: "Don't open it. Don't read it on the road. If you understand it wrong, it won't work for Fatima."

*TOKEN granted Act I — folio received, sealed, no exterior marking.*

---

**ACT II — "The Classification Checkpoint" (Road west from Nishapur, toward Tabriz)**

A Jalayirid administrative checkpoint on the caravan road. The officer in charge has three categories for manuscript documents: religious text (free passage), legal argument (reviewed and taxed), and personal correspondence (declared and stamped). He wants to open the folio and read the title.

*Skill check: Persuasion DC 12 — establish that an ancient Sanskrit council narrative translated into Arabic by a Pahlavi commentator is neither a current legal argument nor commercial goods, but a private scholarly gift.*

*On success:* He stamps the exterior of the fold: *"Personal scholarly effects — no jurisdictional application."* He waves you through. *The Plaint Fragment now carries a transit stamp on its outermost fold — the third annotation on this document, the first added in transit.*

*On fail:* He holds it for review. You wait two days in a caravanserai while a cleric is consulted. The cleric rules it non-jurisdictional. You lose two days but the folio is returned intact.

*TOKEN check Act II — transit stamp added to exterior.*

---

**ACT III — "The Woman Who Needed the Form" (Tabriz)**

Fatima's house is well-kept and watched. A man sits outside with no obvious purpose. You have been told about him: he is a Mongol court agent from the administrative office that stripped her of her husband's estate. They are waiting to see if she petitions.

Inside, Fatima reads the folio standing. She reads it twice. Then she sits down, takes out paper, and begins writing — her own petition — in the exact form of the question Draupadi asked. She addresses it not to the Mongol court but to Weimar: "To the archive which preserves what courts refused to answer."

She finishes and holds it out to you. "Attach this to the folio. Let the archive receive both — the original refusal and the later use. That's what makes it impossible to ignore. One refusal is an incident. Two is a form."

The agent outside knocks on the door.

*Skill check: Persuasion or Deception DC 13 — exit with both documents before the agent can demand to examine them.*

*On success:* You leave by a side passage Fatima has prepared. The agent sees you from a distance but cannot reach you before you are in the street. *The Plaint Fragment is folded with Fatima's petition tucked inside — two documents now, though the outer seal is intact.*

*On fail:* The agent reaches you at the door and demands to see the folio. He cannot legally stop you (personal scholarly effects — transit stamp). He takes the transit time to record your description.

*TOKEN check Act III — Fatima's petition added inside the fold; agent has your description.*

---

**ACT IV — "The Road to Weimar" (Anatolian-Balkan transit road)**

Two men stop you on the road before a river crossing. They work for the Mongol court agent. His mandate: the petition must not arrive anywhere. The folio is secondary; Fatima's petition is the primary target.

*Combat: 2 opponents, AC 12, HP 22 each.*

*The Plaint Fragment and the attached petition are both intact after the fight.* You search the men: a letter from the agent authorizing the interception. The letter calls the petition a "document prejudicial to administrative record." It does not name the archive.

*TOKEN check Act IV — both documents intact; agent's authorization letter recovered.*

---

**ACT V — "The First Filing Date" (Weimar)**

Archivus Sweelinck reads the folio first, then Fatima's petition. He is quiet for a long time.

"The question was asked at Kuru's court in the time of the Sanskrit kings. It has never been answered in any source I have. It has never been refused on grounds of logic. It has been refused on grounds of the same thing every time."

He sets both documents on the desk.

"She used the exact form. Present tense. Addressed to the assembly. Same sequence of questions: the prior loss, the claim of ownership, the question of standing. Same silence requested, as the form implies the answerer must break." He looks up. "She addressed it to us because no court has been willing to be the first to answer it. We cannot answer it either. But we can be the first institution to give it a filing date."

He writes two entries in the archive ledger:
- *Sanskrit Council Records — Sabha Parva: Unanswered Question. Original refusal date: unknown. Commentary date: uncertain.*
- *Comparative Legal Petitions — Later Use: Fatima bint-Yusuf, Tabriz, 1367. Form derived from Sanskrit precedent. Petition received, filed alongside original.*

"The question was first refused in an assembly that no longer exists. It arrived here still open. This is the first time it has received a filing date."

*TOKEN taken Act V — both documents received under new dual classification.*

### Theme

**"The question that was refused without being answered survives as legal form; Draupadi asked before the elders and they were silent, and that silence became the shape of the question — which other women used to ask it again before courts that had seen the form before and could not refuse it without acknowledging the original refusal; the archive is the first institution to give the question a filing date."**
