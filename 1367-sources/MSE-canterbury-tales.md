# MSE — The Canterbury Tales
*Source: from memory. File confirmed correct (Purves ed., Gutenberg #2383, 1.7 MB) but exceeds the 400 KB read-directly gate. Geoffrey Chaucer, c. 1387–1400.*

---

## Four Sentences

The Canterbury Tales is the most complete portrait of medieval English society ever assembled in a single work — thirty pilgrims of every rank, from a Knight who has fought in every Christian crusade to a Pardoner who sells pig bones as saints' relics, gathered at the Tabard Inn in Southwark and told to entertain each other on the road to Canterbury by telling stories, two each way, with the best tale winning a free supper. Chaucer died before finishing: he completed roughly twenty-four tales and left others unfinished or unassigned, which means the Canterbury Tales is not a finished monument but a living argument about what stories are for, interrupted mid-sentence. The tales cover every genre the medieval world knew — courtly romance, fabliau, saint's life, beast fable, moral allegory, tragedy, parody — and Chaucer uses the contrast between tellers and tales to expose the gap between what people claim to be and what they are: the Pardoner is the most brilliant preacher in the book and the most corrupt man on the road. What makes it immortal is not any single tale but the frame — the pilgrims who interrupt, argue, get drunk, challenge each other, and reveal themselves in the act of telling other people's stories, a human comedy conducted entirely at walking pace on a muddy road in 1387.

## Long Summary — Heroes, Decisions, Romance, Human Condition

### The Frame and the Pilgrims

The Host, Harry Bailey of the Tabard Inn, proposes the storytelling contest and serves as judge — cheerful, bossy, occasionally cruel, usually right. The pilgrims he shepherds are a cross-section of every world available to a fourteenth-century Englishman.

**The Knight** is genuinely noble — he has fought at Alexandria, Prussia, Lithuania, and a dozen other campaigns, and his clothes are still stained from his coat of mail because he came straight from overseas without time to change. He is modest in a way that suggests he has nothing to prove. His tale is the most elevated in the collection.

**The Prioress** speaks French with an English accent and pretends to courtly refinement — she feeds her lapdogs roast meat and white bread while beggars eat scraps. Her tale is a child-martyr story of such sweet sentimentality that its antisemitism arrives like a blow inside something soft.

**The Monk** has abandoned his monastery for hunting and fine eating: he has horses, greyhounds, bridles ringing as clear as chapel bells, and a roasted swan for his favorite dish. He is not embarrassed about any of this.

**The Friar** is the great corrupter — he sells absolution for cash and knows every tavern-keeper and barmaid in his territory better than the sick and the poor he is supposed to serve. He is also charming, musically gifted, and the best beggar Chaucer ever drew.

**The Merchant** conceals his debts behind a prosperous manner and talks endlessly about the profitability of the sea-lanes. His tale is the bitterest marriage story in the collection.

**The Oxford Clerk** has spent all his money on books and is thin as a rail. He thanks Chaucer formally for asking him to speak and then tells the Griselda story — the most devastating tale of obedience in the tradition.

**The Sergeant at Law** knows every case and statute since the Conquest, or appears to. He is always busy, or appears to be.

**The Franklin** is an epicure of immense good cheer — his table is permanently set with wine and food, he changes his cook when the food is bad, he believes happiness is wealth's highest purpose, and his tale is the most romantic resolution in the book.

**The Wife of Bath** — Alisoun of Bath — is gap-toothed, red-faced, bold, five times married, and the most living character in the book. Her Prologue is longer than most of the tales: she explains her marriages (the first three husbands were good, rich, and old; the fourth she made jealous to punish him; the fifth she loved and hated and he beat her and she boxed his ear and he lost his hearing and they were even). Her Tale is a question: what do women most desire? The answer is sovereignty over their husbands. She knows this because she has always had it, or taken it.

**The Parson** is the one genuinely virtuous man on the pilgrimage — poor, learned, patient, unwilling to excommunicate anyone for tithes unpaid, unlike the corrupt clerics around him. He walks while his parishioners ride. Chaucer gives him a long prose sermon at the end of everything, which most readers skip, which tells you something about what virtue earns.

**The Miller** is drunk before they leave Southwark. He insists on telling his tale second, after the Knight, which breaks protocol. His tale is a revenge on carpenters (he hates the Reeve, who is a carpenter's son), and it contains one of the most structurally perfect dirty jokes in English literature.

**The Pardoner** sells fake relics — a pillowcase he calls the Virgin's veil, a pig's bone he calls a saint's bone that cures sick animals, a piece of cloth that works as a pregnancy test. He knows he is a fraud; he says so openly before his tale; he preaches against greed because it is what he does best; and his tale about the three rioters who go looking for Death is the finest short story in the collection. He then tries to sell his relics to the pilgrims immediately after, and the Knight has to separate him from the Host.

**The Canon's Yeoman** joins the pilgrimage in progress, having left his master (a practicing alchemist) behind on the road. He immediately begins exposing the tricks of the trade — how alchemists bilk their clients — in a flood of bitter technical detail. His master disappears, which the Yeoman seems to have planned.

### The Knight's Tale — Palamon, Arcite, and Emily

Two Theban knights, Palamon and Arcite, are captured in battle by Theseus and imprisoned in a tower in Athens. From the tower they see Emily — Theseus's sister-in-law — walking in the garden below, and both fall instantly in love. They argue about who loves her more correctly (Palamon says he saw her first as a goddess; Arcite says he loves her as a woman, which is more honest). Arcite is eventually released through a friend's intervention; Palamon escapes years later. They meet by accident in a grove outside Athens, about to fight to the death over a woman neither can approach, when Theseus finds them.

Theseus organizes a tournament: Palamon and Arcite each gather a hundred knights; winner takes Emily. Each prays to a different deity — Arcite to Mars for victory, Palamon to Venus for Emily, Emily to Diana to remain a maiden. All three prayers are granted through competing divine interference. Arcite wins the tournament. As he rides his victory circuit, his horse stumbles and falls on him. He dies. Palamon marries Emily.

Chaucer through the Knight raises the question that runs under the whole tale: what does it mean that Arcite won the tournament and lost everything? The gods have no ethics; fate is not just; the man who prayed for victory got it and died. The tale ends with Theseus's philosophical speech about the First Mover and the necessity of making a virtue of necessity — which is the most Chaucer will give you by way of consolation.

### The Miller's Tale — Nicholas, Alison, Absolon, John

Nicholas, an Oxford student, boards with a carpenter named John and immediately begins seducing John's young wife Alison. He gets her, easily. The problem is Absolon, a parish clerk who also loves Alison and comes to serenade her at her window every night. She tells him to go away. One night, in the dark, she sticks her bare bottom out the window for him to kiss, which he does before he understands what he is kissing. He returns with a red-hot coulter. He asks for another kiss. Nicholas, who has taken Alison's place at the window for the joke, gets branded. He screams "Water!" — which is the signal John has been waiting for, having been told by Nicholas that a second Noah's Flood is coming and he should sleep in a tub hanging from the rafters ready to cut the rope. John cuts the rope, falls, breaks his arm, and the whole neighborhood comes running. The tale ends in total chaos, everyone's humiliation complete.

### The Wife of Bath's Prologue and Tale

The Prologue is autobiography: Alisoun explains her five marriages with the confidence of a woman who has thought about this more than anyone alive. "Experience, though no authority / Were in this world, is right enough for me." Her fifth husband Jankyn read misogynist literature at her from a great book of wicked wives every evening until she tore three pages out and punched him in the face. He hit her so hard she lost hearing in one ear. She fell to the floor. He wept and burned the book. After that they were happy.

The Tale: a knight in Arthur's court rapes a maiden and is sentenced to death unless he can discover in one year what women most desire. He fails everywhere. On the final day, an ugly old woman tells him the answer — sovereignty — on the condition that he marry her. He does. In their wedding bed she gives him a choice: old and ugly and faithful, or young and beautiful and unfaithful. He tells her she can choose. She becomes young, beautiful, and faithful. The answer is the answer to her own question applied to him.

### The Pardoner's Tale — Three Rioters and Death

Three rioters in a tavern, drunk, hear a funeral bell and learn a man has been killed by Death. They vow to find Death and kill him. An old man on the road tells them they will find Death under an oak tree on a hill. They find eight bushels of gold coins instead. They send the youngest to town for food and wine; the two remaining plan to kill him when he returns and split the gold two ways. The youngest buys poison and puts it in two of the three bottles; the two older men kill him when he returns and drink the poisoned wine to celebrate. All three die. Death was under the oak tree all along. The Pardoner's moral: *Radix malorum est cupiditas* — greed is the root of all evil. He then tries to sell his relics to the people he has just preached at.

### The Franklin's Tale — Dorigen and Arveragus

Dorigen marries Arveragus on a mutual agreement of sovereignty — she agrees to obey him openly, he agrees never to exercise authority over her in private. He goes to England to win honor. She waits in Brittany, terrified of the black rocks on the coast that might wreck his ship returning. A young squire named Aurelius tells her he loves her. She tells him: remove every rock from the coast of Brittany and I will be yours. He takes this as a promise. He finds a magician who creates the illusion that the rocks have disappeared. He returns to Dorigen and claims his right. Dorigen, in despair, tells her husband. Arveragus — the most surprising character in the book — tells her she must keep her word, though it destroys him. She goes to Aurelius. Aurelius, seeing her grief and her husband's extraordinary generosity, releases her from the promise. The magician, seeing Aurelius's generosity, releases him from the debt. The Franklin poses a question: which of the three was most generous? He does not answer.

### The Clerk's Tale — Patient Griselda

Walter, a Marquess, marries a peasant woman named Griselda on the condition that she obeys him in all things, no matter what he commands. She agrees. He takes their daughter away, telling her the child has been killed. She accepts this. He takes their son. She accepts this too. He then tells her he is divorcing her to marry a younger woman and sends her back to her father in her shift. She prepares the bridal chamber for her replacement. The replacement is her own daughter. Walter reveals that he has tested her all along and gives everything back. The Clerk presents this as a moral tale of obedience. He then adds, with bitter irony, that he is not recommending actual women behave this way, since Griselda is dead and no women like her remain. The tale is a horror story wearing the costume of a saint's life, and Chaucer knows it.

### The Nun's Priest's Tale — Chanticleer and the Fox

Chanticleer the rooster has a dream that a fox will seize him. His favorite wife Pertelote tells him his dream means nothing and prescribes laxatives. He quotes six authorities in Latin to prove she is wrong. Nevertheless, a fox appears in the yard, flatters Chanticleer's singing, and catches him by the throat when he stretches his neck to crow with his eyes shut. The farm wife gives chase. Chanticleer talks the fox into opening his mouth to address the pursuers. He escapes into a tree. The Nun's Priest loads the tale with mock-epic machinery — divine foreknowledge, free will, the Fall, the destruction of Troy — and ends with a moral: be wary of flattery, keep your mouth shut at the right moment, do not sing with your eyes closed. The rooster and the fox have both learned this in the same five minutes.

### The Human Condition

The Canterbury Tales is not a unified vision. It is a collection of visions, competing and contradicting. The Knight says fate is arbitrary and dignity consists of making peace with it. The Pardoner says greed is the root of all evil, while demonstrating it. The Wife of Bath says authority is a weapon and experience is the only truth. The Miller says the world is a bedroom farce and everyone who thinks otherwise is a fool with a broken arm. The Parson says none of this matters except your soul.

Chaucer does not adjudicate. He gives everyone a tale and an audience and a road, and lets them all be right at once. The pilgrimage is the human condition: you go with strangers toward something holy, you tell stories along the way, and the stories reveal what you cannot say directly.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Tournament of Athens** — Escort Emily from Diana's temple to the tournament stands while Palamon's and Arcite's factions press through the crowd. The garland she has woven at the altar of Diana must arrive intact — it will pass from her hands to the victor's, and then to a funeral pyre.

2. **Find Death Before Sundown** — Three drunk rioters have set out to kill Death. Escort them up the hill to the oak tree before they kill each other on the way. You carry the old man's walking staff. Everything at the oak tree is already arranged.

3. **The Rocks of Brittany** — Aurelius's magician has created the illusion that the rocks off the coast have vanished. Escort Dorigen to Aurelius before she can change her mind, or intercept her and let the illusion lapse. A timed moral choice where doing nothing is also a decision.

4. **The Answer to the Question** — The knight condemned for rape has one year to find what women most desire. Escort him through the kingdom on his search — a series of failed answers — until the old woman on the road offers the true one at the price of marriage.

5. **Chanticleer's Yard** — A fox is in the yard. Chanticleer has been warned in a dream. His wife thinks the dream means nothing. Guard the perimeter while the household argues about free will. A perception-and-investigation quest where the threat is real but no one believes you.

6. **The Pardoner's Relics** — A sick lord has purchased a relic from the Pardoner for a great sum. The relic is a pig bone. The lord has paid in advance and the Pardoner has already spent the money. Escort the Pardoner back to the lord's house before the lord's physician exposes the fraud — a Deception escort quest where the token is false.

7. **Griselda's Journey** — Escort Griselda from Walter's palace back to her father's house in her shift, carrying nothing — not even her name as a lady. Then escort her back again when Walter recalls her. A dignity-and-silence quest where the token is everything she does not say.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: *Emily's Garland*

*Storyline 1 — Escort Quest. Drawn from the Knight's Tale. Emily must be escorted from the temple of Diana to the tournament stands, and her garland of flowers — woven at the altar as a prayer to remain a maiden — must travel with her through the five acts. The TOKEN ends on a funeral pyre.*

**THE TOKEN: EMILY'S GARLAND** — a wreath of white and green woven by Emily herself at Diana's altar at dawn, each flower chosen as an offering to the goddess of maidenhood. She carries it out of the temple on both hands, the way you carry something you are not sure you have the right to hold. The Fighter's charge is simple: get Emily and the garland to the tournament stands before the gates close. What happens after the gates close is not the Fighter's to control.

---

### ACT I — *The Temple of Diana*
*The garland: freshly woven, white and green, in Emily's hands. Location: Diana's temple, Athens, before dawn.*

The temple is cold and smells of pine and old ash. Emily has been here since before first light, weaving her garland at the altar-fire, praying aloud to Diana — not to win the tournament, not for either warrior, but to remain as she is: unmarried, free, belonging to no man's household. The prayer is specific and heartfelt and will not be answered in the way she intends.

She knows this. Her hands do not shake.

The fires on Diana's altar gutter as she finishes — a bad sign, though the priests say nothing. She lifts the garland and turns to find the Fighter waiting at the door.

**Emily:** "Is it time?"

**Fighter:** — (nods)

**Emily:** "Then I am ready. Though I am not."

She walks toward the door carrying the garland in both hands, and the dawn outside is the color of the altar-smoke.

*The Fighter thinks: she does not want either of them. She has asked the goddess to prevent the whole thing. I do not think the goddess was listening.*

---

### ACT II — *The Road to the Lists*
*The garland: carried by Emily, petals already beginning to close in the morning cool. Location: the road to the tournament ground, Athens.*

The city is awake and urgent. Palamon's faction is blue; Arcite's is red; and both are moving through the same streets toward the same gates. The crowd presses in from every direction, and at the narrowest point — the bridge over the Nile-gate canal — two groups of partisans have stopped moving to shout at each other across an increasingly small gap.

Emily sees the blockage and her grip on the garland tightens. The petals are pressing against each other.

**Emily,** quietly: "Find another way."

The Fighter takes her arm and turns them down a side street — longer, rougher-cobbled — but clear. They move at pace. Behind them, at the bridge, someone throws a punch and someone else blows a horn.

At the tournament gates, a steward checks credentials. He looks at the garland. He looks at the Fighter.

**Steward:** "What is she carrying?"

**Emily:** "A prayer answered at Diana's altar. It is mine."

He waves them through.

*The Fighter thinks: the garland is already wilting at the edges. The day will be long.*

---

### ACT III — *The Tournament*
*The garland: held in Emily's lap in the stands, half-wilted, watching. Location: the tournament lists, Athens, midday.*

Palamon's hundred and Arcite's hundred fight. It is not clean or beautiful — it is a hundred men working to disable a hundred other men with every weapon they are allowed, and the stands shake with the crowd's noise. Emily watches without expression.

Arcite wins. He pulls off his helmet in his victory circuit, his face open with joy and relief, and he looks up at Emily in the stands — the look of a man who has finished a very long journey. Emily stands. She holds out the garland.

He rides toward her to receive it.

His horse stumbles on something in the churned earth. One leg folds. The horse goes over. Arcite goes with it and the horse comes down on his chest.

The crowd goes silent the way a crowd goes silent when it has seen something that cannot be unsaid.

Emily sits down. The garland is in her lap.

*The Fighter thinks: he was looking at her. He did not see the ground.*

---

### ACT IV — *The Deathbed*
*The garland: held by Emily at Arcite's side, her hands around it. Location: a chamber in Theseus's palace.*

Arcite's chest is crushed. The physicians can do nothing that matters. He breathes in the particular way of men who are deciding whether to continue. Palamon has been brought in and stands at the far wall, unable to move, unable to leave.

Emily kneels beside the bed. The garland is still in her hands — she has carried it through the whole day without thinking about it, and now she is holding it as the only thing left to hold.

Arcite turns his head toward her.

**Arcite:** "What is hurt in love is not worth grief... I have had more than most men have..." He reaches toward the garland. She lets him touch it. "Let Palamon take your hand, Emily. He has loved you longest. He will love you well."

She does not answer. She takes his hand as it falls and holds it.

Palamon, across the room, looks at the Fighter and looks away.

*The Fighter thinks: he won the tournament. He asked Mars for victory and Mars gave it. This is what victory looks like at the end of the day.*

---

### ACT V — *The Pyre*
*The garland: burned. Location: the funeral pyre on the plains outside Athens.*

Arcite's pyre is built of oak, ash, and fir. It is sixty feet high. Theseus has laid on it everything a warrior could need in whatever comes next. The garland — dried now, brown at the edges — has sat in Emily's hands through the days of mourning.

When the fire is lit, she waits until the pyre is properly burning. Then she steps forward and places the garland on the lowest step of the wood and steps back.

It catches in seconds. White smoke.

Palamon stands beside her. He does not take her hand — not yet. Theseus will speak later about necessity and sorrow and what a man must do when grief has done its work. Emily will listen. Palamon will listen. Eventually there will be a wedding, conducted with appropriate solemnity.

But right now there is only the fire, and the white smoke, and Diana's garland returning to air.

**Emily,** to no one: "I asked to be kept as I was. The goddess did not answer."

**Palamon,** very quietly: "She answered Arcite."

Neither of them speaks again until the pyre has burned down.

*The Fighter thinks: I carried a prayer from a cold temple to a burning pyre and it was answered wrong every step. That is not my fault and it is not a comfort.*

## Cycle 2: Find Death Before Sundown

### Pass 1 — Plot Précis (Find Death Before Sundown)

Three rioters in a plague-summer Flemish tavern hear the funeral knell for their friend and decide, with complete sincerity and a great deal of wine, to go find Death and kill it. An old man on the road outside — ancient, wrapped against the summer heat in every cloth he owns, carrying a white staff — tells them Death sits under an oak tree down the road. Under the oak: eight bushels of gold coin. The three forget about Death immediately. The youngest goes to town for food and wine; plans to poison the others and keep the gold alone. The other two plan to kill him when he returns. He returns; they kill him; they drink the wine; they die. The old man in the tale had been knocking on the earth with his staff asking to be let in. He could not find anyone willing to exchange youth for age. He has been standing at road junctions pointing strangers toward Death because he cannot find it himself.

### Pass 2 — Fighter's Role (Find Death Before Sundown)

The Fighter arrives as the three rioters set out. The old man at the road junction gives the Fighter his walking staff with a single instruction: plant it at the oak when the work is done. He does not explain what "the work" is. The Fighter follows the rioters — carrying the staff, not the rioters' purpose — as the escort of a transaction that does not need protection, only a witness who will complete the final gesture.

### Pass 3 — What This Angle Reveals That Cycle 1 Missed

Cycle 1 was fate working through beauty — a garland burned on the pyre of a man who won everything and lost it in a single afternoon, the gods answering every prayer and ruining everyone anyway. *Find Death Before Sundown* reveals the darker comedy: these men do not need to go looking for Death. It was in the tavern in the form of their friend's corpse. The gold under the oak is Death, specifically and materially — it does not wait for them in disguise, it is exactly what it looks like and they know exactly what it does to people and they choose it anyway. What cycle 1 missed: the old man. He has been trying to die for a very long time and the world will not cooperate. He cannot find Death even when he goes looking for it directly. He can only point other people toward it. Carrying his staff is carrying his failed errand — brought to completion at last by three men who thought they were doing something else entirely.

Active threads do not include: *the errand that must be completed by a substitute; the object that marks a transaction already in motion; the witness who carries the marker to its placement without becoming a participant in what the marker records.* The old man's commission is the only one in the collection that has no urgent party — no living person who cares whether the staff gets planted. The Fighter does it because the old man asked. That is sufficient. New thread proposed: **The proxy errand; the commission given by someone who cannot complete it themselves; the marker planted not to celebrate what happened but to record that the world ran its course and someone was present for it.**

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *Find Death Before Sundown*

*Storyline 2 — Vigil and Placement. Three rioters have gone to find Death. They will find it. TOKEN: the Old Man's Walking Staff — received from the old man at the road junction Act I, carried through Acts II–IV, planted at the oak Act V.*

**THE TOKEN: THE OLD MAN'S WALKING STAFF** — a staff of white wood, worn smooth from years of use, carrying the specific weight of something that has been leaned on for a very long time. The old man gives it to the Fighter without explanation. He sits down against the milestone as if he has been standing since before the Fighter was born. In Act V the staff is planted in the earth at the oak's roots beside the three dead men and left there. The old man's errand is complete.

---

#### ACT I — *The Road Junction*
*The staff: in the old man's hands, almost his. Location: TVO — tavern outskirts, Flemish plague-summer road.*

The three rioters are already outside the tavern, armed and loud. The funeral bell of their friend is still in the air. They are swearing to each other — genuine oaths, not tavern boasting — that they will go find Death and kill it before the sun goes down, because their friend is dead and someone ought to answer for it.

The old man is at the road junction. Ancient, bundled against summer heat in every cloth he owns, his face the face of someone who has been at this junction long enough to stop being surprised by what comes down either road. He watches the three rioters with the stillness of deep familiarity.

He looks at the Fighter. He holds out the staff.

**Old Man:** "You are going the same direction. Take this to the oak and plant it when the work is done."

He does not say whose work.

He sits down against the milestone as if this is a relief. He looks at his empty hands for a moment with an expression that is hard to read.

*The Fighter thinks: he has been carrying this a long time. He is glad to put it down.*

*Insight check DC 12: understand what he is giving you — not a weapon; not a sign of authority; a marker for a transaction already running, which needs only a witness who will complete the final gesture.*

**Failure:** You take the staff with the bearing of someone prepared to use it. The old man says, quietly: "Not like that. You are not going to need it for that." You adjust your grip, but you have already shown him that you heard it wrong. The three rioters move down the road and you follow with the staff held like something you are learning the weight of.

**Success:** You receive the staff in both hands, the way you receive something given to be kept. The old man's face settles. He closes his eyes. You turn and follow the three rioters down the plague road with the staff across your back, and the junction is empty behind you before you have gone twenty steps.

---

#### ACT II — *The Road*
*The staff: carried across the back. Location: TVO — road between the tavern and the oak.*

The three rioters take the road at a half-run, shouting. One of them falls back — the middle one, who swore the loudest at the tavern and is now walking at the same speed as walking-away-from-a-mistake. He falls in beside the Fighter.

**Rioter:** "You'll swear brotherhood with us, won't you? Four is better than three. Death won't fight four."

He means it. He is also afraid. He wants one more body between himself and what they are walking toward.

*The Fighter thinks: if I swear, I become the fourth rioter. The old man's commission requires that I remain something else.*

*Persuasion check DC 12: decline the oath without breaking his courage — find the form that keeps you walking beside him without binding you to what follows.*

**Failure:** You swear. He grips your arm. The three become four, which means you have stepped out of the witness role and into the group that is going to find the gold and do what the gold requires of people who find it. The staff is still in your hand but the commission it represents has been complicated by the oath.

**Success:** "I will walk with you. I cannot swear brotherhood — I have already sworn something else. But I will walk to the oak." He looks at you for a moment — deciding whether this is enough — and then nods and picks up his pace. He is satisfied. You are still what the old man needed you to be.

---

#### ACT III — *The Oak*
*The staff: still across the Fighter's back. Location: OKD — the oak at the crossroads.*

The oak is old and wide, and under its roots there is gold. Eight bushels of it. It catches the afternoon light in a way that seems specific — aimed — as if the light has been arranged.

The three rioters stop talking. They stop walking. They look at the gold with the expression of men whose purpose has just been completely replaced by a different purpose, in the space of one breath.

The youngest speaks first: "We could keep this. If someone went to town for food and wine, we could stay through the night and take it home by cart in the morning." He says this looking at the oldest, who is already counting, and not at the middle one, who has not yet made his decision. His voice is reasonable. His eyes are already at the next step.

*The Fighter thinks: the youngest knows exactly what he is going to buy in town alongside the wine.*

*Perception check DC 13: read the calculation running behind the youngest's eyes in the first three seconds — the shape of what is already decided inside him, before he finishes saying the word "wine."*

**Failure:** You see a man proposing logistics. You do not see what the logistics contain. The youngest goes to town with the ordinary manner of someone doing an errand, and you do not yet know what the errand is for.

**Success:** You see it: the extra purchase, the two bottles, the specific counting of what he will come back with and what one bottle will do. You understand the shape of what is about to happen. You cannot change it. You hold the staff correctly for what comes next.

---

#### ACT IV — *The Wait*
*The staff: in the Fighter's hand. Location: OKD.*

The youngest has gone to town. The two remaining rioters look at the gold. Then they look at each other. Then they look at the Fighter.

The oldest says: "You were hired for escort. The escort is done. But you've seen the gold. And we can't have someone walking back to town talking about eight bushels of gold under an oak tree."

They are not cruel men. They are men who have found something that makes reason adjacent to the point.

*Combat: Two Rioters, AC 13 HP 22 ×2. The staff is in one hand; the weapon is in the other. The oak is at the Fighter's back.*

**Failure:** They take the staff from you in the scuffle. They are not thorough — they leave the Fighter alive and at the field's edge — but the staff is with them when the youngest returns, and what the youngest returns with changes who holds what very quickly thereafter.

**Success:** The two rioters discover that carrying a dead man's commission makes you harder to move than it should. They fall back. They return to counting the gold. The youngest will be back before evening.

---

#### ACT V — *The Planting*
*The staff: still in the Fighter's hand. Location: OKD — the oak, late afternoon.*

The youngest returns with bread, meat, and two bottles — one clean, one poisoned, in the manner of a man who has decided his arithmetic and purchased accordingly. He finds the other two dead. He sets down the food. He opens the clean bottle and drinks to steady his hands. Then he opens the second bottle because it is there, and because his hands are still shaking.

He sits against the oak root with the second bottle and looks at the Fighter with the expression of someone who has understood something too late to be useful.

**Youngest:** "Did they find what they were looking for?"

He is asking an honest question. He has a few minutes left.

The staff is in the Fighter's hand. The earth at the oak's roots is soft.

*Composure check DC 12: plant the staff and walk away from eight bushels of gold without touching it.*

**Failure:** You take one coin. It is impossible not to — you have been standing over it for two hours and the old man asked you to plant a stick and then leave, which is a strange commission, and one coin is nothing against eight bushels. You plant the staff. You pocket the coin. The old man's work is done but you are carrying something that does not belong to you. The coin is heavier than it looks, the rest of the walk.

**Success:** You plant the staff in the earth at the oak's roots — between the three bodies and the gold — and you leave. The road behind you fills with the sound of nothing in particular. The oak stands with the staff planted at its base and the three men arranged in the shapes their deaths took them and the gold catching the last light. The old man sent something to the oak that he could not carry himself, and it arrived, and the work is done.

*The Fighter thinks: he has been sitting at that road junction waiting for someone willing to take the errand to the end. I do not know how long he has been sitting there. I do not know how many times he has tried. The staff is planted. I think that is what he was waiting to hear.*
