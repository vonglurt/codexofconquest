# VIE — Faust: Der Tragödie erster Teil
**Johann Wolfgang von Goethe | 1808 (Part 1) | Philosophical Drama | 218 KB — read from file**

---

## Three-Pass Summary

### Pass 1 — Full Spoiler Telling with Scene Notes

**Dedication, Prelude, Prologue in Heaven**
The text opens in layers of frame. A dedication mourns absent friends. A theatrical prelude argues between Director (give the crowd spectacle), Poet (give art permanence), and Clown (give them pleasure now). Then the Prologue in Heaven: God and Mephistopheles make a wager over Faust. God says Faust serves him confusedly but will be led to clarity. Mephisto bets he can pull Faust off the right path entirely. God permits it. "A good man, in his darkest striving, is aware of the right way." This wager frames the entire play: the outcome has been established before the action begins.

**Night — Faust's Study**
Faust, a doctor and professor, sits in his vaulted Gothic study at midnight in total despair. He has mastered every academic discipline — philosophy, law, medicine, theology — and found all of it empty. He knows nothing, he can teach nothing, he has nothing. He turns to magic. He opens the book of Nostradamus. He summons the Earth Spirit (Erdgeist), which appears in red flame. Then recoils from him: "You are like the spirit you comprehend, not me." His pedantic assistant Wagner interrupts, enthusiastic about academic learning. Faust considers suicide. Easter bells ring; the sound of childhood memory stops him.

**Before the Gate**
Faust and Wagner walk among holiday crowds outside the city. People are enjoying the spring. A black poodle follows them home.

**Study Room — The Pact**
The poodle transforms into Mephistopheles, who introduces himself: "Part of that force which always wills evil and always creates good... the spirit that always denies." He is charming, perceptive, and bored. He tries to leave but is trapped by a pentagram Faust drew at the door. He escapes by summoning spirits to sing Faust to sleep. He returns properly dressed on a second visit. Faust, unable to satisfy his hunger for absolute experience through magic alone, agrees to the pact: if Mephistopheles can ever cause him to say "Verweile doch! du bist so schön!" (Stay a while, you are so beautiful!) — if Faust can ever rest in a single moment — Mephisto takes his soul. Until then, Mephisto serves him.

**Auerbach's Cellar**
Mephisto shows Faust tavern drinkers amusing themselves. Faust is not interested in their pleasures.

**Witch's Kitchen**
Mephisto arranges a youth-potion for Faust from a witch's cauldron. Faust glimpses a beautiful woman in a magic mirror. He demands to know who she is. Mephisto: the potion will soon make every woman look that beautiful. But Faust wants specifically her.

**Street — Faust Meets Gretchen**
Faust meets Margarete, called Gretchen, outside a church. She is 14 or 15, modest, devout, entirely without guile. He tries to speak to her. She refuses politely: she is neither noble nor beautiful enough to require an escort. He is immediately obsessed. He demands Mephisto arrange a meeting. Mephisto says he has no power over her — she is clean of any sin he could exploit. Faust insists.

**Evening — Gretchen's Room**
Mephisto brings Faust into Gretchen's empty room. Faust is genuinely moved by its simplicity and orderliness: everything in it speaks of a life lived without decoration or pretense. Mephisto leaves a jewel box hidden in the wardrobe.

**Stroll**
Gretchen's mother confiscates the jewels, gives them to the church. Mephisto arranges a second box through the neighbor Martha Schwerdtlein.

**The Neighbor's House**
Martha receives the jewels; she wants a death certificate for her husband who disappeared years ago. Mephisto will provide false testimony if Faust will lie with him about Martha's husband dying abroad.

**Street II / Garden**
The double meeting is arranged. Faust and Gretchen walk in Martha's garden. Gretchen tells him about her family — her mother's strictness, her dead sister, her simple life. Faust is genuinely happy, genuinely present, almost stopped in time. Gretchen asks if he believes in God. He gives a pantheistic evasion: "Who can name it? Who can say 'I believe'?" She is confused and unconvinced. The scene is the closest Faust comes to the thing he's supposed to want: rest in beauty. He doesn't say the words.

**Garden Pavilion**
A stolen kiss. Gretchen: "You are such a good man." Mephisto appears, makes time pressure known, hurries them apart.

**Forest and Cave**
Faust alone, in a soliloquy given by the Earth Spirit, reflects: nature has been given him as companion and mystery, but Mephistopheles pulls him always back to Gretchen. He cannot resist.

**Gretchen's Room**
Gretchen alone at her spinning wheel, singing: "Meine Ruh ist hin, / Mein Herz ist schwer" (My peace is gone, / My heart is heavy). She is in love and already ruined by it — not yet physically, but the psychological destruction has begun. She cannot stop thinking of him. She already knows something is wrong.

**Martha's Garden — The Religious Conversation**
Gretchen asks Faust directly: does he believe in religion? She would not give herself to a man who doesn't. He evades beautifully but she senses the evasion. He gives her a sleeping potion — she is to administer it to her mother so they can be together at night without her mother waking. The potion is from Mephisto. The dosage is unclear.

**At the Well**
Gretchen hears her friend Lieschen mocking another girl who has become pregnant and is ruined. Gretchen recognizes that she is now the same thing Lieschen is mocking. The shame descends.

**City Wall Niche**
Gretchen prays to the Mater Dolorosa: "Ah, incline, / O Thou most Sorrowful, / Thy face in mercy toward my need." The prayer is the most beautiful moment of pure faith in the play.

**Street Before Gretchen's Door — Valentin**
Gretchen's brother Valentin, a soldier, has learned of her dishonor and waits outside the house to fight Faust. He and Faust duel. Mephisto paralyzes Valentin's sword arm; Faust wounds him fatally. Dying in the street before a gathering crowd, Valentin curses Gretchen by name. He publicly destroys what remained of her reputation before he dies.

**Cathedral**
Gretchen at mass. An evil spirit whispers her guilt in her ear — her mother dead from the sleeping potion, her brother dead from the duel, her body no longer her own. The choir sings Day of Wrath. She faints.

**Walpurgis Night**
Mephisto takes Faust to the witches' sabbath on the Brocken. Faust dances. Mid-celebration he has a vision: Gretchen, pale, moving slowly, with a thin red line around her neck like a mark from an axe. Mephisto distracts him with a beautiful woman who turns out to have a mouse in her mouth.

**Overcast Day, Field (Prose Scene)**
Faust discovers what has happened. Gretchen's mother died of the sleeping potion — too strong a dose administered too long. Gretchen became pregnant. She has drowned her infant. She is in prison awaiting execution. Faust explodes at Mephisto: this misery of one human being — wrought by their actions — and Mephisto is unmoved. Mephisto points out quietly: "Who plunged her into ruin? I or you?" Faust demands to rescue her. Mephisto agrees: the horses are ready.

**Night, Open Field**
Faust and Mephisto ride to the city. Ravens fly overhead.

**Dungeon**
Mephisto has stolen the prison keys. Faust enters alone. Gretchen is in a cell, singing a fragment of a fairy tale about a child put to death by her mother. She takes the entering figure for her executioner. Then she recognizes Faust. She calls his name. She wants to hold him. She is tender and frightened and not entirely coherent.

Faust begs her to come — he will break her chains, she can flee with him now. She does not. She begins to ask questions about the grave: will it be in a proper place, will her mother and brother be nearby? She asks about their child. She names the dead: the mother (the potion), Valentin (the duel), the baby (the water). She names each one quietly without accusation.

Then she hears Mephisto's voice. She does not hear the word "come"; she hears the entity. She knows immediately who he is. She asks: is that your voice? Where is he? She names Mephisto: the devil, the destroyer. She refuses to flee with a man summoned by that voice: "What is this place? What do you want from me? Do not touch me! Lord, thy judgment comes, and I commit myself to thee."

The last words are addressed to God, not to Faust.

Mephisto: "She is condemned!" (*Verdammt!*)
A voice from above: "She is saved!" (*Gerettet!*)
Mephisto drags Faust into the dark. Faust calls her name. She is heard no more.

### Pass 2 — Character Deep-Dives

**Heinrich Faust**: Not a villain and not a hero — a mind with an appetite nothing real can fill. His despair at the opening is genuine: he has done everything that can be done within human knowledge and found it empty. His pact with Mephistopheles is not greed for wealth or power; it is a philosopher's bet that experience itself will finally satisfy what thought cannot. The tragedy is that the pact requires him never to stop — the moment he says "stay, you are beautiful" he loses — so he is constitutionally unable to let love rest, which means he cannot love without destroying the thing he loves. He is responsible for four deaths: Martha's husband (his perjured testimony), Gretchen's mother (the potion), Valentin (the duel), the infant (his actions leading to Gretchen's isolation). His rage in the prose scene is real. It changes nothing.

**Mephistopheles**: The most interesting devil in European literature because he never lies, never uses powers Faust doesn't ask him to use, and is losing the whole time. He introduces himself as the part of the universe that wills evil and produces good — a genuine paradox he states without irony. He is charming because his perceptions are accurate: he reads Faust perfectly, reads Wagner perfectly, reads Gretchen's nature perfectly (he knows she is outside his power; he cannot seduce someone without a sin-angle). He wins every plot point and loses the wager. God was right.

**Margarete (Gretchen)**: The moral center of the play and the proof of God's wager. She is probably 14-16 years old, devout, orderly, without vanity. She recognizes Mephisto on first meeting as someone who gives her a chill — she has no name for what he is, but her moral instinct knows. She asks the right questions: does Faust believe in God? She doesn't accept the evasion. She is destroyed not by her own moral failure but by her love for a man entangled in something she couldn't have known about. Her drowning of the infant is the act that condemns her legally; the question of what drove her to it is what the play refuses to simplify. Her refusal to flee at the end is the play's one completely clear-eyed act: she cannot live in the world Faust would take her to, she will not accept a rescue that requires her to disappear from what is true about what happened, and she names Mephisto for what he is when everyone else is running. "She is saved" is not a surprise — she has been the proof of God's proposition from the beginning.

**Wagner**: The foil. He loves learning for its own sake and has no appetite for the infinite. He is not stupid; he is satisfied. He represents the life Faust could have lived if he could feel less. He is unaware that this is a gift.

### Pass 3 — Pivotal Scene: The Dungeon

Gretchen has been in the cell long enough to lose her mind and find it again in pieces. She is singing when Faust enters — a fragment of a fairy story about a mother who killed her child. In the dark she takes him for the executioner. The form of address she uses is formal and distancing, as if she is practicing for a stranger.

Then she recognizes his voice.

The scene's tension is that she is simultaneously completely lucid and completely lost. She knows where she is and why. She can name every person she has failed or been failed by. She wants to hold Faust and cannot let go of the need to enumerate the dead. She asks about the grave — where it will be, whether her mother will be close, whether there will be a place for the baby. She is not asking these questions as evidence of madness. She is making arrangements.

Faust tries to cut through this: *come, I will break your chains, come now*. She does not hear this as possible. She does not imagine the chain-breaking. She asks instead: how long has he been up? Has he thought about her? She still loves him. But she will not flee.

The hinge: she hears Mephisto outside. Not his words — his voice. She asks: is that your voice? Where is he? And then: He is there. And she refuses. She names what she is choosing: "Lord, thy judgment — I commit myself to thee." The judgment she has been dreading for the entire play is the thing she turns toward at the end. Submission to it is the only honest option remaining because it is the only thing that does not require her to pretend the dead are not dead or the past did not happen.

Mephisto's "She is condemned" is his final misreading of a situation he has been misreading all along. The voice's "She is saved" is the play's thesis statement delivered in three syllables.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **"Margarete's Account"** — A woman has been condemned for infanticide. Before execution she dictated a sworn account to the prison chaplain — not to claim innocence, but to name the circumstances on the record. The account names the father of the child and the chain of events: the sleeping potion, the isolation, the choices made by others. She is not asking to be freed; she is asking to be heard. The chaplain hires the Fighter to carry the sealed deposition to the city Archivus before the execution, because every previous courier has been turned back by the father's family. The Fighter carries not a rescue but a right to speak.

2. **"The Scholar's Confession"** — A university professor, dying of a seizure, has written a sealed confession naming the guild members who paid him to falsify testimony that condemned an innocent woman. The confession is addressed to the chancellor. The Fighter carries it through the university district against agents of the named guild who want it destroyed before it is read.

3. **"The Cancelled Bond"** — A young woman's family signed a seven-year labor-indenture to a mine lord under duress. An escape clause buried in the fine print requires the original contract to be presented to the city court within thirty days of signing to invoke duress-voidance. The thirty days expire tomorrow. A reforming notary has obtained the original; the Fighter carries it through the mine lord's blocking agents to the court.

4. **"The Voiding Order"** — A new city ordinance voids all life-debt contracts signed under the old guild rules. A guild's legal officers are delaying official delivery of the ordinance until after the annual renewal ceremony, when the debt auto-renews for another year. The Fighter carries the official voiding order from the civic court to the guild master, against legal officers with retainers at every gate.

5. **"The Perjured Record"** — A court clerk who fabricated testimony against a condemned man twenty years ago has written a recantation, notarized and sealed, on his deathbed. The man's surviving children hire the Fighter to carry it from the clerk's house to the Archive of Civic Records, against the interests of the magistrate whose career was built on the original conviction.

6. **"The Wager's Record"** — Two powerful men made a private agreement — the guild would stop dumping into the harbor in exchange for the magistrate's silence about price-fixing. The magistrate had it written and sealed. The guild is now denying it; the harbor is fouled; the magistrate is discredited. His secretary hires the Fighter to carry the original sealed agreement to the city council meeting now in session.

7. **"The Undelivered Release"** — A usurer's will contains a clause releasing all life-debts held at his death; he converted to a religious order and wanted his estate clean. His heir has buried the will in the estate archive and is collecting the debts anyway. The Fighter carries a certified copy of the relevant clause from the notary who wrote the will to the city court.

## 5-Act Vignette Play

*The Fighter moves through a walled German imperial city at dawn. The air smells of lime-wash and old rain. The execution is scheduled for the first bell after midday. It is before dawn.*

---

**ACT 1 — Gretchen's Prison Tower (MGR)**
*The chaplain is waiting in the stone anteroom. He has been awake all night. He is a small man with ink-stained hands and the specific exhaustion of someone who has been carrying someone else's dignity for a long time. He passes the Fighter a linen roll, sealed twice: once with the prisoner's own mark — a pressed thumb in blue wax — and once with his counterseal. Addressed to the Archivus in a careful clerical hand.*

"She dictated it yesterday morning," he says. "She dictated it clearly. She knows what she is asking and what she is not asking. She is not asking to be freed. Read the last line if you doubt me." *He does not wait for the Fighter to ask. He opens his hands. They are shaking.* "The father's family has turned back two couriers already. The second one was beaten at the court gate. I cannot go myself — I am confined to the prison district by the bishop's order. I have a transit warrant from the Dominican prior — it is rolled inside the outer wrapper. Use it if they try to bar you at the Dominican court. Get there before the second bell."

*The Fighter takes the deposition. The seal has a thumb-impression in it. The handwriting on the address is not the chaplain's — it is careful and slow, the writing of someone not used to writing, who wrote it anyway.*

**TOKEN GRANT:** The Prison Deposition — a linen roll containing Margarete's sworn account, dictated to the prison chaplain and sealed with her mark and his counterseal, addressed to the Archivus. The last line reads: *I ask only that this account be opened in my presence, if that is possible, and in my absence if it is not.*

**SKILL CHECK:** Insight DC 12
- *onPass:* The Fighter reads the chaplain's terror accurately — he is afraid for her, not for himself, and the distinction matters: he has no stake in the document's content except her right to have said what she said. The Fighter takes the commission without hesitation.
- *onFail:* The Fighter misreads the anxiety as self-preservation; takes the commission but with an unnecessary suspicion of the chaplain's motives that costs them the warmth of full understanding. They proceed but carry a fraction of the wrong weight.

**ITEM GRANT:** A small roll of sermon candles, tied with cord — the chaplain's payment in kind, the only thing he has to offer. The candles smell of beeswax and old smoke.

---

**ACT 2 — Prison Gate to Dominican Quarter Road**
*The gate from the prison district to the civic street is manned by a notary's clerk in a green coat, posted there before dawn. He has a ledger. He has been told to log all correspondence exiting the prison district this morning. He asks what the Fighter is carrying.*

"Official correspondence addressed to the Archivus," the Fighter says.

The clerk's pen moves. "All court-addressed correspondence from the prison district is subject to verification hold. Standard three days. I'll give you a receipt." *He holds out his hand for the document.*

The execution is before midday. Three days is a polite way of saying never.

**SKILL CHECK:** Persuasion DC 13
- *onPass:* The Fighter invokes the prisoner's right to direct address — a sealed document addressed to the Archivus is official correspondence, and interfering with official correspondence before delivery is obstruction under imperial statute. The clerk, who knows this perfectly well and was hoping the carrier wouldn't, backs down. He writes "logged — passed" and waves them through.
- *onFail:* The clerk demands to see the outer seal before passing. The Fighter must argue that breaking the chaplain's counterseal before delivery voids the document's legal standing — and convince the clerk that the Dominican prior's transit warrant inside the wrapper is genuine without opening the wrapper to show it. The argument succeeds, barely, with a note in the clerk's ledger that will cause problems at the next gate.

**ITEM GRANT:** A folded transit warrant signed by the Dominican prior — it was rolled into the document's outer wrapper by the chaplain, who forgot to mention it. It says, in Latin, that the bearer is acting in the service of a sacramental obligation and should not be impeded. It smells of incense.

---

**ACT 3 — Dominican Legal Court (DKN)**
*The duty clerk at the Dominican court is a young man with the face of someone who has been briefed. He receives the Fighter at the intake desk and reviews the outer address. Then he produces a folded writ from the stack beside him: a restraining order on "unsolicited depositions submitted by prison inmates in cases already adjudicated," filed this morning at first light. The family's seal is on it. The court's heating costs for the winter are mentioned in the court clerk's notation at the bottom.*

The writ is new and of uncertain legality. The Fighter has the Dominican prior's transit warrant in hand.

*From the corridor: the sound of boots. Two men in the father's household livery are coming from the side hall.*

**SKILL CHECK + COMBAT:** Persuasion DC 14 — the Fighter argues that prisoners retain dictation rights under imperial law even in adjudicated cases; a writ filed by an interested party at first light on the morning of an execution is facially invalid and constitutes interference with the record. The clerk would know this; the clerk is being leaned on. If the Fighter names the pressure correctly — speaks to the clerk's own liability if the document is found to have been unlawfully withheld — the clerk stamps a transit receipt and steps back.

*If the persuasion fails, the two retainers arrive:*
- **2× household retainers**, AC 13, HP 18 each, armed with short swords. They are here to physically remove the Fighter from the court, not to kill — but they are not careful about the distinction.

**ITEM GRANT:** A stamped court transit document — the clerk stamps it before the retainers arrive, whether or not he files the deposition then and there. The stamp shows the document was in the court's hands this morning. This will matter.

---

**ACT 4 — Dominican Quarter to Weimar Archive District (WM)**
*The road from the Dominican court to the Archivus runs through the morning market square — carts, vendors, the smell of bread and cabbage, the noise of the day beginning. The Fighter is halfway across when a mounted agent in the father's colors cuts into the square from the east road, heading directly for the archive gate.*

The agent knows where the document is going. He is going to arrive first.

**SKILL CHECK:** Athletics DC 13 — moving through the market square at speed: between the fish carts, past the grain vendors, through the clutch of students arguing about nothing at the archivists' corner door. The mounted agent cannot enter the square at speed; there are too many stalls. But he is going around.

- *onPass:* The Fighter reaches the archive gate before the agent rounds the square. They are inside and at the intake counter when the agent arrives at the gate, one minute too late. He cannot enter without a warrant.
- *onFail:* The Fighter arrives at the gate at the same moment as the agent, who has a prepared writ of pre-emption from the family's city solicitor. The gate-warden is reading it. The Fighter must argue their way past the gate-warden before the writ is accepted — pointing to the Dominican court stamp on their transit document as superseding a civil solicitor's writ. The argument works but costs another quarter-hour.

**ITEM GRANT:** A pressed copper disk — a market vendor's booth-token pressed into the Fighter's hand by an old woman selling salt fish, who stepped a barrel into the path of the agent's horse when he rode too close to her stall. She did not look at the Fighter when she did it. She is back behind her counter before the agent can say anything.

---

**ACT 5 — Weimar Archive District (WM)**
*The Archivus Sweelinck is a grey man with glasses and a precise manner. He receives the Fighter at the main intake counter, examines the transit stamp, reads the outer address, notes the chaplain's counterseal. He says:*

"Standard procedure for sealed depositions in active criminal cases: the document is logged, held for two working days for verification, then opened in the presence of at least two civil witnesses." He says this without looking up. His pen is still.

*The second bell will ring before midday. Two working days is after the execution.*

*The Fighter holds the document. The Archivus is not moving. He has said the standard procedure. He has not moved his pen. He has not called for a junior clerk.*

**SKILL CHECK:** Insight DC 12 — reading what Sweelinck is actually doing. He is not enforcing the procedure; he is citing it. He is waiting for something. There is a document on his desk, half under a blotter, with the family solicitor's seal visible at one corner: a request for voluntary hold, filed this morning. He doesn't want to comply with it. He wants reason not to. If the Fighter names the pressure — the solicitor's request, the family's interest, Sweelinck's own authority to expedite in cases where delay constitutes denial of record — Sweelinck will open the register.

- *onPass:* The Fighter names it directly: the request for hold, the conflict of interest, the woman's own words in the last line of the document — *I ask only that this account be opened in my presence, if that is possible, and in my absence if it is not.* She has already anticipated the delay. She has already written her own permission for the archive to act without her. Sweelinck takes off his glasses. He takes the document. He opens the register. He logs it received before midday. He slides the solicitor's request under the far corner of his desk without looking at it.

  The document is in the permanent record before the sentence is carried out. Margarete's account — her own words, her own mark — is the official version.

- *onFail:* Sweelinck logs the document at the close of business, after the execution. He is apologetic. It is filed correctly and permanently. The account is in the record. But it was filed after, not before. The sentence was carried out first.

**TOKEN TAKE:** The Prison Deposition — filed into the Archivus's permanent collection, sealed with the register's own receipt stamp. The linen roll now belongs to the archive. It will not be destroyed, it will not be suppressed, it will be read by anyone who has standing to request it. The Fighter leaves with an empty hand and a receipt stamp on their transit document.

## Cycle 2 — The Perjured Record

**What cycle 1 missed:** Margarete's Account was about a living person asserting her right to be heard before execution — the document as the only self-representation available in the narrowing window before silence becomes permanent. The Perjured Record reveals what happens when the silence has already lasted twenty years: the court clerk who fabricated testimony is dying and has written a notarized recantation. The man he condemned is likely dead. His children are alive and carry the weight of a false conviction in their names. The recantation must reach the Archive of Civic Records before the magistrate — whose career was built on the original conviction — can suppress it through the same procedural apparatus that made the fabrication possible. Cycle 1 was about the right to speak before execution. Cycle 2 is about the obligation to correct the record even when correction can no longer repair what the record destroyed.

**New angle:** The dying clerk gives the Fighter the sealed recantation at his deathbed. The Fighter carries it through the magistrate's legal officers, past a procedural injunction, and through an enforcer at the archive gate, to the intake desk — where the Archivus opens it and reads the opening line, which names the magistrate.

---

### Pass 1 — Token

**TOKEN: The Clerk's Sealed Recantation** — a notarized document in the clerk's hand, signed by the clerk and a witness-notary, sealed with the notary's mark. It names the guild members who commissioned the false testimony. It names the specific testimony that was fabricated. It names the magistrate who received the fabricated testimony and used it. It is addressed to the Archive of Civic Records. The man it exonerates has been dead for eleven years. His children are in the city.

---

### Pass 2 — Seven Storyline Ideas

**1. Margarete's Account** *(USED — Cycle 1)* — The condemned woman's deposition. The archive before execution. The right to be heard in her own words.

**2. The Scholar's Confession** — The dying professor's sealed confession naming the guild that paid him to fabricate testimony.

**3. The Cancelled Bond** — The original indenture contract. The thirty-day escape clause expiring tomorrow.

**4. The Voiding Order** — The civic ordinance that voids all life-debt contracts. Delivered before the annual renewal.

**5. The Perjured Record** *(CHOSEN — Cycle 2)* — The dying court clerk's sealed recantation, twenty years late. The man he condemned is dead; his children are alive. The Archivus receives it. The magistrate's career sits in the opening line.

**6. The Wager's Record** — The sealed private agreement between the guild and the magistrate. City council meeting now in session.

**7. The Undelivered Release** — The certified copy of the usurer's will clause releasing all life-debts.

---

### Pass 3 — Theme Check

Existing themes through CDG cycle 2: grief in object, loyalty, honor vs. shame, love and fate, truth posthumous, healing through hostile law, carrier hunted, token's journey, ancient witnesses, corruption wears mercy, fate through broken thing, heroism earns nothing, invitation as weapon, poem ransoms life, anonymous sympathy, pyre-arrangements, proxy errand, absent jurisdiction, scattered truth, last gift against design, credential before the man, one who will not weep, vigil at extraction, errand of removal, eclipse witness, thing too true, word dying man cannot carry, question across ocean, carrier mid-commission change, last words to absent father, complete roll of dead, warning that races the threat.

**Themes I am avoiding:** "Truth delivered posthumously" (LGW) — the clerk is alive (barely) when he writes it; the document travels in the window before his death, not after. "The condemned who refuses rescue still has the right to be heard" (VIE cycle 1) — that was about a living person's right; this is about a dead person's right to have the lie against their name corrected after the fact.

**New theme introduced by VIE cycle 2:** *The recantation that arrives twenty years too late: the correction to the archive that cannot un-condemn the condemned; the document that makes the record honest without making the life better; the Archivus's intake as the only available form of justice* — the man is dead; the correction cannot reverse his execution or his years in prison; but the archive that says he was convicted justly is a lie, and a lie in the archive corrupts every record that touches it; the Fighter carries the correction because the archive deserves to be true even when the correction comes too late to help the person it exonerates.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: "The Perjured Record"

**The Token · The Clerk's Sealed Recantation ·
a notarized document in a dying man's hand ·
signed and sealed by the notary who watched him sign ·
it names names that are still in power ·
addressed to the Archive of Civic Records ·
the magistrate's agents are already on the street outside**

---

### Act I — The Deathbed
*The Clerk's Sealed Recantation · in the clerk's hands ·
a small residential house near the civic quarter, a sickroom,
late morning, the notary already gone*

The clerk is propped on pillows. He is seventy, thin, in pain in the specific way of
a man who has been in pain for several months and has stopped trying to manage the
expression on his face. The notary was here an hour ago and left. The document is
sealed and on the bed beside him.

He picks it up and holds it toward you. His hand is shaking with the effort.

*"Twenty-two years ago I was paid to give false testimony in the matter of Gerhardt
Unger. He was convicted. He was executed. His children are in the city — the oldest
is a glassblower on the south road. The document names everyone who was involved.
Including the magistrate Haas. Particularly the magistrate Haas."*

He looks at you. He is not asking for absolution. He is not asking you to say it is
all right. He is asking you to take the document to the archive.

*"They have people outside already. I saw one from the window this morning. You will
need to leave through the kitchen passage."*

**DC 12 Insight — understand that this document is not about the clerk's conscience; it is about Gerhardt Unger's children having a city archive that says their father was falsely convicted, which is a different thing from having a father; understand that the clerk knows this and is not confused about the distinction.**

*If you fail:* You take it with the wrong understanding — you are doing something for the dying man, which makes the errand feel personal. The right understanding is that you are doing something for the archive, which is impersonal in a way that requires a different kind of steadiness.

*If you pass:* The document is for the archive. The dead man's children are not part of the commission — they are the reason the commission matters. You take it through the kitchen passage.

---

### Act II — The Side Street
*The Clerk's Sealed Recantation · inside your coat · the side street behind
the clerk's house, the magistrate's legal officers visible at the main gate*

Two men in civic livery at the front gate, one on the side street watching the kitchen
passage door. The side street is narrow, ends in an alley, connects to the civic
quarter road three buildings down.

The man at the kitchen passage door has seen you. He is twenty feet away.

**DC 12 Stealth — move toward the alley end of the side street at the pace of someone who lives in this district and turned into the wrong alley and is correcting course; do not accelerate until you are out of his sightline.**

*If you fail:* He calls after you. You run. He runs. You reach the civic quarter road a hundred feet ahead of him, which is enough, but the chase has been noted.

*If you pass:* You turn right at the alley's end at the exact pace of a resident making a correction, and you are on the civic quarter road before he decides whether to follow.

---

### Act III — The Civic Quarter
*The Clerk's Sealed Recantation · in your coat · the civic quarter,
the magistrate's senior legal agent with a procedural injunction*

He is in the road between you and the archive. He is holding a folded writ. He is
courteous and precise.

*"Post-mortem testimony in cases closed under the thirty-year statutory bar. The
Unger matter was formally closed nineteen years ago. Any new deposition is inadmissible
under statute. I'm authorized to accept the document for referral to the statutory
review board, which will schedule a hearing in the spring session."*

The review board, scheduled for the spring session, is chaired by the magistrate Haas.

The thirty-year bar does not apply to cases where the original conviction was based
on fabricated testimony — because a case built on fabrication was never legitimately
opened. The bar protects closed cases. This case was never validly opened.

**DC 13 Persuasion — the statutory bar requires a legitimately-closed case; a conviction obtained through fabricated testimony was never legitimately reached; the bar has no application to a case that was, legally speaking, a procedure that never validly occurred.**

*If you fail:* He disputes the interpretation. You need to involve a third party — a passing civic notary who agrees with your position but only partially. You get through, but with the legal agent's formal objection logged in the street.

*If you pass:* He looks at you for a long moment. He knows the argument is correct. He steps aside. He will report to the magistrate immediately. The archive is three streets ahead.

---

### Act IV — The Archive Gate
*The Clerk's Sealed Recantation · in your coat · the archive building gate,
a legal officer and an enforcer blocking entry*

The legal officer has a court order barring entry to individuals carrying "potentially defamatory materials related to ongoing civic proceedings." The enforcer beside him is there in case the legal officer's authority is disputed.

The court order was issued this morning. The clerk signed his recantation this morning. The speed of this is not a coincidence.

**Combat — Legal Enforcer (AC 13, HP 24, short sword)**

*If you fail (reduced to 0 HP):* You are taken to the side room. The document is confiscated. The legal officer inspects the seal and seizes it under the court order. It will go to the statutory review board, which will meet in spring, chaired by Magistrate Haas.

*If you pass:* The enforcer is down. The legal officer steps back. You are through the archive gate.

---

### Act V — The Intake Desk
*The Clerk's Sealed Recantation · in your hands ·
the archive intake room, the Archivus at her desk,
a ledger open, a candle, morning light*

The Archivus is a woman in her fifties with ink on her left hand from a morning of filing. She looks at the document you set on her desk. She looks at the notary's seal. She looks at you.

*"Addressed to this archive. Properly sealed and notarized. I am required to accept it."*

She breaks the seal — this is her function; she is not opening someone's private correspondence; she is receiving a formal archival submission. She reads the first two lines.

She looks up. The document names the magistrate Haas in the second sentence.

*"This is going to be a complicated day for everyone in this building,"* she says. She sounds entirely calm.

She picks up her intake stamp. She looks at you again.

*The Clerk's Sealed Recantation is on her desk. She is about to make it a permanent part of the Archive of Civic Records. What the magistrate does next is his business. What the city does is the city's business. What the clerk's twenty-two years of silence cost Gerhardt Unger's family is not in this room. What is in this room is the stamp, the ledger, and the document.*

**DC 11 Composure — remain present while she stamps it; do not say anything; the document is complete; the archive's intake is its own act and does not require commentary.**

*If you fail:* You say something — something accurate and well-intentioned — about the clerk's state of mind, or the family, or the magistrate. The Archivus looks at you and says, gently, that she didn't ask. You stop. She stamps.

*If you pass:* She stamps the document. She enters it in the ledger. She sets it in the intake tray for filing. She looks at you.

*"I'll need your name for the chain of custody."*

You give it. She writes it. You leave. Outside, in the civic quarter, the magistrate's legal agent is running.
