## Cycle 1 — Angle: The obligation that arrives with the news

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

The Boy's Froissart (1879) is Sidney Lanier's adaptation of Jean Froissart's Chronicles for young male readers. Lanier (1842–1881) — the Southern poet, Confederate veteran, and consumptive musician who spent his last years writing "boy's books" against mounting medical debts — brought his literary gifts fully to the task. This is not a dumbing-down of Froissart but a selection: Lanier stripped the diplomatic machinery and the financial negotiations, compressed the battles, and expanded the personal episodes — the moments where individual men face choices that test what they are made of. He was making a moral argument to his audience, and he chose his episodes accordingly.

The adaptation follows the same historical arc as the full Chronicles (1327–1400) but with different weight. Where Froissart distributes his attention across courts and campaigns, Lanier concentrates it on four or five episodes of personal decision. The result is less complete as history and more complete as moral narrative.

**Sluys and the War's Opening.** Edward III's claim to the French throne through his mother Isabella; the naval battle of Sluys (1340), narrated with the excitement of a sea adventure; the opening of the Channel as the English strategic corridor. Lanier sets this up quickly — he is heading toward Crécy.

**Crécy (1346).** Lanier gives this extended treatment. The Genoese crossbowmen; the longbowmen; the sixteen cavalry charges failing on the same arrows. The central scene: the Black Prince hard-pressed enough that his chamberlain sends to Edward III for reinforcement. Edward's reply — "Let the boy win his spurs; I am certain he will acquit himself so that you will be satisfied with him" — is rendered by Lanier with great care, because it is exactly the episode he is looking for: a father making a calculated and dangerous gift of his son's own crisis. Edward will not protect him from the battle, because the battle is what his son needs to become who he is supposed to be.

**Calais (1346–47).** The six burghers with halter ropes; Philippa's intercession. Lanier retells this with full emotional weight. He finds Philippa's calculation — the design of the intercession so that Edward can say "I cannot refuse you" — and renders it sympathetically.

**Poitiers (1356) and the Capture of John II.** The Black Prince against three-to-one odds; the longbow anchors; the capture of the French king in person. Lanier is most attentive to the dinner scene: the prince serving King John at table, refusing to sit because it would not be fitting to sit at the table of so great a prince. He is interested in this moment as the chivalric code fully inhabited — not performed, not calculated, but lived. He quotes the exchange at length.

**The Treaty of Brétigny (1360) and the Hostage System.** After Poitiers, John II was ransomed for three million gold écus — a sum that would take France years to pay. John returned to France to manage the collection, leaving hostages including his son Louis of Anjou in English custody as surety. The installments fell short repeatedly.

**John II's Voluntary Return to Captivity (1364).** This is the episode Lanier treats with more gravity than anything else in the adaptation. Louis of Anjou, tiring of confinement in Calais, simply left. He rode out of English custody without leave. The English sent notice to France. John II called his council.

His councillors argued that Louis's escape voided the hostage arrangement. John was under no legal compulsion to return; he was a free king in his own capital with his own army; no force in Europe could compel him. Returning voluntarily would place him back in captivity without gain — no additional leverage, no military advantage, no political benefit whatsoever.

John listened to all of it. Then he said, and this is the sentence Froissart records and Lanier underlines in his moral framing of the whole book: *"If good faith and honor were banished from the rest of the world, they ought still to be found in the hearts and mouths of kings."*

He returned to England voluntarily. He was received with honor at London. He fell ill — from the crossing, or the captivity, or an illness he had been carrying since Poitiers. He died at the Savoy Palace in April 1364. He never returned to France. He was never ransomed.

Lanier ends the chapter on John with a single sentence: "And so he died, a king."

**The Routiers.** The free companies after Brétigny — professional soldiers without employers, occupying the roads and smaller towns of France, extorting ransoms, fighting for whoever would pay or for themselves when no one would. Lanier covers this as lawless energy: the companies as the war's residue, what a generation of professional violence leaves when the formal conflict pauses.

**The Siege of Limoges (1370).** The Black Prince, already dying of an intestinal illness, carried to the siege in a litter after the bishop of Limoges betrayed the English cause. The Black Prince in his litter orders the assault; the city is sacked. Three French knights — Jean de Villemur, Hugh de la Roche, Roger de Beaufort — fight on alone in a corner of the burning city after all other resistance has ended. The Black Prince, watching from his litter, sees them and says they are worthy men whose lives should be spared. He has them brought to him and receives them personally. The episode sits in strange moral counterpoint to the massacre surrounding it: the code working in miniature while the code fails wholesale.

**The Tournament of St. Inglevert (1390).** Three French knights — Boucicaut, Renaud de Roye, Jean de Sempy — erect their shields in a meadow and announce they will hold against all comers for thirty days. English, Scottish, Spanish, and Portuguese knights arrive to challenge. The episode has the quality of theater — the shields hung from a tree, the herald carrying challenges back and forth, the daily jousts recorded in the tally. Lanier is drawn to its formality: chivalry organized as performance, with rules that everyone agrees to observe.

**Richard II and the End.** The Peasants' Revolt: Richard at Smithfield, Tyler's death, the young king's ride into the crowd. The long deterioration. Flint Castle. Lanier ends there. He died two years after the book's publication; it was his last completed major prose work.

**What the adaptation argues:** That the chivalric code is not a social system but a moral discipline, and that a man who inhabits it fully — as the Black Prince inhabits it, as John II inhabits it in the one decision that defines him — is not performing virtue but living it. Lanier, writing in 1879 to boys in a post-war South, believed that the form of honor he was describing was still real and still available, and that the 14th century was its best recorded instance. Whether he was right about that is a different question. He was right about John II.

### Pass 2 — Slower

**King John II of France** is Lanier's primary moral protagonist — not the active hero (that is the Black Prince) but the man whose one great decision makes the whole book cohere. He was a poor king in the political sense: defeated at Poitiers, unable to manage the ransom, governing a France broken by war and plague. He was not strategically gifted; he was not politically astute; he made the decisions that put him in captivity in the first place. What he had, and what Lanier locates as the book's real subject, was an understanding of what the word "king" obligated — not as privilege but as discipline. When Louis escaped, John's legal obligation was void. He returned because he understood that being a king is not a circumstance but a commitment, and that commitments of that kind do not become optional when circumstances change.

**The Black Prince** is the book's active hero — the man who does everything well, from Crécy to Poitiers to Limoges, and who ends in a litter. His arc is the decline of the active form; his illness strips the capacity for action from the discipline that remains. What he can still do from the litter is recognize: he sees three knights fighting on alone in a burning city and names them as what they are. Recognition is the last capacity to go.

**Louis of Anjou** is the figure whose absence creates the story. He was a hostage in Calais who got tired of waiting and left. He did not come back. He was not present for his father's decision about what to do next. He lived until 1384. He never reconciled with his father in any documented way. His escape is the occasion of John's great moment, but Louis himself is not the story — John's response to Louis is the story.

**Edward III** in Lanier's version is more fully drawn than he usually is: the man who designed the "earn your own spurs" moment at Crécy, who understood that a father can give his son the crisis that makes him or breaks him, and that this is a different kind of love from protection. He is also the man who received John back with full honor when John returned voluntarily — Froissart records that Edward was moved by the gesture, which is the appropriate response to it.

**Sidney Lanier himself** is the book's invisible protagonist. He is present in every selection, every emphasis, every moral frame. He was writing in 1879 to boys in a defeated South, and he believed that personal honor — freely chosen, costly, unrewarded by circumstance — was the last defense of the interior self against everything the world could do to it. John II confirmed his belief.

### Pass 3 — Elaborate One Scene: The Council Chamber

John II called his council in Paris in early 1364. He had been home for three years — free, governing, trying to manage the ransom installments that France could not afford. His council knew him; they had served him through Poitiers, through the captivity in London, through the Treaty of Brétigny, through every humiliation the war had produced. They were not afraid of him. They were afraid for him.

He told them Louis had escaped from Calais. He told them he intended to return to England.

The silence lasted as long as silences last in a room full of men who have just heard something they cannot immediately organize a response to.

Then they began. Their arguments were intelligent and they were correct. Louis's departure voided the hostage arrangement under the terms of Brétigny. John was under no legal obligation whatsoever; the English themselves could not argue he was. He was a free king in his own city with his own administration and his own army. Returning voluntarily would accomplish nothing — no ransom leverage, no military advantage, no diplomatic gain. He would simply be in captivity again, for the same sum, with the same problem, having given up the freedom that had taken years of negotiation to achieve.

John listened to all of it. He had been listening in his own mind for days before he called the council; he had already argued this case, on both sides, to himself. He knew the arguments. He knew they were good arguments.

"If good faith and honor were banished from the rest of the world," he said, when they had finished, "they ought still to be found in the hearts and mouths of kings."

He said it in the way a man says something he has been preparing to say — not dramatically but with precision, as though the sentence had been refined in private until it was exactly right. He was not asking for agreement. He was naming what he was and what it required of him.

His council did not argue further. There is no argument available against that sentence that doesn't concede its premises.

He sailed for England in January. He was received with full honor at London — Edward, the sources say, was moved. John settled at the Savoy Palace. He fell ill in February; the sources cannot agree on the cause. He died in April 1364, in the same palace his successors' free companies would burn seventeen years later in Wat Tyler's revolt, without having been ransomed, without having returned to France.

Lanier ends it in one sentence, which is the right number of sentences: "And so he died, a king."

The sentence works because "king" in Lanier's usage is not a description of rank but a description of what John proved. He was a bad king by most measures of kingship. He was a king by the only measure that Lanier, and perhaps Froissart, considered finally relevant: he kept faith when he could have chosen not to, at the cost of his freedom and his life.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Notice of Anjou** — Louis of Anjou has escaped English custody at Calais; the English chancellor must dispatch the sealed notice of escape to John II in France, because John's honor requires that he know; John's council in Paris will do everything short of open force to prevent the notice reaching the king before the council session closes — they know what he will do the moment he reads it; the Fighter carries the notice from London through Calais, across the Channel, and into the king's hand before his councillors can seal him away from it.

2. **The Prince Refuses the Chair** — After Poitiers, the Black Prince has arranged a dinner for King John II of France; a court faction has prepared a seating plan placing an English duke at the captured king's table; the prince's written instruction refusing all English nobles a place at so great a prince's dinner must reach the royal steward before the duke's chamberlain arrives with the competing arrangement.

3. **The Routier's Window** — A free-company captain has taken a French town for ransom and is holding the sack in abeyance for three days; the ransom payment is delayed at a merchant's vault in the next city; the Fighter carries the payment voucher through the captain's own picket lines and the merchant's locked vault before the three-day window expires and the captain's men begin without authorization.

4. **Three Knights at Limoges** — At the sack of Limoges, three French knights are fighting on alone in a burning quarter after all other resistance has ended; the Black Prince, watching from his litter, has given a verbal order to take them alive; the written confirmation of that order must reach the assault commander before his men decide the verbal order was never confirmed.

5. **The Crécy Certification** — After Crécy, a herald must certify that the Black Prince earned his spurs in the formal chivalric sense — meaning he fought without reinforcement when it was offered; a rival lord's faction claims the prince accepted informal support from a flanking unit; the squire who was with the prince at the critical moment has signed a sworn deposition that no support arrived; the Fighter carries it to the College of Heralds before the rival faction's formal objection is enrolled.

6. **The St. Inglevert Tally** — At the tournament of St. Inglevert, a dispute has arisen over whether a specific lord's shield-touch was formally recorded before the day's session closed; the witnessing herald has a correction on his person but is detained at the tournament gate by a credential dispute; the Fighter must get the correction to the master record before the lord's formal complaint is entered as an unanswered dispute.

7. **The Escaped Hostage's Letter** — Louis of Anjou, having escaped, wrote his father a single letter from the road explaining his decision; the letter is on a courier intercepted between Calais and Paris; it would give John the one thing his council cannot give him — his son's voice before his own decision; the Fighter must retrieve it from the courier's confiscated bag and deliver it to John before the council meeting at which John will announce his intention to return.

### 5-Act Vignette Play — "The Notice of Anjou"

**TOKEN:** The Anjou Escape Notice — a sealed diplomatic document from the English Crown's Lord Chancellor, recording that Louis, Duke of Anjou, first son of King John II of France, departed from English royal custody at Calais without royal leave on the date stated; addressed to the King of France personally; the document that makes John's return to captivity possible because it makes his obligation known to him

---

**ACT I — The Chancellor's Seal**

*The Anjou Escape Notice · drafted, in the Lord Chancellor's office · held by the sealing clerk · London, the English chancellery, 1364*

The Lord Chancellor's office smells of tallow and old parchment and sealing wax kept at a low boil on a small brazier near the door. The notice has been drafted. It sits on the clerk's desk, written out in the precise formal hand of a chancellery letter, addressed to the King of France personally. The clerk has been standing with the wax pen for twenty minutes.

He is not a political man. He knows what the letter says. He understands, with the specific knowledge of a man who has worked in the chancellery for fifteen years, that when a French king receives a notice that his son has escaped English custody, the notice contains an obligation — not a legal one, but something else, something harder to name. He knows what John II is. He does not want to be the man who sent this particular letter.

*His fear is not of the Fighter. His fear is of the letter and what it will do when read.*

The Fighter enters. The clerk looks up. He looks at the notice on his desk. He looks at the wax pen.

"He will come back," the clerk says. Not an accusation. A recognition. "When he reads it. You understand that."

*He is asking whether the Fighter understands what they are carrying.*

"He already knows Louis left," the Fighter says. "He is waiting for the formal notice to act on it."

The clerk is silent for a moment. He has not thought of it this way — that the notice does not cause the decision but enables it; that John II is waiting in Paris for the letter that gives him the form of his own obligation.

He seals it.

The Anjou Escape Notice is heavier than formal diplomatic paper has any right to be.

---

**ACT II — The Transit Agent at Calais**

*The Anjou Escape Notice · sealed, in transit · held by the Fighter · Calais harbor*

The harbor at Calais smells of salt and rope and the Channel mist that sits low in the mornings. France is visible on clear days. This is not a clear day. The French transit agent at the diplomatic passage desk is a thin man in a grey surcoat who has a legitimate authority to inspect correspondence entering France under the Brétigny transit protocols.

He holds out his hand.

"All diplomatic correspondence crossing from English jurisdiction into France is subject to review under the channel agreement," he says. He is not hostile; he is procedural. He has been procedural all morning and he will be procedural until sundown.

The notice is addressed to the King of France personally.

*The addressing protocol exists for exactly this scenario: the agent's review authority does not extend to correspondence addressed to the sovereign.*

"The document is addressed to the King of France personally," the Fighter says. "The channel agreement's review clause applies to correspondence addressed to French subjects. The King of France is not a subject."

The transit agent looks at the address. He looks at the review clause in the agreement, which he has been carrying folded in his surcoat for two years. He reads the clause. He looks at the address again.

He stamps the transit log.

"Proceed," he says, with the expression of a man who has correctly identified that he has been outmaneuvered and has chosen to allow it.

---

**ACT III — The Councillor's Corridor**

*The Anjou Escape Notice · sealed, in transit · held by the Fighter · the corridor outside the French royal antechamber, Paris*

The corridor outside John II's antechamber is high-ceilinged and cold, stone floor, two candle stands flanking the inner door. Pierre, the chief councillor — a careful, loyal, frightened man who has served John through Poitiers and London and the Treaty and the installments and every other disaster — stands in the corridor with two chamberlains. Not barring it. Present.

"We will receive the correspondence on His Majesty's behalf," Pierre says. He sounds entirely reasonable. "The king is in preparation for the afternoon council session. All incoming correspondence is being received by the council today."

"The document is addressed to the king personally," the Fighter says. "Personal correspondence cannot be received by council on the king's behalf under the addressing protocol. Receiving it yourself would be opening the king's personal mail."

Pierre looks at the Fighter. He is not stupid. He knows the Fighter is correct about the protocol. He also knows what is in the letter and what John will do when he reads it, and he has three more years of service in him and does not want them to be years of watching his king choose to die in England for the sake of a principle.

*He is not deciding about protocol. He is deciding whether to love his king by preventing him from being who he is.*

"I understand your concern for him," the Fighter says. "He knows what he is doing. He is waiting for this letter."

Pierre looks at the envelope. He looks at the chamberlains. He steps aside.

He does not stop the chamberlains from stepping aside with him. They take their instruction from Pierre.

---

**ACT IV — The Antechamber**

*The Anjou Escape Notice · sealed, in the Fighter's hands · who holds it: the Fighter · the royal antechamber, Paris*

The antechamber is thirty feet of stone floor crowded with petitioners, minor nobles, a secretary at a small table near the window, and the usher at the privy chamber door at the far end. The privy council session is underway behind the closed door. It will end in — the secretary, asked — perhaps ten minutes, perhaps five, at which point the king will enter private retreat for the afternoon and receive no correspondence until tomorrow morning's formal session.

Tomorrow morning is too late. If the English receive no acknowledgment of the notice within the window, they will record John's failure to respond as a formal violation.

The Fighter is at the near door. The usher is thirty feet away with his back turned, checking the session's entry log.

*Ten minutes. Thirty feet. Fifteen petitioners and nobles between the Fighter and the door.*

The Fighter moves through the room — not at a run, which causes alarm, but at the pace of someone who knows exactly where they are going and has no doubt they belong there. A minor noble steps aside by reflex. A secretary lifts his papers out of the way. The petitioners part for a person moving with that specific quality of certainty.

The usher turns before the Fighter arrives.

"Personal correspondence," the Fighter says. "Addressed to the king. Urgently."

The usher looks at the seal. He looks at the addressing. He opens the door.

---

**ACT V — The King Reads**

*The Anjou Escape Notice · opened, read, on the table · held by the moment · the privy chamber*

John II stands with the notice in his hands. He has read it. He sets it on the table and looks at it for a moment, and then at the Fighter. His council has paused around him — four men who know what the letter must contain and are watching him with the particular attention of people who already know what he is going to say and cannot believe he is going to say it.

"He left on the fourteenth," John says. To no one in particular.

He does not appear to be in pain. He appears to be organizing something — not the decision, which is already made, but the form in which to announce it; the sentence that tells men who love him and are afraid for him that this is not a decision they can argue about.

*He is looking for the word. Not the reason. He has the reason. The word that lets a king say the hardest thing without asking for agreement.*

The Fighter says: "You have been waiting for it."

John looks at the Fighter. Something in his expression settles — the last piece placed. He was waiting for it. The notice does not create the decision; it completes it.

"Yes," he says. He folds the notice and gives it to his steward. "Send word to England. I am returning."

He picks up his pen and writes his acknowledgment on the small slip the steward provides. He signs and seals it. He gives the Fighter the copy.

The council watches. No one argues. The sentence he used was the right sentence — not "If good faith and honor were banished from the rest of the world, they ought still to be found in the hearts and mouths of kings," which is what he will say in three days when he repeats this scene for his full council, the version Froissart will record — but the single word that preceded it, in this room, with these men: *yes.*

## Cycle 2 — Angle: The purchased grace — what the routier extorted from the institution

*Prior cycle (Cycle 1) missed:* Cycle 1 took the angle of John II's voluntary return — the man who chose obligation over freedom. The Boys' Froissart also contains the opposite figure: the Archpriest, whose company forced the Pope to pay them in the currency of grace. Cycle 1 found the man who dignifies the institution by obeying it; Cycle 2 finds the man who degrades it by making it negotiate. Lanier presents both without choosing between them. The form of obligation is the same; only the direction of the payment differs.

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

The Boys' Froissart's routier chapters occupy a structurally essential position in Lanier's selection. Having narrated Crécy, Poitiers, and the capture of John II, Lanier pauses to describe what a generation of professional warfare produces when the formal conflict pauses: the Free Companies. These are soldiers without employers, men trained for nothing else, the residue of two decades of Anglo-French war moving through a kingdom with no mechanism to absorb or disarm them.

The Archpriest is the central figure of this interlude. Sir Arnold de Cervole — "more commonly called the archpriest" — was a former canon who had taken up arms and risen to command a substantial force. Froissart records the episode succinctly: the Archpriest assembled a large body of men after Poitiers, when it became clear that the truce had ended professional pay without ending professional soldiers. They marched into Provence and burned their way toward Avignon. Pope Innocent VI, resident in Avignon, found himself negotiating with a man who had spent the previous months demonstrating what he was willing to do to cities and monasteries.

The negotiation was not long. The Archpriest and his men entered Avignon. They were received — and here Froissart's register shifts into something drily remarkable — "with as much respect as if he had been son to the King of France." He dined with the pope and cardinals. He received absolution for all his sins. He was given forty thousand crowns "to distribute among his companions." Then he left.

Lanier, writing for boys in 1879, presents this without commentary. He does not explain whether the absolution was genuine or the crowns were blackmail. He lets the episode sit in the same register as the Crécy and Poitiers chapters — as one of the things that happened in the 14th century. The boys must read it themselves and draw their own conclusions. Lanier trusts them.

The Archpriest is not the only routier in the Boys' Froissart. A Welshman named Ruffin commanded a company between the Seine and the Loire: no one dared travel between Paris and Orléans. Sir Robert Knolles had acquired "upward of one hundred thousand crowns" through the same trade and was generous enough with his soldiers that they followed him without compulsion. These were men who had made war into a supply chain. They had the skills the world had paid for, and the world had stopped paying, and they continued to use the skills they had.

By 1367 — the game's setting — many routier companies had followed the Black Prince to Spain for the Nájera campaign against Henry of Trastámara. But not all companies had gone. The Boys' Froissart's routier chapters describe the conditions producing the game's Périgord setting: the limestone towns with locked gates, the question of which agreements between armed men without common authority could be made to hold.

**Historical moment:** Froissart wrote throughout 1360s–1400. Lanier adapted in 1879, choosing episodes arguing that personal honor is the last interior defense. The routier chapters sit between his narrative of Poitiers (1356) and the Jacquerie (1358) — lawlessness as consequence of formally sanctioned violence without formal conclusion. This is not moralizing; it is accurate observation.

**Themes and morals:** The Archpriest episode argues that institutions negotiate with power when they have no other option; that the ceremony of absolution can be used as currency; that a man who understands this is operating in the same system as the Pope, with different leverage. What the episode does not argue is that the Archpriest was wrong. He was effective. His men received forty thousand crowns. The Pope received the departure he needed. The ceremony served its purpose.

**Chapter Notes**

- **Chapter LXXXV (Archpriest):** Arnold de Cervole assembles Free Companies; marches to Provence; extorts and receives papal absolution and forty thousand crowns; departs. Structurally: the institution purchasing the removal of a problem it cannot militarily resolve.
- **Chapter LXXXVI (Ruffin):** A Welsh routier captain between Seine and Loire; Sir Robert Knolles active on the Norman coast; the countryside of France emptied of travelers. Structurally: the same phenomenon, without the Avignon negotiation endpoint.
- **Chapter LXXXVII (Provost):** The Paris uprising and murder of three of the regent's knights; the institution under simultaneous threat from a different direction.
- **Chapter LXXXVIII (Jacquerie):** The peasant uprising in Beauvoisis; its ideology and violence. Structurally: the third destabilizing force of the post-Poitiers period.
- **Chapter LXXXIX (Meaux):** The Earl of Foix and the Captal of Buch suppress the Jacquerie; seven thousand killed. Structurally: restabilization of the noble order through exactly the military violence the routiers represented.

### Pass 2 — Slower

**Arnold de Cervole, the Archpriest** — former canon, routier captain, practical man. What is most remarkable about him is not his violence, which was normal for his profession, but his legibility to the institution that received him. He dined with the Pope and cardinals and received absolution because both parties understood the terms. He was not confused about what he was doing, and neither were they. A man who extorts religious grace from the papacy and accepts it without embarrassment is making a statement about what grace means in a world organized by power. Froissart records it neutrally; Lanier passes it on without editorial.

**Pope Innocent VI** — the man who paid. He is a supporting character but the one who decides. The choice to negotiate rather than refuse and be burned out of Avignon was rational. Whether it was right is the question Lanier leaves open. The cardinals dined with the Archpriest: this is Froissart's image of the institution under pressure, and it is precise.

**Ruffin and Knolles** — two other points on the same spectrum. Ruffin had no nickname from clerical status; he was a Welsh soldier who had ended at the head of a company. Knolles paid his men promptly and appears in the Peasants' Revolt chapters thirty years later still armed and loyal. The Free Companies were not a uniform phenomenon.

**The world's register** — France after Poitiers, 1356–1360: travelers cannot move between Paris and Orléans, the nobility flees from château to walled town. The companies are not filling this space so much as revealing how thin the pre-existing structure was. This is the landscape the game's 1367 characters inhabit, seven years further along: some stability restored, but the Périgord roads still watched by men who understand the Archpriest's logic.

### Pass 3 — Elaborate One Scene: The Archpriest Dines at Avignon

The dining room in the papal palace at Avignon is large and well-lit. The candles are good. The floor is tile. The Archpriest sits across from the Pope and the cardinals and eats good food.

He has been in Avignon for several days. His men are outside the walls — there is a limit to how many of them can be in the city at once without the ceremony becoming impossible to maintain — but they are there, and the city knows it, and the papal household knows it. The forty thousand crowns are being counted somewhere in another room. Later, when they are ready, someone will bring the papers.

The Archpriest is a man who was trained for the Church and knows its ceremonies. He knows what absolution requires: contrition, confession, purpose of amendment. He is not contrite. He has no purpose of amendment. What he has is forty thousand crowns for his men and a certificate that will be useful on the road.

The cardinals eat their food. The Pope says the correct things. The Archpriest says the correct things. There is nothing false in the exchange because nothing is being claimed. Both parties know that a company of armed men is outside the walls, and that the company requires payment to depart, and that the payment will be made in two currencies: crowns and ceremony. The ceremony is necessary. Without it, the crowns are blackmail and the Pope has surrendered to force. With it, the crowns are a charitable gift to redeemed men and the Pope has exercised pastoral authority. The distinction matters to the institution. The Archpriest does not care about the distinction, which is why he is able to give the institution what it needs.

He receives the absolution. He accepts it. He departs.

The certificate travels with him — proof on the road that his company has the formal status of absolved men, which is a different legal category from bandits, which is useful at toll gates and in negotiations with town councils who need a reason to open their gates.

He uses it for years. He dies around 1366. The certificate stays with whoever held his papers.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Archpriest's Receipt** — The forty thousand crowns leaving Avignon were tracked by a papal financial officer whose ledger shows the payment under "charitable disbursements to redeemed penitents"; the ledger is in a dissolving Périgord monastery; the Fighter carries it to a neutral archive before the dissolving friars destroy the evidence of the transaction.

2. **The Absolution Certificate** — The Archpriest's original certificate of papal absolution has survived his death and passed to a lesser captain who uses it as a credential in 1367; a Bordeaux merchant wants it authenticated so it can be presented to a town council as proof Free Companies can be formally dealt with; the Fighter carries it from the monastery where it is held to the notary and back before the council session closes.

3. **Ruffin's Passport** — Ruffin the Welsh captain received a formal safe conduct from the Cardinal of Périgord in exchange for leaving the Seine-Loire corridor; the document has been lost; Ruffin's successor in 1367 needs an authenticated copy to prove his company has standing as a legitimate force; the Fighter carries it.

4. **The Knolles Commission** — When Knolles finally left France, he left behind a Gascon lieutenant with a commission authorizing operation in the English interest in Périgord; in 1367 this commission is the only document distinguishing the Gascon's company from an unlicensed Free Company; the Fighter carries it from a Bordeaux registry before the French administration can classify the company as hostile.

5. **The Cardinal's Roster** — The Cardinal of Périgord kept records of which companies had agreed to join the Spanish expedition and which had not; his secretariat was attacked; one record listing twelve companies who had promised to leave and hadn't is in a farmhouse; the Fighter carries it to the Black Prince's headquarters.

6. **The Jacquerie Account** — Three knights who survived the Meaux suppression kept a private account of what they witnessed; one is dying in a Périgord monastery in 1367 and wants the account filed before he dies; it is also evidence against a lord who exceeded his authority during the suppression; the Fighter carries it to Weimar before the lord's agents intercept it.

7. **The Pope's Guarantee** — Urban V sent letters to the Black Prince before Nájera guaranteeing absolution to companies joining the expedition on return; one letter intended for a specific captain was never delivered because the captain was killed; it remains sealed in a Bordeaux courier office; the Fighter carries it to the captain's surviving lieutenant who needs it to finally disband the company.

### Theme Check

Reviewing all accumulated themes and Cycle 1's angle:
- **BLQ:** Document formally correct, substantively fraudulent — the carrier never opens the seal. CRL-S2's document is neither fraudulent nor concealed: both parties know it is a transaction in the form of a grace.
- **LIL (Cycle 2):** Document creates law between parties acknowledging no common authority. CRL-S2's document does not create law; it claims a status.
- **CRL (Cycle 1):** The notice creates the obligation. CRL-S2 concerns a document already completed, now used as precedent.
- **HAV:** The corsair/pirate distinction rests on a single document. HAV's document CREATES the legal category; CRL-S2's TRANSFERS a previously granted status to a new context.

**Existing themes avoided:** the formal fraud (BLQ), the enabling notification (CRL-1), the founding document (HAV), the law-between-parties (LIL).

**Chosen quest:** Storyline 2 — **"The Archpriest's Certificate"**

**Theme:** *The institutional grace that was purchased cannot be transferred, but the precedent it creates can be used by anyone who carries the original; what a reluctant captain needed was not a promise but proof that such promises are made at all — and carrying the evidence of a completed transaction is not the same as carrying a current guarantee, which is sufficient for a town council that needs a reason to open the gate and for a company that needs a reason to march*

This is distinct from all 39 accumulated themes. No previous quest concerns the re-application of a purchased institutional grace as a precedent in a context its creator never imagined — not forgery, not fraud, but the transfer of a prior transaction's authority to a new use.

### 5-Act Vignette Play — "The Archpriest's Certificate"

**TOKEN:** The Certificate of Papal Absolution — a sealed parchment in Latin, bearing the signet of Pope Innocent VI (Avignon, 1357), granted to Sir Arnold de Cervole, known as the Archpriest, upon receipt of conditions of departure from Provence; the document that makes an armed captain into a formally absolved man, which is a different legal category from a bandit, which is all a town council needs to justify opening the gate

---

**ACT I — The Monastery's Last Room**

*The Certificate · cold, in a cedar box · held by Brother Eudo, last remaining archivist · a dissolving Périgord priory, 1367*

The priory is being dissolved. Two-thirds of the brothers have already transferred. Brother Eudo, who has been the archivist for thirty years, is in the final room deciding what goes to the diocesan registry, what can be destroyed, and what — he is uncertain about this third category — he can give to someone who will use it.

The cedar box contains the Archpriest's certificate. It came to the priory in 1366 with the effects of a deceased captain who had served under the Archpriest and held the document in trust after the Archpriest's death. The captain died on the road north of Périgueux. His effects were given to the priory. Now the priory is closing.

*The certificate is quiet in its box, waiting for someone to understand what it is.*

Brother Eudo opens the box and shows the Fighter the document.

"The Archpriest ate dinner with the Pope," he says, not accusingly, but with the precision of a man who has read the document. "This says so. It says he is absolved of all his sins. The forty thousand crowns are not mentioned. The ledger was kept separately."

*The Fighter understands: the document only mentions the grace.*

The Fighter says: "Then let the document say what it says. The grace is real enough to matter to a man who needs the category, whether he earned it or purchased it."

Eudo closes the box. He hands it over.

---

**ACT II — The Road Checkpoint**

*The Certificate · sealed, in the cedar box · held by the Fighter · the Périgord ridge road gate*

The administrative gate is a timber frame with a registry desk. The official is a thin man with a book of rules and authority to apply them. He looks at the cedar box.

"What is in it?"

"A sealed document. From Avignon. Bearing the pope's signet."

He opens his registry book to the section on ecclesiastical correspondence. The relevant clause: documents sealed with a papal signet are subject to diocesan authority, not administrative review. But the official is uncertain whether "sealed with" means "currently bearing the intact signet" or "originally sealed by the pope and subsequently handled."

*The seal is intact. The question is jurisdictional, not factual.*

*The Fighter considers the clause's exact wording.*

The Fighter says: "The clause reads 'sealed with the apostolic signet.' The signet is present and intact. The clause applies. Your registry has no inspection authority here."

The official reads the clause. He reads it again. He makes a notation: *Papal signet, transit, intact — diocesan authority confirmed.* He stamps the log.

"Proceed."

---

**ACT III — The Bordeaux Inn**

*The Certificate · sealed, in the cedar box · held by the Fighter · the Free Company inn district, Bordeaux*

The Bordeaux inn district in early 1367 is a city within the city: companies forming for Spain, companies deciding whether to join, companies that have decided not to join and watching the others leave. The captain's name is Gaubert de Montardin — not a great name, not a great company, but forty men who have held a pass through the Dordogne valley for three years and are still here.

Gaubert is a practical man. He has been told the Spanish expedition will bring absolution. Told it by the Black Prince's agents. By the Cardinal's secretariat. By two Franciscan friars. Not in writing.

"The Archpriest had it in writing," he says. He doesn't look at the box. He knows it is there.

*He wants the precedent, not the document. He wants to know the institution keeps its word when it is in the institution's interest to keep it.*

The Fighter sets the box on the table.

"This document proves the Church has made such grants before. Not to you — to a man. The form exists. The institution used it. What you are asking for is something this proves can be asked for."

Gaubert looks at the box for a long time. He doesn't touch it.

"Monday," he says. "I'll need the authentication."

---

**ACT IV — The Notary's Study**

*The Certificate · opened, being examined · held by the notary · the Avignon representative's office, Bordeaux*

Pierre de Foix, notary and Avignon representative in Bordeaux, reads the Archpriest's certificate for a long time. His study: small, one south-facing window, the smell of good wax and Latin ink. He is being asked to write an authentication confirming the document is genuine, the signet intact, the absolution formally granted.

He sets it down.

"What you are asking me to attest is that the Church transacts with routier captains by purchasing their departure with ceremonies of grace."

A pause.

"This is true. I will write the authentication."

*He is a notary. His function is to attest to what is true. He does not have to approve of the transaction he is attesting.*

The Fighter says: "The notary's attestation confirms facts, not the wisdom of the men who produced them. What you confirm here is that the signet is Innocent's, the Latin is canonical, and the form is correct. All of those are true."

Pierre picks up his pen. He writes for several minutes. He seals the authentication with his own mark.

"Two things are now in writing. One from 1357; one from 1367. Together they form a chain of custody."

---

**ACT V — Gaubert Reads**

*The Certificate · authenticated, on the table · held by the moment · the Bordeaux inn room, Gaubert's company assembled*

Gaubert holds the authenticated document and reads it. His men watch his face. He sets it on the table. He lights wax. He writes — four lines — to the Black Prince's secretary. Seals it.

"Monday," he says. To his company, to the room, to no one. "The pass will be clear Monday afternoon."

He gives the Fighter the sealed letter and the cedar box together.

*What the certificate did: it did not promise Gaubert anything. It showed him that the institution makes such promises in writing. That was sufficient. He was not uncertain about the Church's intentions. He was uncertain whether intentions become obligations. The document shows that they did — once, for one man, in one transaction both parties needed to work. That is enough of a precedent to march on.*

"The archive gets the box," he says. "The secretary gets the letter."

He stands up. His men begin moving. The cedar box is returned. The certificate goes to the diocesan archive. The pass through the Dordogne valley is clear Monday afternoon. Forty men march east toward Spain.
