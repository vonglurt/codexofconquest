## Cycle 1 — Angle: The record that belongs to the man who wrote it

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

Don Quixote is the foundational novel of Western literature — not the first prose fiction but the first novel that knows it is a novel and uses that knowledge as material. Cervantes (1547–1616) wrote Part I in 1605, probably while imprisoned, certainly in debt, having spent his adult life fighting at Lepanto (where he lost the use of his left hand), being captured by Algerian pirates (five years in captivity), and serving Spain in various minor administrative capacities that produced debt and legal trouble. He was nearly sixty when Part I appeared and famous almost immediately. Part II (1615) is darker, stranger, and formally more complex; Cervantes wrote it partly in response to a pirated sequel by a writer using the pseudonym Avellaneda, and the knowledge that a fake Quixote existed in the world enters Part II's plot directly.

**The Setup.** Alonso Quijano, a minor country gentleman of La Mancha, has read so many chivalric romances that he goes mad. He names himself Don Quixote de la Mancha, names his broken-down horse Rocinante, designates a local farm girl named Aldonza Lorenzo as his lady Dulcinea del Toboso, recruits a neighboring peasant named Sancho Panza as his squire, and sets out to be a knight errant. His niece, his housekeeper, the local curate, and the barber all know him well enough to be frightened for him; at the beginning they burn his books, hoping that without the source material his madness will resolve. It does not.

**Part I — The Adventures.** The structure of Part I is episodic, organized around adventures that follow a consistent pattern: Don Quixote perceives something (windmills, sheep, a barber's basin, an inn, a chain gang of prisoners) as a chivalric phenomenon; he acts on the chivalric perception; reality punishes him; he explains the punishment as enchantment or bad luck, not as a correction of the original misperception. Sancho Panza witnesses all of this with a combination of affection, exasperation, and peasant wisdom that grounds every episode.

The windmills: the most famous episode. Don Quixote charges thirty or forty windmills, which he takes for giants. His lance shatters against one sail; he and Rocinante are tumbled badly. He explains that an enchanter transformed the giants into windmills at the moment of his charge. He is not corrected; he simply continues.

The chain gang: a group of prisoners are being marched to the royal galleys by armed guards. Don Quixote, applying chivalric logic, decides that no free man should be forced against his will and liberates them. He fights the guards; the prisoners scatter. One of them, Ginés de Pasamonte — the most dangerous, intelligent, and self-aware member of the group — had a manuscript confiscated when he was arrested: his own autobiography, which he had been writing in prison. He tells Don Quixote about it. The book is unfinished, he says, because his life is not finished. He escapes with the others. The guards, retreating, still have the confiscated property including the manuscript. Ginés attacks Don Quixote and escapes. He reappears in Part II as "Maese Pedro" (Master Peter), a traveling puppet-master running a scam with a trained monkey — a rogue who has reinvented himself as a theatrical impresario.

Mambrino's Helmet: Don Quixote encounters a barber wearing a brass basin as a rain-hat. He takes it as the legendary golden helmet of Mambrino from the chivalric romances. The barber pursues him; at an inn there is a mock legal proceeding where everyone present is asked to testify to the nature of the object — basin or helmet. Don Quixote's companions play along; the barber is outmanoeuvred. The object is both basin and helmet simultaneously, depending on who is looking at it.

The interpolated stories of Part I include: Cardenio and Luscinda (a pastoral romance of jealousy and reunion), Dorotea and Don Fernando (a seduction and eventual reconciliation), the Captive Captain (a Spanish soldier captured at Lepanto, held in Algiers, escaping with Zoraida, a Moorish woman who wants to become Christian), the story of the Curious Impertinent (a Renaissance novella embedded in the main text). These are not subplots but demonstrations — each one shows a different version of the central question about fiction and reality, idealism and its consequences.

At the end of Part I, the curate and the barber capture Don Quixote by enchantment (they put him in a cage on a cart) and bring him home. He returns home still believing he is enchanted.

**Part II — The Darker Mirror.** Part II begins with the knowledge that Part I has been published and read. Don Quixote is famous; people he meets have read the book about him. This creates a doubled reality: Don Quixote's chivalric fictions are now surrounded by a theatrical reality in which educated people stage elaborate performances for their own amusement.

The Duke and Duchess: a wealthy aristocratic couple who have read Part I and invite Don Quixote and Sancho to their castle specifically to mock them by staging elaborate fake adventures. They give Sancho the governorship of a village called Barataria. Sancho governs with astonishing wisdom — he dispenses justice in peasant-logic judgments that are consistently right. The Duke's household is staging the comedy; Sancho becomes genuinely competent. He resigns eventually because governing is exhausting and he prefers the road.

The Cave of Montesinos: Don Quixote is lowered into a deep cave and has what is either a long sleep or a true vision — he reports seeing the knights of the chivalric romances, including Montesinos, Durandarte, and a Dulcinea who is temporarily enchanted into a peasant form. Sancho suspects he made it all up. Don Quixote is not sure. Cervantes introduces genuine doubt about whether his own character knows the difference between vision and dream.

Samson Carrasco: a young bachelor from Don Quixote's village, who has read Part I and decides the Don should be brought home. He disguises himself as the Knight of the Mirrors and challenges Don Quixote to single combat, intending to defeat him and make him renounce knight-errantry. He is unhorsed — Don Quixote wins by accident. Carrasco, humiliated and furious, returns as the Knight of the White Moon at Barcelona and defeats Don Quixote. He forces Don Quixote to his pledge: one year without arms, returning home. Don Quixote honors the pledge completely. He goes home. He falls ill. He recovers his sanity — he explicitly recants his belief in chivalric romances, names himself Alonso Quijano the Good, and dies. He dies sane. The sanity kills him.

**What the novel argues:** Cervantes does not decide whether Don Quixote is mad or sane, right or wrong. The novel holds both possibilities simultaneously. Don Quixote is manifestly wrong about windmills, sheep, basins, and inns. He is not wrong about honor, loyalty, courage, and the obligation to defend the weak. The world he applies his code to punishes every application; the code itself is never refuted. Sancho Panza begins as a comic foil and ends as the novel's most reliable moral compass — a man who has absorbed his master's idealism without losing his common sense. When Don Quixote dies sane, Sancho weeps and tells him not to die but to go out again as a shepherd. He is right. The novel's great sadness is that sanity and vitality are in opposition: Don Quixote alive and mad is alive; Don Quixote sane is dying.

**What stays with you:** The windmills (because everyone is the windmill in a different story). Ginés telling Don Quixote that his book is unfinished because his life is not finished. The Duke and Duchess, who are crueler than any giant. Sancho governing Barataria with unstudied wisdom. The last chapter, in which Don Quixote dies in the wrong direction — toward sense, toward death, away from Dulcinea.

### Pass 2 — Slower

**Don Quixote (Alonso Quijano)** is the figure at the novel's center but not always its moral anchor — that position is increasingly shared with Sancho. What defines Don Quixote is not delusion but commitment: he knows the world does not match the code; he applies the code anyway. This is either madness or integrity, and the novel will not decide which. His death is the novel's final argument: when the commitment is removed (by defeat, by return home, by recovery of "sense"), what is left is a man who has nothing to live for.

**Sancho Panza** begins as a fool and becomes the novel's wisdom. His peasant logic is never wrong about immediate reality and only slightly wrong about Dulcinea (he invents a response to a letter he never delivered, which seems like lying but is actually its own form of care). At Barataria he governs correctly without instruction. He weeps when Don Quixote dies and begs him not to die. He is the most reliable moral intelligence in the book by Part II.

**Ginés de Pasamonte** is the most interesting of the chain gang. He tells Don Quixote that his autobiography is better than Lazarillo de Tormes (the picaresque masterpiece), that it is unfinished because he is still alive, and that it will be finished when his life ends. He is a criminal with a literary project. He is also the character who, in Part II, becomes Maese Pedro — a theatrical impresario who stages a puppet show and runs a scam with a monkey, a man who has converted the chaos of his criminal life into a career as a professional maker of fictions. He is Don Quixote's dark reflection: a man who also lives by stories, but who knows they are stories and monetizes that knowledge. The manuscript he lost when he was arrested is his autobiography — the thing that makes his life a narrative rather than a series of events.

**Samson Carrasco** is the cruelest character in the novel, though his intentions are kind. He dresses as a knight to defeat a knight and make the knight sane. He succeeds. The knight goes home, recovers his reason, and dies. Cervantes presents this without explicit condemnation but the implication is clear: there are worse things than believing in enchantments.

**The Duke and Duchess** are the novel's darkest invention in Part II: educated, sophisticated, culturally superior people who treat human beings as theatrical props for their entertainment. They are not villains in the traditional sense. They simply have the wealth and the cruelty to stage a world in which other people's delusions are performed for their amusement. Cervantes judges them more harshly than he judges Don Quixote.

### Pass 3 — Elaborate One Scene: Ginés Describes His Book

Don Quixote has stopped the chain gang. The prisoners are being marched in chains to the galleys at Seville; Don Quixote, applying chivalric logic, has decided that no free man should be enslaved against his will and is questioning each prisoner about his crime. He reaches Ginés de Pasamonte, who is described by the guards as the most dangerous man in the group: a convicted thief with a long record, sharp-tongued, quick, contemptuous.

Don Quixote asks what his crime was. Ginés tells him.

Don Quixote asks if he has committed other crimes. Ginés says he is writing a book about them.

Don Quixote asks if it is a good book. Ginés says it is so good that Lazarillo de Tormes and all the picaresque novels of the same type can go hang. It is about real things, he says, and it is written from experience, and that is worth more than all the invention in the world.

Don Quixote asks if it is finished. Ginés says it cannot be finished while he is alive. How can he write the end of a life that has not ended?

Don Quixote asks where the book is. Ginés says the guards took it when he was arrested. He looks at the guard with the confiscated property bag.

This is the detail that matters: Ginés is not describing the book as a source of pride or as a bargaining chip. He is describing it as what it is — the record of a life still being lived, temporarily separated from the man living it. He says this to Don Quixote, who is the one person in the world most likely to understand the relationship between a man and the narrative of that man's life. Don Quixote understands completely and moves on to the next prisoner.

After the liberation, the prisoners scatter and stone Don Quixote. Ginés is the last to throw stones — he has picked up his leg-irons, which he was carrying, and throws those too. Then he runs. He does not go back for the manuscript. The guards, retreating with their confiscated property, take it with them.

The book is unfinished. The life continues. The two have been separated.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Pasamonte Pages** — Don Quixote freed the chain gang; the guards retreated to a roadside inn with the confiscated property including Ginés de Pasamonte's unfinished autobiography; Ginés has moved on as Maese Pedro; the Fighter carries the manuscript through the guard's post, past a parish priest who wants to burn it, and to Ginés's traveling puppet show before he crosses into the next province — not because Ginés is virtuous but because the manuscript belongs to the man who wrote it.

2. **Sancho's Dispatch** — During his governorship of Barataria, Sancho sent Don Quixote a dispatch describing the Duke's theatrical stagecraft; the Duke's steward intercepted it; the Fighter must retrieve the intercepted dispatch and destroy it before it reaches Don Quixote — or deliver it, and watch the knight's illusions shatter.

3. **The Basin and the Helmet** — A local magistrate has confiscated the barber's basin / Mambrino's helmet as evidence in a property dispute; the barber wants his property back; Don Quixote's companions want the matter dropped; the Fighter must carry the object to a neutral archivist before either party files a formal claim that will require a court to decide which version of the object is legally real.

4. **The Cave Transcript** — A scribe was present when Don Quixote described his Cave of Montesinos vision; the scribe wrote it down verbatim; Samson Carrasco wants the transcript destroyed before it can be used as evidence that Don Quixote's madness is deepening; the Fighter must decide whether to deliver it to the curate (who will use it to increase the commitment to bring Don Quixote home) or suppress it.

5. **Dorotea's Letter** — In the Sierra Morena, Dorotea wrote Don Fernando a letter before her reunion with him; the letter contains terms he has not met; a copy exists at the inn where the resolution was staged; if it reaches the circuit judge before the parties' stated reconciliation is registered, the reconciliation is voided; the Fighter must carry the copy to the neutral notary before Fernando's man reaches the judge.

6. **Zoraida's Madonna** — The Captive Captain's companion Zoraida carries a small Madonna figurine that was her first connection to Christianity; it was seized at the Valencian port by customs officials examining their baggage; the Fighter must recover the figurine from the customs house before the ship carrying the Captain and Zoraida departs without it — because without the figurine, Zoraida has no tangible object for her faith, and her faith is the only thing she brought from Algiers.

7. **The Knight of the White Moon's Warrant** — Samson Carrasco, as the Knight of the White Moon, has a challenge warrant that formally binds Don Quixote to terms if the combat is lost; the warrant was witnessed but the witnessing clerk has died; without the warrant's authentication, Don Quixote might later dispute the pledge; the Fighter carries the warrant to the Barcelona notary before Don Quixote departs the city.

### 5-Act Vignette Play — "The Pasamonte Pages"

**TOKEN:** The Pasamonte Pages — a leather folder of loose manuscript pages, handwritten in a tight quick hand, some water-damaged from the road; the confiscation seal of the Castilian highway guard on the cover; inside, an unfinished autobiography that begins *I, Ginés de Pasamonte, who have done things both better and worse than what is written here, set down the following account of my life not to justify it but to complete it*

---

**ACT I — The Guard's Inventory**

*The Pasamonte Pages · in a confiscated property bag · held by Sergeant Vargas, highway guard · a roadside inn on the Sierra Morena road, the morning after the chain gang liberation*

Sergeant Vargas is cataloguing the confiscated property in the inn's back room, working from a list. He is not a cruel man and he is not a bureaucratic one; he is a tired man who was attacked by a lunatic knight and pelted with leg-irons by the prisoners he had been trusted to deliver, and is now doing paperwork in a tavern that smells of wine and old straw.

The bag contains: seven sets of chains, four leg-irons, one knife taken from the prisoner Ginés de Pasamonte, and one leather folder of manuscript paper sealed with the confiscation mark.

The Fighter enters and asks for the manuscript.

Vargas looks up. He is calculating — is this person connected to yesterday's liberation? Is asking for the manuscript a sign of guilt? His expression is the expression of a man who wants to do the right thing but is not certain what the right thing is.

*His hesitation is not corruption. It is the hesitation of a man trying to determine whether releasing the manuscript is part of the problem or separate from it.*

"The man who wrote it was in that chain gang," Vargas says. He looks at the folder. "I don't know what's in it. The guard captain said it was evidence. Evidence of what, exactly, he didn't say."

The Fighter says: "Evidence of a life. Not a crime."

Vargas looks at the confiscation seal. He looks at the list. He crosses off the entry.

"Take it," he says. "Whatever it is, it's not mine."

---

**ACT II — The Road Magistrate**

*The Pasamonte Pages · sealed, confiscation mark still on the cover · held by the Fighter · a transit post on the Camino Real, La Mancha*

The transit magistrate runs his post from a stone building at the road's fork, and he has been told about the chain gang liberation, and when he sees the confiscation seal on the folder the Fighter is carrying, he does not look away.

"Property seized from escaped prisoners is subject to civil review pending adjudication of their recapture," he says. He has a ledger. He opens it. "I'll need to log this."

"The folder contains a private manuscript," the Fighter says. "It was confiscated because Ginés de Pasamonte was on a list, not because the manuscript is evidence. A man's personal papers are not the property of the crown simply because the man ran."

The magistrate looks at the confiscation seal. He looks at the ledger. He looks at the seal again.

*The law on personal papers of escaped prisoners is genuinely ambiguous. He is deciding whether ambiguity resolves toward retention or release.*

"There's a case from Toledo," the Fighter says, "where a bookseller's account ledger was returned to his family after his arrest because it was personal property, not evidence. The ruling was from 1587."

The magistrate is a man who respects prior rulings. He considers. He makes a note in the margin of the ledger.

"Pass through," he says. "I'll log it as personal effects, not inventory."

---

**ACT III — Father Augustín's Review**

*The Pasamonte Pages · under the Fighter's arm, personal-effects status logged · who holds it: the Fighter · the road between the magistrate's post and the Montiel fair track*

Father Augustín del Camino is a parish priest with a diocesan authorization to review manuscripts passing through his parish without proper clerical imprimatur. He is not a fanatic; he is methodical. He burned Don Quixote's library once — not this Father Augustín, but a priest in a village not far from here, which everyone in La Mancha knows about. He is standing in the road with two diocesan bailiffs.

"A manuscript by an unknown writer, transported without imprimatur, falls under diocesan review before it proceeds," he says. He holds out his hand. "I will return it within forty-eight hours if the contents are acceptable."

"The contents are a man's autobiography," the Fighter says. "The diocesan review right requires a filed complaint. No complaint has been filed. What you are describing is informal possession, not review — and informal possession of another person's private manuscript is a different thing from review."

Father Augustín considers this. He is a careful man. He prefers not to be wrong about jurisdiction.

*The bailiffs do not care about jurisdiction. They care about what Father Augustín's expression says next.*

His expression does not resolve quickly enough. The bailiffs move.

The road is narrow. The bailiffs are not fighters — they are administrative enforcers accustomed to compliance, not resistance. The fight is brief. Father Augustín watches with the expression of a man who has just been made responsible for something he did not intend.

"The manuscript proceeds," the Fighter says. "The diocesan complaint, if you choose to file one, should be addressed to the traveling puppet master at the Montiel fair."

Father Augustín decides not to file.

---

**ACT IV — The Road to Montiel**

*The Pasamonte Pages · intact, in the Fighter's hands, one corner scuffed from the road · who holds it: the Fighter · the fork in the Castilian road, two miles from the Montiel fair*

Word travels fast on the Camino Real, and Ginés de Pasamonte — now calling himself Maese Pedro, Master Peter, with a trained monkey and a traveling puppet theater — has heard that someone is coming with something. He is packing up. He packs up quickly. A man who has escaped a chain gang and made himself into a different person has the specific skill of knowing when to leave before having to explain why.

The Montiel fair road forks: one track is shorter, passes a tollgate watched by a constable who has been given a description matching the Fighter; the other track is longer, through a dry riverbed, adding twenty minutes.

*Ginés is breaking down his stage. Twenty minutes may be the difference.*

The Fighter takes the shorter road. The constable is at his post. The gate is the obstacle — not the constable's authority, which is thin, but the delay of being stopped and questioned at all.

The Fighter does not slow down. Forward momentum through a checkpoint, at a walk that is almost a run, with the certain expression of someone who has already been cleared — the constable starts to speak, reconsiders, does not step into the road.

The Montiel fair is ahead. The puppet stage is still standing, barely.

---

**ACT V — Maese Pedro Receives**

*The Pasamonte Pages · in the Fighter's hands, offered · held by the moment · the Montiel fair, Ginés de Pasamonte's puppet stage*

Ginés de Pasamonte looks like a different man from the one in the chain gang. He has a monkey on his shoulder and a theatrical curtain half-rolled under his arm and the specific wariness of someone who has been recognized before. He looks at the leather folder. He knows what it is. He has not looked for it — he made himself not look for it, because looking for it would have required him to be, in some sense, still the man who wrote it.

He does not take it immediately.

"That's finished," he says. Meaning the man who wrote those pages. "I'm someone else now."

He looks at the folder for a moment — at the confiscation seal, the water damage, the tight quick hand visible at the corner where the cover is not fully closed. His hand.

*He is not deciding whether to take it. He is deciding whether taking it means admitting he is still that man. The Fighter needs to give him the word that distinguishes a life unlost from a life resumed.*

"It's unfinished," the Fighter says. "You said so yourself. You can't finish it while you're still alive."

Ginés looks at the Fighter. Then he looks at the monkey, who looks back at him with the expression of a monkey that has heard everything and has opinions about none of it.

He takes the folder. He opens it to the last written page, reads two lines, closes it. He sets it in the puppet box beside the wooden figures — the kings and the knights and the damsels, the characters he uses to make other people's stories.

"I'll finish it when I'm ready," he says. Not a promise. A fact.

He picks up the puppet box.
