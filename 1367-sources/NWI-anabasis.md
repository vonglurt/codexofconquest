## Cycle 1 — Angle: *The Republic of Necessity — What holds men together when every authority has been killed*

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

Xenophon wrote the *Anabasis* in the third person, calling himself "Xenophon" throughout as though narrating someone else. He was a young Athenian gentleman, a student of Socrates, who had gone to Persia on the invitation of a friend named Proxenus, to serve as a volunteer officer in a Greek mercenary force assembled by the Persian prince Cyrus the Younger. He did not know, when he left Athens, that Cyrus was planning to overthrow his brother Artaxerxes II and seize the Persian throne. By the time he figured this out, the army was somewhere in Mesopotamia, too far from home to turn back.

The *Anabasis* — the title means "the march up," the march into the interior — is the account of what happened after everything went wrong.

**Book 1:** Cyrus assembles his force at Sardis in western Anatolia: around ten thousand Greek heavy infantry (hoplites) and cavalry under their own officers, plus a much larger Persian-allied force. The Greeks are told the march is against a local rebel called Tissaphernes. They grow suspicious as the march continues east through Cilicia and Syria and Babylonia — this is not the route to suppress a local rebel. At Tarsus the army nearly mutinies; Cyrus negotiates with each contingent. Near Babylon, the Persians finally appear. At Cunaxa, Cyrus's forces engage Artaxerxes's army. The Greek hoplites on the right wing rout the Persian troops opposite them and pursue. But in the center, Cyrus charges at his brother personally, wounds the king, and is counter-attacked and killed. The Persians never break the Greeks in the field. The Greeks have won their sector and lost the war simultaneously. Cyrus is dead. They are in the middle of Mesopotamia with no patron, no mission, and no route home.

**Book 2:** Tissaphernes approaches under flag of truce and proposes a negotiated return. The Greeks are skeptical but have few options; they agree to march back with a Persian escort. For weeks the arrangement holds. Then Tissaphernes invites the five senior Greek generals — Clearchus, Proxenus, Menon, Agias, Socrates of Achaea — to a conference. All five go. None return. They are seized, taken to Artaxerxes, and executed. Tissaphernes also sends soldiers against the Greek camp at the same moment, killing a number of officers and men.

Xenophon's portraits of the dead generals are among the most honest character studies in ancient literature. Clearchus: a man who loved war the way other men love food and sex — not cruel, but never so alive as in a crisis, and therefore always engineering crises. He was beloved and feared and had no interest in peace. Proxenus: an idealist who paid Gorgias the Sophist for an education in how to be great, and genuinely believed excellence and leadership were the same thing; he was too honest to manipulate men and therefore often failed to lead them when leadership required something other than honesty. Menon: a pure operator — Xenophon's portrait is nearly forensic in its coldness; Menon believed loyalty was a performance, used pederastic relationships as political leverage, and survived on the calculation that he was more useful alive than dead. He was tortured for a year before his execution, which Xenophon notes without apparent satisfaction and without apparent regret.

**Book 3:** The night after the generals are killed. The surviving officers are too shocked to act. The common soldiers are frightened. Xenophon, who had no command position — he was a volunteer, not a general — cannot sleep. He has a dream: lightning strikes his father's house. He wakes and reads the dream as a warning: fire is coming, but fire is also light. He rises, wakes the other surviving officers one by one, and gives the speech that saves the army.

The speech is short. Its core argument: we have two choices. We can lie here and wait to be killed or enslaved. Or we can act. If we act, we need generals. The dead generals were not elected; they held their positions by personal connection to Cyrus. We can elect new ones. We are not an army without leadership — we are an army that has not yet chosen its leaders.

The soldiers assemble before dawn. New generals are elected by vote — Timasion, Xanthicles, Cleanor, others, and Xenophon himself, replacing Proxenus. They burn the wagons, the tents, the baggage animals they cannot carry. They form a hollow square — infantry on all sides, cavalry and supply in the center — and begin marching north toward the Black Sea and home. There is no road. There is no guide. There is only the agreement that they will walk.

**Book 4:** The march north through what is now Kurdistan and eastern Turkey. The Carduchians (mountain people of the Taurus range) are not Persian allies and not Greek allies; they simply attack anyone who passes through their valleys. The fighting in the mountains is worse than anything with the Persian army — the Persians fought in formation on flat ground; the Carduchians rolled boulders from ridges and sniped from positions that Greek heavy infantry could not reach. The army crosses through at the cost of significant casualties.

In Armenia, winter falls. Snow waist-deep. Men lose toes, feet, eyes to frostbite. A condition the Greeks call *boulimia* — extreme hunger-weakness — strikes men who ate nothing for days: they fall, cannot rise, die in the snow looking alive. The soldiers learn to press on past men lying in the road, because stopping means freezing. Those who collapse are left. Some of them, Xenophon notes, are not dead — they are just past moving. When the enemy cavalry rides forward to capture them, some of the collapsed men suddenly stand and fight. The will to survive turns on when the alternative becomes specific.

Underground village houses: the locals in Armenian villages live in houses dug below the frost line, entered through holes in the ground by ladder. Livestock share the lower level. The soldiers stay three days, eating barley stored in vats with barley beer brewed in the grain. The village headman taken as guide leads them through the snow with a rope around his wrist.

At a Taochian mountain fortress, the garrison's families — women, children, old men — throw their own children over the cliff as the Greeks breach the walls, then throw themselves. The Greeks stand and watch. There is nothing to do. The wealth of the fortress — cattle, slaves, grain — is divided. No one speaks much about what they saw.

**Book 5:** Mount Theches. The rearguard hears shouting from the van. This far into the journey, unexpected shouting means an attack — the men at the rear arm themselves and run forward, fearing the van is being cut down. They find the van men embracing each other, weeping. The shout is: the sea. From the hilltop, the Black Sea is visible. The army is roughly where they started — or at least, where the sea is, the Greek world is, and they have walked there from Mesopotamia.

They pile stones into a cairn, as they have no other monument. Xenophon mentions this briefly.

Below is Trapezus — the Greek colony on the Black Sea coast, modern Trebizond. The Trapezuntines are Greeks and receive the army with something between relief and concern. Games are held: foot races, wrestling, Cretan archers, a torchlit cavalry display on the hillside. The games at Trapezus are the closest thing the army gets to a homecoming, and they know it; after this the march will become complicated again.

From Trapezus the army moves west along the Black Sea coast. Foraging parties push inland. At the stronghold of the Drilae tribe, the usual tactics fail — the Drilae set fire to their own fortress before abandoning it and the Greeks nearly get trapped in the burning ruin. The army learns discipline again through small disasters.

At Cotyora, the army meets the Mossynoecians — a people who store sliced dolphin meat in tubs and raise children fat on nuts and chestnuts as a practice of beauty, and whose king conducts state business publicly while his subjects conduct private business behind closed doors — the exact reverse of Greek custom. They make alliance against a rival clan. The alliance holds.

Sinopean ambassadors arrive and ask the army to stop plundering their hinterland. The army constitutes itself as a court of justice and tries three cases: a soldier accused of murder (acquitted), a man accused of desertion under fire (punished), a quartermasters' dispute. The army does not have a state apparatus; it becomes one when it needs to.

Xenophon proposes that the army found a new city on the Black Sea coast — a Greek colony built by veterans who have no home to return to. The idea is popular among the men who have nowhere to go. Silanus, a soothsayer, sabotages it — he tells the generals that the army will disperse if given land to settle. The colonization plan dies. Xenophon doesn't say he was right or wrong; he records that Silanus sailed home and that the army marched on.

**Book 6:** At Heraclea on the Black Sea, the army fractures into three contingents: the Arcadians break off, the Spartan-allied forces under Cheirisophus take one route, Xenophon commands what remains. The Arcadians are ambushed by Bithynian tribesmen in a night attack and badly mauled. Xenophon receives a messenger and marches through the night to reach them; the combined force fights off the pursuit. The lesson is obvious; no one draws it aloud.

Cheirisophus dies — disease or poisoned medicine, the text is not certain. Xenophon records it in one line. Cheirisophus had been the Spartan officer in nominal senior command for much of the march; his death leaves Xenophon the most prominent surviving officer, which is not a position Xenophon seems to have wanted.

The Cleander incident: Cleander is a Spartan governor visiting with authority to review the army's behavior. A soldier under Xenophon's command, Agasias, rescues a comrade from Dexippus (a deserter who had earlier fled the army with a Spartan ship). Stones are thrown. Cleander threatens to withdraw all Spartan support from the army. Xenophon manages the incident through a public concession — offering Agasias for punishment, framing it as respect for Lacedaemonian authority, watching while Cleander's anger gradually deflates. The army keeps its access to Greek ports. Xenophon does not record feeling good about this.

At the end of Book 6, soldiers bring a case against Xenophon in an impromptu assembly: he struck men during the winter march. Xenophon answers each charge individually, from memory, case by case. He says: I struck a man who lay down to die in the snow when enemies were coming — he stood up and survived. I struck a man who was stealing supplies from a sick soldier. I struck one who refused to move and threatened to stop others from moving. He asks each man he struck to stand and say if he was wronged. None stand. The assembly disbands. The issue is closed.

**Book 7:** Byzantium, the threshold of the Greek world. The army is technically inside the walls after negotiation, but the Spartan governor Anaxibius locks the gates one morning while most of the army is outside — he is trying to get rid of them. The soldiers outside storm the gates. Anaxibius threatens to fire on them. Inside the city, the army's men are beginning to sack houses. Xenophon goes to the gates and talks the soldiers down — his argument is that the Lacedaemonians control the sea and the ports and all the trade routes home, and that no army of footsoldiers, however skilled, can survive indefinitely against a naval power that chooses to deny them supply. The soldiers listen. The gates are opened. The sacking stops. Xenophon does not record whether he believed everything he said.

Seuthes, a Thracian prince trying to reclaim his family's territory, hires the army for winter operations. He promises payment in silver, cattle, and three towns: Bisanthe, Ganos, and Neontichos. The winter campaign succeeds; Seuthes gets his territory. He then refuses to pay. Xenophon sends emissaries; Seuthes stalls, offers cattle and sheep instead of the promised silver and towns. The army is paid partially and in livestock.

On the Salmydessus coast, the army comes across a beach littered with the cargo of wrecked ships. Among the debris: a mass of written books. The soldiers call it a find. The text gives no further detail. The books are not catalogued; they are simply there, scattered on the beach among wine-jars and timber.

Laconian emissaries arrive: Thibron wants the army for a campaign in Asia. This is the exit everyone has been waiting for — formal employment under recognized Greek authority. But before the handover, soldiers bring Xenophon before the Laconian commissioners to answer charges: that he enriched himself from the march, that he treated soldiers harshly, that he used the army's hardship for personal advancement. Xenophon's self-defense before the commissioners is the longest speech in Book 7 and possibly the most carefully constructed in the entire work. He addresses each charge. He calls witnesses. He names specific amounts, specific incidents, specific men. He says: I chose this march. I stayed when I could have left. I struck men to keep them alive, not to punish them. I gave away the horses I took as plunder to men who needed them more. He is not acquitted by a formal judgment; the charges simply collapse under the weight of the specifics.

He crosses to Asia. He leads a final raid against a Persian satrap named Asidates — the first attempt fails at a fortified tower; the second attempt, larger and better coordinated, captures Asidates himself along with his family and property. The army hands its captives to Thibron's commissioners. The *Anabasis* ends there, without ceremony, in the middle of an ordinary assignment.

#### Chapter Notes

- **Book 1 — The March Up**: The recruiter's lie (not told outright, merely implied by the route) and the army's willingness to keep marching anyway. The Greek hoplites win at Cunaxa. They are not defeated. They are simply abandoned by the result.
- **Book 2 — The Generals' Death**: Tissaphernes's hospitality trap is the founding catastrophe. Xenophon's portraits of the five dead generals are the book's real content — five men, five kinds of failure and courage, five lessons in what leadership costs.
- **Book 3 — The Election**: The structural pivot. A leaderless army in the middle of hostile territory votes itself a new command structure before sunrise. This is the moment the *Anabasis* becomes something other than a disaster narrative.
- **Book 4 — The Mountains**: Each obstacle in turn: the Carduchians (enemies who fight without rules), the Armenian winter (a different kind of enemy), the Taochian cliff (the nature of despair). The army learns what it can survive only by surviving it.
- **Book 5 — The Sea and the Shore**: The cry at Mount Theches is the emotional climax; everything after is a coda. The politics at Trapezus and Cotyora show the army trying to become a functioning society, which is harder than fighting.
- **Book 6 — The Army Splits**: The consequences of exhaustion and distrust. Cheirisophus's death removes the nominal Spartan authority; Xenophon is what's left. The Cleander incident is a lesson in institutional deference under pressure.
- **Book 7 — The Exit**: Byzantine politics, Seuthes's betrayal, the books on the beach, the self-defense before the commissioners, the final raid as coda. The army dissolves into normal history. Xenophon rides away.

### Pass 2 — Characters

**Xenophon** — The narrator who insists on calling himself "Xenophon" throughout, as though watching someone else. Young Athenian with a good education and no official authority, who happens to be present and awake the night everything fell apart. His method is practical: he asks what needs doing next, then does it. His speech rhythms are short, repetitive, insistent. He is not charismatic in the way Clearchus is charismatic — he persuades through specificity, through the appearance of having thought about the consequences already. He also hits men in the snow and defends it in front of a commission twenty years later and still believes he was right.

**Clearchus** — The Spartan general who loved war more than anything else. Not sadistic; simply organized around crisis. In peace, a failure; in the field, the most competent officer in the army. The men feared him enough to obey and trusted him enough to follow. He walked into Tissaphernes's tent because he didn't believe anyone would dare move against him. He was wrong once, terminally.

**Proxenus** — The idealist who invited Xenophon and got him killed — nearly. Proxenus believed that greatness was a skill that could be taught, and that being a just man was the same as being an effective commander. Xenophon liked him enormously and thought he was wrong about the second part. Proxenus could not punish his own men because he wanted them to like him; they liked him and did not quite respect him. He died at the same tent.

**Menon** — The operator. Xenophon's portrait is an outlier in the text: detailed, cold, nearly forensic. Menon believed every relationship was instrumental; he used personal attraction as political currency; he survived longer than the other generals by being more useful to Tissaphernes alive. He was tortured for a year before his execution. Xenophon does not explain why Tissaphernes kept him alive that long. The reader works it out.

**Cheirisophus** — The Spartan in nominal senior command for most of the march. Competent, institutional, not brilliant. He and Xenophon quarrel occasionally over tactics and almost never over strategy because they agree on the fundamentals. He dies before the army reaches Byzantium. The cause is uncertain. The army goes on without ceremony.

**Seuthes** — The Thracian prince who hires the army, uses it effectively, and then refuses to pay. Not a villain in the ordinary sense — he is a prince trying to reclaim territory and he correctly judged that the army needed work and could not enforce its contract from the position it was in. He paid in cattle and kept the towns. Xenophon argues with him through emissaries for weeks and receives one talent of silver at the end, which is partial payment. He doesn't pretend it was sufficient.

**The Army** — The *Anabasis* has a collective protagonist in a way ancient literature rarely allows. The army votes. The army tries cases. The army argues in assembly about whether to found a new city. The army constitutes itself as whatever institution the moment requires. It is not a democracy — it is a necessity that learned to look like one.

### Pass 3 — The Pivotal Scene: The Election Before Dawn (Book 3, Chapters 1–2)

The night after the generals are killed. The army does not know its officers are dead yet — they know they haven't returned, which is the same thing. The soldiers sit in their companies, not sleeping, not organizing. Xenophon can't sleep either. He gets up, gathers the surviving senior officers one by one, and brings them to a spot between the watchfires.

He says: Look at yourselves. You are lying here waiting for something to happen. Nothing is going to happen unless you make it happen. The generals are dead. We can elect new ones. We have done everything we needed to do today — we haven't been beaten, we haven't run, the men are still here. What we have to do now is decide to keep existing as an army. That decision is ours. No one is going to make it for us.

He is twenty-seven years old, approximately. He has no formal rank. He is technically a guest.

The officers listen. They call a full assembly. Before dawn, the men vote. New generals are elected company by company. Xenophon is elected to replace Proxenus.

Then comes the second speech — to the full army this time. Its core:

We are not worse off than the men who founded cities in strange places, who sailed from Greece with nothing but ships and came back with civilizations. We are not worse off than people who have always been where they are and have never had to choose to be anywhere. We chose to be here. That makes us, in some sense, free — not because nothing is constraining us, but because we are the constraint. We are the army. We decide what the army does.

The speech has a practical dimension — he tells them to burn the baggage, reduce weight, organize the hollow square. But the practical dimension follows from the philosophical one. The argument is: you are not waiting to be rescued. You are the rescue.

Then a detail that Xenophon includes without comment: just as the first speech was ending, a soldier sneezed. Sneezing was an omen in Greek tradition — specifically, a sneeze at a decisive moment was read as divine affirmation. Every man in the assembly heard it. Every man knew what it meant. They stamped their feet and called out the name of Zeus the Savior.

The sneeze is not in any other ancient history. It is in this one because Xenophon was there and it happened and he thought it was important. He doesn't editorialize. He records it and goes on.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **"The Rearguard's Log"** — The army kept daily records: distance marched, terrain encountered, engagements fought, supplies taken or lost. A relay of scribes and under-officers maintained the running account; Xenophon drew on it when writing his history. A partial copy of the original day-log for the mountain crossing through Kurdistan — a different document from the literary Anabasis — survives in a Pontic monastery. A fighter escorts it from the monastery at Trabzon to the Weimar archive before a planned demolition disturbs the library.

2. **"Proxenus's Letter"** — Proxenus wrote the letter that brought Xenophon to Persia. The letter is never quoted in the Anabasis — only its result is described. A Boeotian monastery near Proxenus's home city of Orchomenos has in its archive a collection of letters from a noble family of that city. One of the letters, filed under private correspondence from the 390s BC, may be a later copy of that invitation. Fighter escorts it from Orchomenos to Weimar.

3. **"The Oath at Calpe"** — Tissaphernes swore sacred oaths by the gods of Greece and Persia that the generals were safe at his tent. The diplomatic formula of such oaths was standardized. A Persian clay tablet in a Venetian collection, acquired from a 9th-century Arab library, contains what appears to be the Persian-language formula for this class of safe-conduct oath. The dating and provenance make it the type of oath Tissaphernes would have used. Fighter escorts the tablet.

4. **"The Salmydessus Books"** — The Thracian coast at Salmydessus was notorious for shipwrecks, which the coastal Thracians encouraged. When the army passed through in winter, they found on the beach the cargo of wrecked ships, including a large number of written books. A catalog of a Thracian harbor master's salvage — drawn up a generation after — lists among recovered cargo six crates of writing material from a single wreck. One item is described as a Greek text in seven sections. Fighter escorts the catalog from a Varna archive to Weimar.

5. **"The Cretan's Account"** — The Cretan archers were in the van at Mount Theches when the first shout went up. An old soldier who had served as a Cretan archer and later settled at Sinope dictated, in old age, a brief account of what he saw from the front rank: the shape of the hill, the change in light, the moment he understood. The account was preserved by a Sinopean merchant family. Fighter escorts it to Weimar before the family sells it to a Constantinople collector.

6. **"The Scillus Deed"** — After the march, Xenophon settled at Scillus near Olympia, where the Spartans gave him an estate. He built a small temple to Artemis there, with a stone inscription describing the estate's grant and obligations. A traveler's account from the 2nd century AD describes seeing the inscription still standing. A Peloponnesian monastery's foundation records contain a survey of ancient inscriptions in the area; among them is a transcription of the Scillus stone. Fighter escorts the survey from the monastery to a neutral archive.

7. **"The Towns That Were Not Given"** — Seuthes promised the Greek army three Thracian coastal towns as permanent settlement if they completed his winter campaign. They completed it. He kept the towns. A treaty document, if it ever existed, would have recorded both the promise and the breach. A Thracian noble family near the Bosphorus holds a collection of old territorial records; one of them may be a written record of the promised grant, never honored. In 1367 this document is worth money to someone — the towns are still there, still valuable. Fighter must get the document to a neutral archive before the family sells it or it disappears.

## Theme Check

**Existing themes to avoid**: loyalty under long watch; grief in an object; honor vs. shame; love and fate; posthumous truth; healing through hostile law; the hunted carrier; ancient witnesses; corruption/mercy; fate through broken things; heroism without shelter; invitation as weapon; the ransoming poem; the one who won't weep; love refusing loss; the martyr's death-command; the prophecy that changes nothing; the original fire; anonymous reparation; the compelled traitor; the silenced victim; the cracked relic; the enemy's timing strategy; the condemned's right to be heard; the impossible witness; the champion's erased near-disaster; mercy-before-the-seal; the son's flight; authorship as claim; the wound as witness; divine vs. human record; the debt to the dead; the last just act; the bound man's incomplete transmission; the enemy made your monument.

**NWI theme chosen**: *An army without a patron becomes its own republic; what holds it together is the vote — the willingness of strangers to choose each other when every hierarchy has failed.*

This is distinct from all above themes. The nearest neighbors are "the last just act" (final moment of institutional function) and "the bound man's incomplete transmission" (obligation carried past its authority) but neither covers: the spontaneous reconstitution of authority by the governed; the moment leadership passes from appointment to election under mortal pressure; the army that decides it exists rather than waiting to be told it does.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: *What the Sea Knows*

### The Token

*The Rearguard's Leaf — a single vellum leaf cut from a 12th-century Pontic monastery copy of the Anabasis. The leaf covers one chapter: the approach to the high ridge above Trabzon, the confusion among the rearguard when the shouting reached them, the discovery that the shout was the sea. The leaf is kept folded inside a cedar board binding held shut by a leather cord. The monastery burned in 1349; this leaf was among materials salvaged and brought to Constantinople. It belongs in Trebizond — the city that was there when they arrived.*

---

**ACT I**

*The Rearguard's Leaf — still in its cedar boards — on the reading table of Demetrios Kydones, Constantinople, late afternoon.*

Kydones is fifty-two, a theologian and diplomat who has spent years trying to reconcile the Greek church with Rome and mostly failed. He has a large library and a practiced understanding of what is about to be lost. Constantinople is not falling today or this year, but the walls are old and the treasury is empty and anyone with a library and a long view is thinking about distribution. The leaf in front of him describes an army finding the sea from the high ground above Trebizond — the same city that still stands. Trebizond has an archive. The leaf belongs there. Kydones cannot make the journey; he is under observation by factions in the patriarchate who consider his Latin sympathies a problem.

He hired the Fighter through a mutual contact on the Genoese side of the harbor. He wants the leaf in the Trebizond archive before anyone who objects to his activities can inventory his library and confiscate what he has.

The Genoese inspect departing cargo at the Bosphorus ferry station. The leaf is small but the cedar boards are distinctive.

*— The inspector looks at things by weight and shape. The leaf weighs nothing. The boards look like a book.*

> **Skill Check: Deception DC 12.** Convince the Genoese dock inspector that the boards contain personal correspondence of no commercial value. The inspector knows what a manuscript board looks like; the question is whether the penalty for lying to a Genoese freight agent matters less than the penalty for letting a notable scholar's property leave without assessment.

**Fail:** The inspector holds the boards for a fee — double the standard rate for assessed manuscripts. The Fighter pays or negotiates; the leaf passes regardless, but the departure is noted in the harbor log.

**Pass:** The inspector marks the item as personal effects and waves the ferry through. The Bosphorus crossing is gray and choppy. The Anatolian coast comes up from the south.

**TOKEN GRANTED: The Rearguard's Leaf** — single vellum leaf inside cedar boards tied with a leather cord, describing the approach to the hill above Trabzon and the sound of the army crying out when the first men in the van saw the sea.

---

**ACT II**

*The Rearguard's Leaf — in the Fighter's pack — at a coastal caravanserai on the Black Sea road, three days east of the Bosphorus.*

A Venetian collector's agent named Marco Zane has been in Trebizond for months acquiring manuscripts from the failing archive of the local church. He was on his way back to Venice when he heard, through the Genoese harbor network, that a notable Constantinople scholar had dispatched something to Trebizond by private courier. Zane makes his living knowing what scholars send where. He waited at this caravanserai.

He approaches the Fighter at the evening meal with the politeness of a man who has money and knows how to spend it. He is not threatening. He says: whatever you are carrying, I will pay twice its assessed value, no questions. He believes manuscript material belongs to whoever can afford to keep it, and he has kept a great deal of it very well in a house in Venice. He is not wrong that Venice has a good archive. He is wrong that the exchange is simple.

*— He thinks the leaf belongs to whoever arrives with it. He has not thought about where it is going and why.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 12.** Explain to Zane that the leaf's destination is not a matter of possession — it is a matter of completing something the text describes. The army that cried out on the hill above Trabzon was looking at the city where the leaf is going. Moving it to Venice breaks something the document has been trying to say for seventeen hundred years.

**Fail:** Zane does not understand this argument and thinks it is theater. He increases his offer. He is not going to assault a Fighter in a caravanserai, but he will send word ahead to Trebizond's harbor agent to flag all incoming couriers. The Fighter arrives in Trebizond with the harbor watching.

**Pass:** Zane sits with the argument for a while. Then: "The archive there is not doing well." "I know." He thinks. "Is it at least going to survive?" "I don't know." He looks at his hands. "I have kept a great deal of things very well." "I know you have." He stands up and walks back to his room without making a counteroffer. In the morning, he is gone.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT III**

*The Rearguard's Leaf — in the Fighter's pack — on the mountain road above Trabzon, the final approach.*

The route from the coast up to the plateau above Trabzon is the same route the army used coming down. The geography matches the text: a ridge, a view, a descent. Brigands use the road regularly; travelers moving alone or in small groups are targets.

Four of them are waiting at the path's narrowest point, where a cliff on one side drops to a stream and a rock face on the other leaves no room to move around. They want the pack. They have not yet identified it as unusual cargo.

*— The pass is narrow enough that they cannot flank and cannot run. Neither can the Fighter.*

> **Combat: 4 Brigands** — AC 12, HP 14 each. Armed with handaxes and short bows; the two with bows are positioned at the back of the group, which means the front two must be dealt with before the shooters become the primary problem.

After combat, the Fighter pauses at the top of the ridge. The city is below. The water is visible past it.

*— Every soldier in the van saw it from approximately here. That was the moment.*

> **Skill Check: Perception DC 10.** Read the terrain — confirm the route down into the city, note the harbor, locate the archive district near the imperial palace quarter.

**Pass or Fail:** The way down is clear either way. The skill check determines whether the Fighter enters the city knowing the layout or having to ask.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT IV**

*The Rearguard's Leaf — in the Fighter's pack — at the gate of the imperial archive, Trebizond, morning.*

The archive is in the lower palace complex. Its keeper is a deacon named Bardas, who has been detained for two weeks pending review of his correspondence with a Pontic merchant family. The detention may be political; the charge involves questions about what Bardas has been sending to Constantinople and who is paying him to send it. The archive's door is locked. The deputy left in notional charge is a young monk named Ioannes, twenty-three, who has been sitting in the anteroom since the detention started and has not accepted a single item.

Ioannes opens the door a crack when the Fighter knocks. He listens to the explanation. He says, carefully, that he has no authority to accept items without Bardas's counter-signature. He says this the way a person says something they know to be technically true and practically wrong.

*— He is not afraid of making the wrong decision. He is afraid of making any decision at all.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 13.** Convince Ioannes that the leaf will not be here when Bardas is released if he does not take it now — it will be somewhere in the city or lost or in Venetian hands — and that accepting a document into provisional custody, clearly noted as pending Bardas's formal review, is not the same as making an archival decision. It is delaying the decision, which is what Ioannes already knows how to do.

**Fail:** Ioannes apologizes and closes the door. He does not latch it. The Fighter must find a senior church official — the metropolitan or a senior palace chaplain — to provide counter-authority. This adds a day and a separate negotiation.

**Pass:** Ioannes opens the door fully. He gets paper and a quill. He writes, in very careful letters, a provisional receipt noting the item, the date, the provenance, and the phrase "held pending the return of the archive's keeper." He signs it with his name and his position — which is not, strictly speaking, an official position, but it is the name of the person present. He takes the cedar boards. He does not open them.

"Should I look at it?" he asks.

The Fighter says yes.

He unties the leather cord. He reads the leaf without speaking. He reads it again.

"Is this the city?" he asks.

"Yes."

He nods. He closes the boards and carries them to a shelf.

**TOKEN CHECKED.**

---

**ACT V**

*The Rearguard's Leaf — in Ioannes's custody — in the archive room, Trebizond.*

The archive room smells of old wood and tallow and the salt air that comes through the window shutters from the harbor below. Ioannes has placed the cedar boards on the intake shelf with his provisional receipt folded and tucked under the cord. He is at the table, writing the log entry in his careful hand.

He stops writing.

"I read the text," he says. "Not the whole thing — only the part about the hill." He looks at the boards. "They were so far from here and they walked back." He doesn't say the rest of it. He doesn't need to.

He dips his pen and finishes the log entry. He closes the logbook. He says, without looking up: "Bardas will want to see it properly. I'll make sure it stays in the provisional category until he can complete the intake." He pauses. "The people who were shouting — did they make it all the way home?"

The Fighter says: most of them.

Ioannes nods slowly, like someone filing an answer that confirms what they already thought.

He sets his pen down. The intake is complete.

**TOKEN TAKEN: The Rearguard's Leaf**

The cedar boards rest on the intake shelf of the Trebizond archive. Ioannes's provisional receipt is tucked under the cord. The leaf inside describes the city visible through the window.

## Cycle 2 — Angle: *The Towns That Were Not Given — The Contract Kept and Broken*

**What this angle reveals that cycle 1 missed**: Cycle 1 showed the army reconstituting itself through collective will — the election that made ten thousand leaderless men into a self-governing republic. Cycle 2 shows what came after they reached home: they completed their final obligation — Seuthes's winter campaign — and Seuthes kept the three promised towns. The document that records the grant is not the army's victory; it is the breach. In 1367, the towns are still there, still valuable, which is why someone wants the document to disappear, and why it must reach a neutral archive before it becomes either a political weapon or ash.

---

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Towns That Were Not Given*

#### The Record

*The Seuthes Grant Record — a Hellenistic parchment copy of the original Thracian grant, the original issued on wooden tablet in 399 BC — specifying three named towns on the Thracian coast as permanent settlement for the Greek army in exchange for one season's winter campaign; the reverse side carries a Thracian notation in an older hand; the family seal — a stag, worn — on the exterior fold. Held for six generations by a Thracian noble family near the Bosphorus, who found it in their grandfather's territorial records and recognized it as either a significant historical document or a significant liability.*

---

**ACT I**

*The Seuthes Grant Record — in a cedar chest in the family archive room — Bosphorus, Thracian noble estate, morning.*

The family steward's name is Dragomir. He is sixty, careful, and has been managing this family's archive since he was appointed at thirty. He located the grant record four months ago while cataloguing the old territorial files. He has spent those four months deciding whether to sell it, destroy it, or release it to a neutral carrier. He has chosen the carrier because selling it would make one party very powerful and destroying it would make that same party very comfortable.

He sets the parchment on the table between you. He does not touch it again.

"The army completed the campaign. Seuthes kept the towns. The Greeks went home to cities that didn't want them anymore. The towns are still there." He nods toward the document. "In 1367, this is the kind of thing that starts a claim. I am not giving it to a claimant. I am giving it to someone who will put it where it cannot be used."

*— He has already decided. He needs to know you understand the difference between a neutral carrier and an agent.*

> **Dialogue DC 12.** Tell Dragomir what distinguishes a neutral carrier from someone who carries for a principal — that the archive is the destination, not a step toward a use; that the record of a breach belongs in the record, not in anyone's file cabinet.

**Fail:** Dragomir sets his hand flat on the parchment. "Not yet. Come back tomorrow — I want to sleep on it again." He is not changing his decision; he is testing whether you'll wait.

**Pass:** He slides the parchment across. He adds the family seal impression to the exterior fold's wax: a notation in his hand — *released to neutral carrier, Thracian noble family archive, Dragomir steward, for delivery to neutral archive only.* "Tell no one where you got it until it's in the box."

**TOKEN GRANTED: Seuthes Grant Record** — Hellenistic parchment copy, family wax seal, Dragomir's carrier notation; reverse: Thracian notation in older hand; the promise and its non-fulfillment in the same document.

---

**ACT II**

*The Seuthes Grant Record — in the Fighter's satchel — Constantinople harbor, morning.*

Nikolaos is a Byzantine fiscal agent stationed at the harbor manifests desk. He reviews departing cargo from the Thracian coast region, specifically looking for territorial documents that may affect Byzantine coastal claims. He sees the family seal. He knows the Thracian noble families near the Bosphorus; he has been tracking their archive activity for two years.

He opens his hands on the desk.

"Territorial grant document from the Thracian coastal zone. This falls under the Byzantine imperial review protocol for documents affecting littoral claims." He is not unfriendly. He is a bureaucrat who has found a category. "I'll need to log it and hold it for review. Standard forty-day process."

*— He found the right category, but it is the wrong category. This is not a current territorial claim; it is a historical record of a contract made 1,770 years ago and never honored.*

> **Skill Check: History DC 12.** Establish that the Seuthes Grant Record is a pre-Byzantine mercenary service contract from 399 BC, predating Byzantine administration of the Thracian coast by more than seven centuries; it records a promise made by a Thracian king to a Greek mercenary army and broken by the same king; it is not a current territorial claim and carries no force against present landholders.

**Fail:** Nikolaos logs it as "territorial document pending review" and issues a forty-day hold slip. The Fighter waits in Constantinople while the review request is forwarded and returns empty. He accepts the historical argument by the third week.

**Pass:** Nikolaos considers. He is also a historian by training; he recognizes when he has misapplied a category. He produces a transit slip: *historical mercenary service record, pre-Byzantine, 399 BC, no current territorial force — transit cleared.* He attaches it to the exterior fold.

**TOKEN STATE 2**: Byzantine fiscal agent's transit clearance slip attached — *historical mercenary service record, pre-Byzantine, no current territorial force.*

---

**ACT III**

*The Seuthes Grant Record — fold with two notations — Ragusa waystation inn, evening.*

Ser Marco has been following the document since it left the estate. He is a Venetian-backed land speculator with interests in the Thracian coastal trade. He is patient, well-dressed, and has a lawyer's instinct for leverage. He sits across from the Fighter at the inn table with an offer already written on a folded paper.

"A document that establishes a historical claim to three towns, even a lapsed claim, is valuable as precedent. Not as law — I know it is 1,770 years old. As precedent. As argument. Someone with money and a persuasive advocate could make interesting use of this." He unfolds the paper. The number is significant. "I will not use it in a way that harms anyone living."

*— He means it. That is almost the problem. A legitimate speculative use is still a use, and the document is going to a neutral archive, not into anyone's argument.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 13.** Decline Ser Marco's offer in a way that forecloses his legal options — the document is already in transit under a chain of custody established at two prior points; a Ragusa notary witness will confirm the chain; Ser Marco cannot establish a prior interest because no prior interest exists; the document was held by the family as historical patrimony, not commercial inventory.

**Fail:** Ser Marco smiles, folds the offer back, and thanks the Fighter for the education. He calls the notary anyway. The notary confirms the chain of custody. Ser Marco's legal avenue closes at dawn; he accepts it.

**Pass:** Ser Marco closes the offer paper. He accepts the argument — he knows when an avenue is closed. "At least let the archive know I made this offer and declined to pursue it. I want that on record." The Ragusa notary public, present throughout the exchange, adds an endorsement: *chain-of-custody confirmed, no competing prior claim established; Ser Marco of Venice formally declined to pursue.*

**TOKEN STATE 3**: Ragusa notary endorsement added — *chain-of-custody confirmed, no competing prior claim established.*

---

**ACT IV**

*The Seuthes Grant Record — three notations on exterior — road north from Ragusa, night.*

Ser Marco's offer was genuine. His hired men are also genuine. He declined the legal avenue at dawn and instructed the men by nightfall; they have been riding since.

*— Four notations on the document. Three of them are defense. None of them stop hired men.*

> **Combat: 2 opponents — AC 13, HP 22 each.** They want the satchel and will stop at half health if the road north looks expensive.

**Fail:** They get the satchel. The Ragusa civic guard takes a theft report; Ser Marco's name is in the notary endorsement, which makes the report actionable.

**Pass:** Both down or broken. Road north clear. Three notations intact.

**TOKEN STATE 4**: Carried through. All notations undamaged.

---

**ACT V**

*The Seuthes Grant Record — four notations on exterior — Weimar archive, the desk of Archivus Sweelinck, morning.*

Sweelinck reads the parchment. He knows the *Anabasis*. He knows Seuthes. He reads the grant carefully, both sides.

"The army completed the campaign." He sets it down. "Seuthes kept the towns." A pause. "The wrong party is 1,770 years dead. The towns are still there. The contract is legible." He picks up his pen. "The question is what category a fulfilled obligation with an unfulfilled payment belongs in."

*— He has the right question. He needs to know which party the file goes under.*

> **Skill Check: History DC 11.** Tell Sweelinck that the file belongs under the army — the party that kept its word; not under the towns, which are a geographic index entry, and not under Seuthes, who is the breaching party; the record of what ten thousand men earned and were not paid is an army record, not a territorial record.

**Fail:** Sweelinck files it under "Historical Territorial Grants — Disputed" and marks it pending. He consults the classification records. He returns with the same argument the Fighter made an hour later; the entry is updated.

**Pass:** Sweelinck writes a new entry header: *Greek Mercenary Service Records — Xenophon's March, Territorial Promise, 399 BC; filed under the army, not the towns.* He enters the three town names as geographic cross-references only, not primary subjects. He files the parchment.

He says, without looking up:

"The army walked home. The towns stayed where they were. The archive will show what the army was owed."

**TOKEN TAKEN: Seuthes Grant Record**

The parchment enters the Weimar archive under Greek Mercenary Service Records. The family seal, the Byzantine transit clearance, and the Ragusa notary endorsement are indexed as chain-of-custody. The three towns appear as geographic cross-references with a notation: *promised, undelivered, 399 BC.*

## Cycle 3 — Angle: *The Cretan's Account — What the Front Rank Saw*

**What this angle reveals that prior cycles missed:** Cycle 1 showed the army's collective will — the vote that reconstituted the republic of necessity. Cycle 2 showed the breach — the compact honored by one side and broken by the other. Cycle 3 enters the moment itself: the first shout. An old Cretan archer was in the van at Mount Theches. He dictated, in old age, what he saw from the front rank: the shape of the hill, the change in light, the moment he understood before the shout spread backward through ten thousand men. **New theme:** *The front-rank view of communal salvation is different from the literary account; the archer who first understood what the shout meant carries a kind of knowledge the writer who came later cannot reconstruct; the moment of collective recognition has a specific shape when you are the first one to know.*

Kept in a cedar box by a Sinopean merchant family for thirty years.*

**ACT I — The Merchant's Son**

*The fighter understands: Luca's offer is specific and immediate. The Weimar commission is general and institutional. The fighter must make institutional custody feel more solid than Genoese coin, which is the harder argument.*

Luca is not hostile. He is efficient. He has been in this situation before — a foreign commission arriving two days after his purchase offer. He knows the institutional argument and he has a counter: his collector will publish a printed edition, make the account publicly available, and name Konstantinos's family in the dedication. The archive will file it and it will be consulted by perhaps six scholars in twenty years.

He is not wrong about the publication. He is wrong about one thing, which the fighter must identify and name.

*What Luca cannot offer is the one thing an archive can: a place where the account cannot be bought again, sold again, or destroyed by a collector's heir who does not share his father's interests.*

---

**ACT III — The Scholar's Addendum**

*The Dictation, in the fighter's satchel · a Constantinople scholar's study, two weeks later · evening, the Bosphorus visible from the window*

His name is Nikephoros Mauros. He teaches at the Chora studium and has published a commentary on the Anabasis that is careful, thorough, and missing the front rank's view entirely — because it did not exist when he wrote it. He has heard the account exists from a colleague in Sinope. He wants to add a paragraph to the next edition of his commentary.

If the fighter shows him the account, he will write the paragraph and attribute it to the Weimar archive — effectively announcing the archive's acquisition to the scholarly world before the archive has received it. This is useful. It is also premature.

The tender moment: Nikephoros reads the account's description of the sound — the shout coming from the front and keeping coming — and sets the folio down. He says: "He was listening to it travel." He says nothing else for a moment.

*The fighter watches a scholar understand, for the first time in his career, what his commentary was missing.*

---

**ACT IV — The Retired Soldier**

*The Dictation, in the satchel · a Constantinople tavern, the same evening · a man named Alexios who sells grain and used to be a soldier*

Alexios has read the Anabasis. He read it twenty years ago when he was a soldier and has thought about it intermittently since. He never served in Anatolia — he served in Thrace — but he has always wondered what the front rank saw at Theches, because Xenophon was in the middle of the column and described it from the middle.

He does not know the fighter has the account. He is simply telling the fighter this because it came up, the way things come up in taverns when two people have been talking long enough.

The fighter opens the satchel.

*There is a particular satisfaction in carrying the answer to a question someone asks without knowing you have it.*

---

**ACT V — The Specific Details**

*The Dictation, delivered · on the Weimar archive intake desk · morning*

The archivist reads the account. He pauses at the passage about the sound — the shout coming from the front and keeping coming. He reads it again.

Then he says: "The chain of custody is commercial. A merchant family, not a monastery." He is not questioning the document. He is noting that the preservation was private, which means the record of its preservation belongs in the archive entry.

He asks the fighter to describe the cedar box: its dimensions, its lock, whether the box had any inscription or maker's mark. He writes the description down. "The box is also part of the record," he says. "Everything that held it."

He files the account under: *Eyewitness testimony — military, classical period. Front-rank perspective, Mount Theches, circa 401 BC.*

*The shape of the hill. The change in light. The sound coming from the front and keeping coming. It is in the archive. The specific details are preserved exactly as the old archer gave them.*

## Cycle 4 — Angle: *Proxenus's Letter — The Invitation That Made the Text Possible*

**What this angle reveals that prior cycles missed:** Cycle 1 showed collective self-governance. Cycle 2 showed the broken compact. Cycle 3 showed the front-rank physical experience. Cycle 4 enters the origin: the letter that recruited Xenophon. Proxenus died before the march was half over — he was one of the generals Tissaphernes executed under the false flag of peace. His letter to Xenophon is the only surviving document from his hand, and it is not a military document; it is a private recommendation. **New theme:** *The letter of invitation is the first act of the text it makes possible; Proxenus recruited the man who would write the Anabasis without knowing he was doing so; the invitation is a form of authorship that predates and enables the account it produced.*
