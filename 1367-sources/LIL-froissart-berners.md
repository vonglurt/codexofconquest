## Cycle 1 — Angle: The architecture of mercy before the irrevocable order

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

Froissart's Chronicles is the great monument of 14th-century European history — not a dry annalistic account but a vivid narrative chronicle, warm with reported speech, eyewitness accounts, and the chronicler's own relentless curiosity. Jean Froissart (c. 1337–1405) was a court poet turned historian who spent decades traveling through England, Scotland, France, Flanders, and Spain, gathering accounts from the men who had participated in the events he describes. He served at the courts of Queen Philippa of Hainault (England's queen consort), Wenceslas of Luxembourg, and Gaston Fébus, Count of Foix. The Chronicles, written primarily in the 1370s through the 1390s and revised several times, cover the period 1327–1400 — essentially the first half of the Hundred Years' War, from the beginning of Edward III's active reign through the deposition of Richard II.

Lord Berners's translation (1523–1525) is the great English rendering: direct, vigorous, with an aristocratic ease that belongs to a translator who understood that he was recording the world his own class had recently left. The Harvard Classics selection in the file draws from the most famous passages — Crécy, Calais, Poitiers, the Black Prince, the Peasants' Revolt, Richard II — edited by Macaulay for a general reading audience in the early 20th century.

**The Hundred Years' War opens.** Edward III of England is the grandson of Philip IV of France through his mother Isabella. When the French throne becomes vacant, Edward's claim exists but the French apply Salic law to exclude inheritance through the female line. Edward disputes it; war follows. The initial advantage is at sea: Sluys (1340) gives England control of the Channel. The war then moves to Flanders and eventually to France proper.

**Crécy (1346).** Edward's army crosses the Channel and marches through Normandy. Philip VI of France assembles a much larger army and gives chase. They meet at Crécy-en-Ponthieu. The English longbowmen stand in three battles on a sloping field; the French cavalry charge sixteen times. The Genoese crossbowmen hired by France cannot match the English rate of fire and break; the French knights ride through their own crossbowmen. Each successive cavalry charge dies on the same arrows. Five thousand French knights are counted dead the next morning. The Black Prince — sixteen years old, commanding the right battle — is pressed hard enough that his standard-bearer sends to the king for reinforcement. Edward asks: is my son dead or felled to the earth? No, the messenger says, he needs help. Edward says: "Return to him and to those that sent you, and say to them that they suffer him this day to win his spurs, for I am determined, if God be pleased, that this journey shall be his." The prince holds. Philip VI is wounded twice and flees the field.

**Calais (1346–47).** Edward lays siege to Calais immediately after Crécy. The city is well-supplied, well-fortified, and commanded by Jean de Vienne — a capable governor who holds out for eleven months. The siege is organized as a war city, with English merchants supplying the camp; it becomes the longest operation of the war to this point. Philip arrives with a relief army the following summer but refuses to attack the English position and withdraws without engaging. Calais has no choice: it surrenders.

Edward's terms: six of the most prominent citizens of Calais must come out of the gates barefoot and bare-headed, in their shirts, with halter ropes around their own necks, carrying the city's keys. He intends to execute them as a lesson about the cost of sustained resistance. The six burghers — Eustache de Saint-Pierre, Jean d'Aire, and four others — volunteer rather than have the city determine by lot who will die. They put on the halters themselves. They come out of the gate in silence, in the posture of absolute submission, carrying the keys between them, and walk to where Edward waits with his council. The English army watches.

Queen Philippa of Hainault is in the camp. She is seven months pregnant. She comes before her husband, kneels before him in front of the entire army, and petitions for the six men's lives. She asks for them as her gift — "for the sake of the Son of God, and out of love of me, be pleased to have mercy upon them." She does not argue policy or precedent. She asks as a wife.

Edward says: "I wish with all my heart that you had not been here today, for you have put me in a position such that I cannot refuse you."

He releases the six burghers to her. She gives them food and clothes and money for their journey home.

The scene is the most famous moment in Froissart because it is the most humanly legible. What it turns on is Edward's line. "I cannot refuse you" is not the language of a king bowing to a queen's emotional appeal. It is the political mechanism by which a king can reverse a public vow without dishonor: the mercy becomes a gift to the queen rather than a capitulation to the condemned. Philippa designed this. She understood that if she knelt before her husband publicly, and if he could frame the release as something he was giving her rather than something he was backing down on, the moment would be preserved as an act of grace rather than an act of weakness. She needed the petition to precede the irreversible order; she needed him to be able to say, truthfully, that he was granting what was asked before the order became final.

**The Black Prince at Poitiers (1356).** Edward of Woodstock — the Black Prince, now a seasoned commander — leads an English army in France. He is outnumbered roughly three to one by King John II of France. He attempts to negotiate; John's advisers refuse the terms. The battle is joined on ground the English have chosen. The longbowmen anchor the flanks; the dismounted English knights hold the center. The French cavalry charges are broken; the French infantry presses forward; King John fights in the front rank and is captured alive along with his youngest son Philip (who fought beside him throughout). The prince takes him to his tent and serves him personally at dinner that evening, refusing to sit at the same table: "It would not be proper for me to sit at table with so great a prince as yourself." The captured king of France thanks him. This is the moment the chivalric code was designed to produce — and it worked.

**The Peasants' Revolt (1381).** Richard II is fourteen years old when Wat Tyler leads the commons of England into London, burning the Savoy Palace, opening the prisons, demanding the abolition of serfdom and poll taxes. The situation spins beyond control; Richard rides out to meet the rebels at Mile End, makes promises he will not keep. At Smithfield, Tyler rides up to the king in an insolent posture; William Walworth, the Lord Mayor of London, stabs Tyler and has him dragged from his horse. Tyler dies. The rebel army sees him fall and nocks arrows. Richard rides alone toward the crowd — fourteen years old, on horseback, no visible guard — and says: "Sirs, will you shoot your king? I am your captain. Follow me." He rides; the crowd follows him; the revolt collapses. Froissart records this correctly but does not entirely understand what he has witnessed — his sympathies are with the court, and the rebels' legitimate grievances about poll taxes and serfdom remain opaque to him.

**Richard II's Deposition.** Richard's reign deteriorates over fifteen years: royal favorites, political miscalculation, the quarrel with the magnates, the Irish campaigns. Henry Bolingbroke — his cousin, exiled without proper legal process — returns from France with an army. England turns to him with alarming speed. Richard is caught returning from Ireland, taken to Flint Castle, and surrenders there. Henry receives him. They ride to London. Richard formally abdicates or is formally deposed — the distinction matters to later claimants and is not fully resolved by Froissart. He dies in captivity at Pontefract Castle the following year, probably of starvation.

Froissart ends his chronicle with unease. He has watched the finest chivalric moment of his era — the Black Prince at Poitiers, the gesture at the dinner table — and watched the same political world produce Richard's deposition and death. He cannot entirely reconcile these observations. The Chronicles do not have a conclusion that resolves them; they simply stop.

**What the Chronicles argue:** That the 14th century was the last moment in which the chivalric ideology and the chivalric practice could be said to describe the same world — however imperfectly, however hypocritically in some men's hands. Crécy proves the longbow makes cavalry charges suicidal; the code that requires cavalry charges is therefore moving toward obsolescence with every battle. The Peasants' Revolt proves that the social order the knights are sworn to protect is fragile and maintained by force more than by right. Richard's deposition proves that a king can be removed by sufficient political will. Froissart records all of this without fully processing it. That is what makes the Chronicles indispensable — the recorder's eye is more honest than the recorder's politics.

**Chapter Notes**

- **Sluys (1340):** Naval battle establishing English Channel control — strategic prelude to the land campaigns.
- **Crécy (1346):** Longbow dismantles cavalry; Black Prince earns his spurs; Edward's deliberate refusal to reinforce his son.
- **Calais (1346–47):** Year-long siege; six burghers with halters; Philippa's intercession; Edward's face-saving release. The chronicle's emotional center.
- **Poitiers (1356):** Black Prince captures John II; the dinner-table courtesy; chivalric ideal fully expressed.
- **Gaston Fébus visit:** Froissart at the court of the Count of Foix — the most vivid personal portrait in the Chronicles; a man of great culture and controlled brutality.
- **Peasants' Revolt (1381):** Tyler's death; Richard's ride into the crowd; Froissart's incomprehension of the social forces at work.
- **Richard's Italian campaigns and favorites:** The political deterioration that makes Bolingbroke possible.
- **Flint Castle:** Richard's surrender; the last images of a king who had personal courage and catastrophic political judgment.

### Pass 2 — Slower

**Edward III** is the great architect of the early war: patient, strategically confident, politically secure enough to let his son fight without reinforcement at Crécy because he trusts the training and knows the lesson is worth more than the risk. He is capable of absolute mercilessness (the halters, the year-long grinding siege) and capable of mercy (Philippa's intercession) because he understands both as instruments of rule. His vow to execute the burghers was genuine; what Philippa gave him was not a reason to break it but a mechanism by which breaking it became an act of grace. He knew the difference.

**Queen Philippa of Hainault** appears once in the Chronicles and her appearance is the scene the century retained. She is not simply a merciful queen interrupting a harsh king. She is a political intelligence who has calculated that the only available tool for mercy in this moment is her body — kneeling, pregnant, publicly — and has deployed it with precision. She designed the intercession so that Edward's response could be "I cannot refuse you" rather than "I have changed my mind." The first maintains his authority; the second destroys it. She understood this before she walked to his chair. She is also seven months pregnant and she did it anyway, which is separately notable.

**The Black Prince (Edward of Woodstock)** is the romance hero of the Chronicles. At Crécy he holds his battle without breaking; at Poitiers he captures a king and serves him dinner. He possesses the chivalric code completely — not as performance but as genuine interior discipline — and the code works perfectly in his hands because he also has the military competence to back it. He is ill by 1376 and dead before his father; his death makes Richard II king, which is the beginning of the end.

**Eustache de Saint-Pierre** leads the six burghers but Froissart gives them no individual voices. They volunteer; they put on their own halters; they walk out. They are the moral objects of the scene, not its agents. The agency belongs to Philippa. The six men are the occasion for the scene but not its subject.

**Richard II** is the tragedy at the chronicle's end: a man of real personal courage — the Smithfield ride is extraordinary — and real cultural sophistication, who could not manage a council of nobles in a room. His deposition is not a simple story of tyranny punished; it is a story of a king who governed by temperament rather than by institution and paid the price when the institutions reorganized against him. Froissart does not say this; he records it. The reader says it.

**Jean Froissart himself** is the book's most interesting presence. He is vain, curious, loyal to the courts he served, and incapable of not writing down what happened. His sympathies are aristocratic but his recording instinct is stronger. He talks to everyone — princes, prisoners, merchants, siege engineers, heralds, and at least one man who was at Crécy — and writes it down. The Chronicles are uneven, sometimes contradictory between books, sometimes revised in ways that change the politics, always vivid. He is the reason the 14th century is legible.

### Pass 3 — Elaborate One Scene: Philippa at Calais

The six burghers came out of the gate at mid-morning of the day after Jean de Vienne formally surrendered. Eustache de Saint-Pierre led them. They had dressed themselves in the stipulated manner — shirts, bare feet, halter ropes around their own necks — and they had put on the halter ropes themselves before leaving the gate. Froissart notes this detail and it is worth noting: having the rope put on by another person is submission; putting it on yourself is the more total act, the acknowledgment that you have entered a category of existence where another person's choice determines whether you live. They carried the city keys between them and passed them forward as they walked so that one man held them at the front when they arrived. They did not look at the crowd.

The crowd was the English army. Eleven months of siege had produced something; the army had been in the camp long enough that Calais had become a place and not just an objective, and the six men coming through the gate were the resolution of something the army had been holding for a year. The silence was not hostile. It was the silence of people watching something that had been decided.

Edward sat before his pavilion with his council. When the six men reached him, he looked at them for a moment and said nothing. Then he gave the order for their heads.

The crowd began to move to give the executioners access.

Philippa came through the crowd on foot. She had no visible escort. She walked to where her husband sat and knelt in front of him — not beside the chair, not at a respectful distance: directly in front of him, on the ground, with her hands together. She was seven months pregnant. The English army watched.

She said: she asked for the men's lives as her gift. For the sake of God, for love of her. Not as policy, not as precedent, not as mercy required by the laws of siege warfare. As her personal petition from a husband who had brought her to a cold camp on the English Channel for nearly a year.

Edward was silent for a moment. Then he said what he said — what the century remembered he said — "I wish with all my heart that you had not been here today, for you have put me in a position such that I cannot refuse you."

He released the six burghers to her.

What the scene required was not mercy but mechanism. Edward had made a public vow in front of his army; breaking a public vow without mechanism is weakness. The mechanism Philippa gave him was herself: the king does not break his vow, the king grants his queen's petition. These are different acts in the political grammar of the 14th century, and Philippa knew that before she walked to his chair. "I cannot refuse you" is not submission; it is the explanation to his army of why this public act of mercy is also an act of strength: a king generous enough to grant his queen's desire, a queen devoted enough to kneel for men she has never met.

The six burghers received food, clothing, and money from Philippa that afternoon. Froissart does not record what they said when she gave it to them. He records what the king said. That is the story.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **Philippa's Gift** — The six burghers of Calais stand waiting with halter ropes around their necks; the execution order is being drafted in the campaign adjutant's tent; Queen Philippa has written a sealed petition requesting it be logged before the order is formally issued, because once the order is sealed even a public intercession cannot prevent it without the king losing face; the Fighter carries the petition through the siege camp before the adjutant's clerk finishes the last line.

2. **The Crécy Count** — The night after Crécy, Edward's heralds count the dead by torchlight to determine ransom and armor rights; a rival lord has claimed the identity seal of a fallen knight whose squire fled with it during the rout; the squire has returned with a sworn deposition of his lord's death, which supersedes the rival claim; the Fighter carries the deposition to the herald's tent before the rival's claim is formally entered in the battle roll.

3. **The Dinner Order** — The Black Prince has captured King John II of France and will receive him at dinner; a court faction wants to use the dinner as diplomatic signal by inserting a specific duke into the seating arrangement; the prince's written seating order, which refuses all English nobles their place at the captured king's table, must reach the steward before the duke's agent arrives with the competing instruction.

4. **The Ransom Letter** — The early terms of King John's ransom were negotiated in letters between Calais and Paris; a French agent has intercepted one letter and substituted a forgery that changes the financial terms; the original is in a courier's saddlebag at a Picardy inn; the Fighter must retrieve it and deliver it to the English commissioners before the French delegation presents the forged version and claims agreement.

5. **Gaston's Dangerous Chapter** — Froissart stayed at the court of Gaston Fébus, Count of Foix, who told him things his enemies in Navarre did not want recorded; a Navarrese agent is riding to intercept Froissart's notes before they can be copied; the Fighter carries the sealed copy from the scribe's hand to a sympathetic archivist two towns away before the agent reaches the inn.

6. **Tyler's Version** — At Smithfield, a London alderman watched exactly how Walworth killed Wat Tyler — close enough to determine whether Tyler drew first or whether the blow was provoked; his sworn statement to the chronicle-keeper, if filed before the official account is sealed, changes the political character of the event for everyone who will later dispute Richard's authority; the Fighter carries the alderman's deposition through the City to the royal chronicle office.

7. **The Flint Confession** — Richard II's chaplain was present at Flint Castle and wrote a private account of the surrender, naming what Richard said and whether it was compelled; the official account says Richard came willingly; the chaplain's account, in the hands of a sympathetic Chester archivist, is the only document that could sustain a legitimacy challenge in the coming years; the Fighter carries it out of Flint before Henry's agents search the castle.

### 5-Act Vignette Play — "Philippa's Gift"

**ACT I — Her Grace's Will**

*The Queen's Sealed Petition · sealed, on a writing desk · held by no one yet — it sits on the desk between the queen and the fighter · Queen Philippa's Pavilion, English siege camp, Calais, 1347*

The tent is blue and gold. Too many candles for the morning. The queen's physician sits at one end looking at nothing; she is seven months pregnant and has been in a campaign camp for three months and he has been unhappy about it since August. The letter is on the writing desk, three hours old and undelivered, addressed to the Campaign Adjutant in a secretary's careful hand with Philippa's personal seal in red wax on the ribbon.

Philippa herself is not looking at it. She has been not looking at it for three hours.

Her senior lady-in-waiting, Dame Eloise, steps between the Fighter and the desk. She is not hostile. She has the specific careful expression of a woman afraid of one particular kind of mistake.

"If the letter is found on a stranger before delivery," Dame Eloise says quietly, "it becomes evidence of the queen acting openly against her husband's declared intention. That is a different crisis from the one she is trying to prevent."

Philippa does not turn around. "We cannot have that," she agrees. "We also cannot have six men dead at noon because no one reached the adjutant's desk at eleven." She looks at the desk, not the letter. "Tell me what you need to know," she says, to the Fighter, "and I will answer honestly."

*The question is not whether to trust the Fighter. The question is what form of certainty will survive the next two hours.*

Dame Eloise reads the Fighter's face for a moment, then steps aside without speaking.

Philippa picks up the letter and holds it out. "The adjutant logs formal documents before noon," she says. "The order will be sealed at noon. That is not approximate — it is when he seals things." She looks at the Fighter directly. "Do not let it be after noon."

The Queen's Sealed Petition is heavier than a folded letter should be.

---

**ACT II — The Camp Checkpoint**

*The Queen's Sealed Petition · sealed, in transit · held by the Fighter · the English siege camp perimeter, Calais harbor wall sector*

The siege camp has been running for eleven months and it is organized the way eleven months organizes an army. There are checkpoints. There are movement lists. The sergeant at the forward perimeter checkpoint near the harbor wall controls passage between the queen's pavilion sector and the forward command area where the adjutant's compound is.

He has a list. The Fighter is not on it.

He is not suspicious — he has the specific methodical expression of a man who runs a list-based system and takes it seriously because systems are how a siege camp functions for eleven months without becoming chaos.

*The seal is on the letter. The question is whether the sergeant understands what the seal means.*

"A letter addressed to the Campaign Adjutant," the Fighter says, "bearing the queen's personal seal, in transit to the adjutant. The seal is the authorization." The Fighter shows him the Hainault arms in red wax. Every man in the English camp knows what the queen's seal looks like; they have seen it on supply warrants, on correspondence, on the standard above the queen's pavilion.

The sergeant looks at the list. He looks at the seal. He has a list. He also has thirty years of service under Edward and Philippa both.

"Move through," he says, and stamps a transit chit.

---

**ACT III — Sir Geoffrey**

*The Queen's Sealed Petition · sealed, moving · held by the Fighter · the camp road between the perimeter and the adjutant's compound*

Sir Geoffrey de Courtenay has been in the king's household guard for sixteen years. He has a very clear idea of what the king requires. He is standing in the middle of the camp road with two men-at-arms when the Fighter rounds the last supply wagon. Not barring the road. Just present.

"The king's order is to execute at noon," he says. He has a legal mind under the hardliner exterior and he has chosen his words precisely. "I am told the queen has been writing letters this morning. Correspondence going to the Campaign Adjutant should probably be reviewed before delivery, given the circumstances."

"The queen's correspondence addressed to a military adjutant cannot be reviewed before delivery," the Fighter says. "That is interception. The letter is addressed to the adjutant. Delivering it to the adjutant is delivery to the correct authority. Anything else is a different act with a different name."

Sir Geoffrey considers. The word "interception" has registered exactly where it was aimed.

*He has decided this will be a review, not an interception. His men have decided it will be a fight. These are different decisions.*

The men-at-arms are moving before Sir Geoffrey can revise his position. The camp road is flat and open. The Fighter moves first.

Sir Geoffrey watches. He does not draw. When it is over, he looks at the Fighter and at the letter and at the road ahead.

"Delivered to the adjutant," he says, carefully. "Yes. The queen's correspondence." He moves out of the road.

---

**ACT IV — Before the Pen**

*The Queen's Sealed Petition · sealed, one corner damp from the road · held by the Fighter · Campaign Adjutant's Registry, forward command tent*

The adjutant's registry is a long tent with a board floor and three clerk tables. The main desk is at the far end. On the main desk: the execution order, being copied in a clerk's fair hand. The clerk is at the third-to-last line. The adjutant is in the inner section, his back to the main desk.

The Fighter is at the tent flap. Fifteen feet. The clerk is moving steadily.

*No subtle path through a registry tent at speed exists. The Fighter goes through it anyway.*

Between the clerk tables: one stack of submitted documents slides and is caught with the left hand and placed back without stopping; one stool is redirected rather than knocked over; the distance closes. Fifteen feet. Ten. Five.

The Queen's Sealed Petition lands on the main desk before the pen touches the last line.

The clerk stops. He looks at the seal. He looks at the document he has been copying. He looks up.

"Is the adjutant available?" the Fighter says.

---

**ACT V — The Log Precedes the Order**

*The Queen's Sealed Petition · on the adjutant's desk, unsealed now, the paper visible · held by the moment · Campaign Adjutant's Registry*

The adjutant reads the petition twice. He is a man in his fifties who has managed military paperwork for thirty years and has an exact understanding of what it means for a document to be logged before another document is formally issued. He sets the petition down. He looks at the Fighter.

"The queen is coming to make this appeal herself this afternoon," he says. It is not a question.

"Yes," the Fighter says.

"And if this is logged before the order is sealed," the adjutant says slowly, "then when the king says he cannot refuse her, there is a prior record that mercy was asked before the order became irrevocable. He does not break his word. He grants a petition already in the log." He looks at the petition, then at the execution order on his desk, four lines from completion. "This is very precisely designed," he says. He does not sound entirely approving. He does not sound disapproving either. He sounds like a man reading a document that has been thought about for longer than it appears.

*He is not deciding whether to have mercy. He is deciding whether to enable a king to have it without losing face. Those are different decisions that require different words.*

"She is his queen," the Fighter says. "She designed this so he could say yes."

The adjutant looks at the Fighter for a moment. Then he opens the campaign log. He writes: *Petition received — Queen Philippa of Hainault, re: Calais burghers — logged prior to order of execution.* He stamps it.

He sets his pen down and does not immediately sign the execution order. He looks at the tent wall, at the sound of the English camp outside, at whatever thirty years of military paperwork has taught him about the distance between what orders say and what happens afterward.

"Tell her it is logged," he says finally. "She will know what to do."

The Queen's Sealed Petition is in the campaign log. It precedes the order. When Philippa kneels before her husband this afternoon, there will already be paper beneath her knees.

## Supplementary Cycle — Angle: 1367 AD — the routier economy after Brétigny; the Black Prince governing Gascony; the safe-conduct as the only law in the lawless space

**What this cycle sees that Cycle 1 missed:** Cycle 1's focal world is hierarchy functioning at its best — a queen who deploys the mechanism of mercy precisely because she understands the hierarchy completely, and a king who can say "I cannot refuse you" because the machinery of formal authority is intact. This cycle's focal world is 1367: the same political system, twenty years older, operating in the gaps between its own structures. The ransom is being paid in installments; the treaty is nominally holding; the companies of armed men who fought the war have no war to fight and are living off the peace by other means. The only law they recognize is the law they make between themselves and the towns they choose not to burn this season.

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

The Treaty of Brétigny was signed in 1360 and it changed the shape of France the way a hard winter changes the shape of a field — everything is still there, but what was standing is bent. Edward III of England gets full sovereignty over a third of France: Gascony, Aquitaine, Ponthieu, and more. France agrees to pay three million gold écus for John II's ransom. John goes home and starts collecting the money. Then his son Louis of Anjou, a hostage in Calais, escapes. John announces that his honor requires him to return to English custody. He goes back to London. He dies there in 1364, still paying the ransom in installments. Froissart, who admires this more than the politics warrants, records it with undisguised respect. His son Charles V — Charles the Wise — is now king of France and is paying the remaining balance with the patient financial intelligence of a man who has decided that military defeat should not repeat as administrative defeat.

The Black Prince has been Prince of Aquitaine since 1363. He is thirty-seven years old in 1367 and governing the English territories in France from Bordeaux. He runs a court that French lords respect — too well-run, some English lords think, too elaborate for a military outpost. He has been managing the Free Companies, the routiers, the great companies of mercenary soldiers who fought both sides of the war and now, with Brétigny nominally holding, have no employer and have not stopped being what they are. They live off the peace: demanding silver from towns in exchange for not burning them, occasionally fighting for hire when a lord with a specific problem appears.

The most famous routier figures of the decade are an education. Arnaud de Cervole, the Archpriest — a man who held a church benefice before the life caught him — led the Great Company through Burgundy and Avignon and extorted Pope Innocent VI into paying for their departure. De Cervole was killed by his own men in 1366. The companies survived him, fragmented and continued. Hugh Calveley. Robert Knolles. Bertrand du Guesclin, who is technically French but works with the companies when the politics make it useful. Thousands of men organized around the principle that force is a resource, that a town is a resource, and that an agreement is only as good as the signature behind it.

The Black Prince's solution to the routier problem is the same as Charles V's: export it. Both sides are organizing the companies' departure to Spain, where Don Pedro of Castile needs military support against his half-brother Henry of Trastámara. In early 1367 the staging is underway from Bordeaux. The companies will ride south under the English banner, fight at Nájera on April 3, win comprehensively — the English always win on ground they choose — and then discover that Don Pedro has no money. They come back from Spain empty. The problem returns to France, larger.

But in the January and February of 1367, before any of that, Bordeaux is the organizing center of the world. The Black Prince is there, planning. The companies are there, some staging for Spain, others waiting to see whether the money is real, others conducting their regular business against the Périgord towns. A town fifty miles northeast of Bordeaux is paying for its protection this season, as it pays every season. The safe-conduct exists. The money has been received. The letter must reach the town before the company's outriders do.

Froissart records the kings and the heralds, the formal exchanges of chivalry, the specific reported speech of men who know they are being watched by history. He half-records the routier economy — the safe-conducts, the seasonal agreements, the precise commercial texture of life in the gaps between battles. He talks to everyone; he talked to routier captains. He doesn't entirely disapprove of them. They operate according to their own code and the code is more consistent than most. But they are not his people. He writes them as background, as context for the chivalric foreground. They are the world the chivalric code was built to manage.

What Froissart doesn't write: the Fighter who carried the documents between the world he is recording and the world he is not quite seeing.

---

**Chapter Notes**

- **Brétigny (1360):** The treaty that defines everything in 1367. One third of France to England; three million écus for John II. Seven years of installments still running.
- **John II's return to captivity (1364):** His son's escape from Calais; John's voluntary return to London; his death there. Froissart records this with more admiration than the strategic situation merits. Honor as a compulsion. John II as the man the chivalric code produces when it is taken absolutely seriously by someone with poor political instincts.
- **Black Prince at Bordeaux (1363–1370):** Governing Aquitaine from a court that runs too well to be purely military. He will be ill by 1370, dead in 1376, never becoming king. His administration of Gascony is the brief period when the system worked at something close to its best.
- **The Free Companies (1360s):** Men from both armies, unemployed by the peace, organized into military-commercial enterprises. Arnaud de Cervole. Hugh Calveley. Robert Knolles. Bertrand du Guesclin operating at the edge of several categories. Their economy runs on seasonal protection agreements.
- **The Spanish campaign (1367):** Black Prince crosses the Pyrenees January–February. Battle of Nájera, April 3 — English victory, du Guesclin captured. Don Pedro defaults on payment. The companies return to France worse than before.
- **Richard of Bordeaux (January 6, 1367):** The future Richard II born during this period. His birth's political consequences are entirely invisible in January 1367. His father is going to Spain. His grandfather Edward III is ill in London. The child will be king before anyone is ready.

### Pass 2 — Slower

**The Black Prince at 37** is not the sixteen-year-old at Crécy or the twenty-six-year-old who served the captured king his wine. He is a governing prince with administrative responsibilities, a campaign to plan, and the early stages of the illness that will kill him at forty-six. He is still absolutely committed to the chivalric code — this is visible in how he runs his court, in how he treats Don Pedro's defaults with the tolerant disappointment of a man who expected better from a situation he entered with open eyes. He is going to get nothing from Spain except a victory and a worsening health condition. He knows this in some layer below the planning, before he crosses the Pyrenees. He goes anyway. The code does not have a clause about expected returns.

**Estève de Castelmoron (a routier captain in the register of the real ones)** is not a villain. He is a professional soldier operating in the gap between institutions. He has no great lord's backing. He has a company of roughly two hundred men and a reputation for honoring his agreements, because honoring agreements is the only way to make the safe-conduct business work over time. If his letters don't hold, towns stop paying and start fighting. He calculates. He is calculating when he hands the safe-conduct to the Fighter and he is calculating when his outriders ride a different road. Whether the outriders' misunderstanding was deliberate or genuine is something he will never clarify.

**Bertrand du Guesclin** is not yet Constable of France in early 1367 — that comes after Nájera, after the ransom, after everything. He is the most capable military commander in France, currently fighting for Henry of Trastámara against Don Pedro, about to get himself captured at Nájera. He is the book's counterweight to the Black Prince: the professional soldier who comes up from the bottom rather than down from the court. He would not have served dinner to a captured king without sitting at the table. He would have negotiated the seating arrangement first.

**Charles V ("the Wise")** is doing what French kings do after catastrophic military defeat: rebuilding quietly, taxing carefully, avoiding pitched battles, using lawyers where his predecessors used lances. He is paying the ransom installments and he is funding the Spanish expedition and he is waiting for the English to make a mistake he can exploit. He is right that they will. He will outlive the Black Prince by eight years and die with most of the English territories recovered. In 1367 all of that is still in front of him and he is paying attention.

**Froissart himself** at thirty is gathering material, attending court, talking to every participant of events he wasn't old enough to witness firsthand. He has already written about Crécy and Calais from others' accounts. He knows what makes a scene legible: the specific reported speech, the humane gesture that reveals the man inside the title. The routier captains give him material but not his kind — they have no royal blood and the chivalric code doesn't fit them as elegantly. He records them anyway. He is more honest than his politics.

### Pass 3 — Elaborate One Scene: The Dinner at Poitiers

The scene is from September 19, 1356 — eleven years before the game's present. Not 1367. But the dinner is the origin document of the 1367 world: the gesture that defines what the chivalric ideal looks like when it is held completely, which is why the 1367 world is still paying off its consequences — the ransom, the treaty, the administration of the captured king's installments — long after the meal was cleared away. The routier economy in Bordeaux in 1367 exists in the shadow of this dinner. The idea that a man's word, formally given before witnesses, creates an obligation that exists regardless of whether a court will enforce it — that idea runs in a direct line from the tent at Poitiers to the inn near the harbor wall.

The tent is not a palace. It is a campaign tent the prince's steward has made into a palace for the evening: boards on trestles for tables, rush-lights and two good candles from the chaplain's supply, the smell of the field still on every man in the room. Outside, the dead are being counted — five or six thousand French, by the heralds' tally. Inside, King John II of France, his youngest son Philip, and the senior French prisoners are at dinner.

John has been fighting since morning in the front rank of his own army. He is in the same armor he wore in the battle. The prince has not offered him the discourtesy of making him wait in a separate tent while his captor changed clothes. John's face has the quality of a man who has finished processing what happened and come to rest on the fact that it happened this way. He fought with absolute personal courage at the moment everything collapsed around him. The English accounts confirm this. The battle was lost; his courage was real; these are separate facts.

The Black Prince enters carrying the wine jug. Not a servant carrying it — the prince himself, going from guest to guest at the high table. He fills the French king's cup. He fills the cups of the French princes and lords. He does not sit.

John invites him repeatedly. The Berners translation renders the refusal with the specific phrasing of a translator who is trying to capture something precise: the prince "would not sit at the king's board for any desire that the king could make, but he said he was not sufficient to sit at the table with so great a prince as the king was."

Not sufficient. This phrase is doing the most work in the scene. The prince has just defeated this man three to one. The field is cleared by dusk. The king of France is in his tent. And the prince says: I am not sufficient to sit beside you.

He means it as courtesy. He also means it — the complexity is that both are true. The chivalric code says a captured king is still a king. Rank is not diminished by defeat. The prince holds the code not as performance but as interior practice. He is not pretending to feel insufficient. He is behaving consistently with a thing he actually believes.

Then: "Sir, do not make no evil or heavy cheer, though God this day did not consent to follow your will; for, sir, surely the king my father shall bear you as much honour and amity as he may do, and shall accord with you so reasonably that ye shall ever be friends together."

And: "Sir, methinks ye ought to rejoice, though the journey be not as ye would have had it, for this day ye have won the high renown of prowess and have passed this day all other on your side. Sir, I say not this to mock you, for all that be on our side, that have seen every man's deeds, are plainly accorded by true sentence to give you the prize and chaplet."

He is twenty-six years old. The man he is speaking to has just lost France.

What the prince is doing — Froissart sees this and records it correctly — is giving the captured king something to carry out of the defeat that is not the defeat. The battle proved one set of things about tactics and the longbow and the relative discipline of the two armies. The prince is officially certifying that the battle proved a separate set of things about John II personally — that he fought without flinching, that his personal valor was real and witnessed. He is making sure the second story is put on record in the moment of the first. He is giving the captured king his dignity back in the only form available: a verbal certification, before witnesses, from the man who just beat him.

John thanks him. The dinner continues. The prince circulates with the wine jug. Froissart records no more of what was said. He does not need to. He has given the scene its peak and trusted the reader to understand what followed: dinner, late into the evening, two men on opposite sides of the day's account eating and drinking together in the only tent in France that contained both of them.

This dinner will cost France three million écus. The Black Prince knew the rough shape of the terms when he made the speech. The speech was not contingent on the terms being mild. He would have made the speech at twice the price. That is what holding the code completely costs: you do not modify it for context.

Eleven years later in Bordeaux, a routier captain sits at a table near the harbor and explains: the seal is the contract. If it arrives intact, the agreement holds. He expects it to mean what it says. His world is not the prince's world. But the idea runs through both of them: a word formally given before witnesses creates an obligation that exists regardless of whether any court can enforce it. The line of descent from the tent at Poitiers runs to the inn at Bordeaux, and from there it runs to the limestone town in Périgord waiting for the letter that is the only thing standing between it and fire.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Ransom Installment** — The French treasury is transmitting a payment on John II's ransom through Bordeaux to the English commissioners, and a routier captain has learned the payment route; the sealed transfer docket authorizing the convoy must reach the English registry before the convoy departs, because without it any English officer who meets the convoy on the road can legally confiscate it. The fighter carries the docket through the Bordeaux harbor district where the captain's agent has been watching departures since dawn.

2. **The Company's Letter** — A Free Company captain in Bordeaux has issued a safe-conduct for a Périgord town in exchange for payment already received; the sealed letter must reach the town before the company's outriders arrive at its gate. There is no court that enforces this agreement — only the captain's word, the town's need, and the Fighter who carries the only document either party will honor.

3. **The Dinner Protocol** — The night after Poitiers, the Black Prince circulated among the captured French nobles with a wine jug in his own hands, refusing the French king's invitation to sit; before the dinner, his written seating order must reach the royal steward ahead of a rival English lord's competing instruction that would place a French duke at the head table and turn a diplomatic masterpiece into an incident. The prince's courtesy is not a performance — it is a precise instrument, and the instrument is a piece of paper.

4. **The Prince's Son** — Richard of Bordeaux was born January 6, 1367, and a Navarrese agent intends to file a document in the English registry claiming the child's birth date falls after a specific religious festival that would affect succession precedence; the sealed birth record from the Black Prince's household chaplain must reach the English royal registry before the Navarrese filing window opens. The fighter carries the birth record through the Bordeaux harbor district in the first week of January, when the city is organizing a Spanish campaign and nobody is looking at the archive building.

5. **The Chronicler's Draft** — Froissart has written fair accounts of events that two specific English lords do not want preserved; a sealed copy of his manuscript is being transmitted to a Flemish scriptorium for safekeeping before those lords can arrange for the original to be corrected, and the copy must pass through the Calais harbor before the lords' agents close the mail-packet inspection. The fighter carries the copy — if both copy and original exist simultaneously in separate locations, suppression becomes pointless — through a port watched by men who have been told to look for anything going to Flanders from a secretary's hand.

6. **The Herald's Patent** — Before the Spanish campaign, every herald operating under the Black Prince's authority received a patent of safe-conduct valid in Castile and Navarre; one patent was incorrectly sealed with a minor lord's seal rather than the prince's and must be replaced before the herald crosses the Pyrenees with defective credentials. The herald is already two days south of Bordeaux, moving for the pass; the corrected patent must reach him at his overnight stop, because once a herald is in enemy territory with a broken credential the rules of heralds' immunity do not apply.

7. **The Road Back from Nájera** — Bertrand du Guesclin was captured at Nájera on April 3, 1367; his ransom negotiation involves a combined document that also releases a secondary prisoner whose family has been waiting fifteen years; an Aragonese factor intends to buy out du Guesclin's debt separately, severing the secondary prisoner's release from the negotiation; the combined ransom letter must reach the Castilian commission before the Aragonese offer closes. The fighter moves through a Castile that has changed sides twice in six months, carrying the one document that would release a man whose name the world has forgotten because du Guesclin's name is louder.

### 5-Act Vignette Play — LIL-02: "The Company's Letter"

**ACT I — The Captain's Word**

*The Company's Safe-Conduct · sealed on the table, not yet handed over · held by no one — it sits between the captain and the Fighter · Bordeaux, a requisitioned inn near the harbor wall, morning*

The inn smells of the company — horse, oiled iron, Gascon leather, and fifty men who have been sleeping and eating here for three weeks and are forming for Spain in the road outside. Estève de Castelmoron sits at a table by the shuttered window with the safe-conduct in front of him and a cup of wine he hasn't touched. He is forty years old, built like the wall behind him, and he has the careful face of a man who has learned that his word is his only durable asset.

He looks at the Fighter for a long moment before he speaks.

"The town paid Tuesday," he says. "Fifty écus. I counted them." He taps the seal — the rampant wolf in black wax. "This letter is their receipt. If it arrives with both seals intact, my outriders pass around the town's walls. If it doesn't arrive, my outriders don't know there's an agreement. If it arrives broken, there is no agreement — the seal is what makes it binding, not the paper."

A sergeant appears at the door with a roster. The company is in the road. Estève looks at the roster, looks at the Fighter, and makes a decision.

"They ride south," he says, meaning the outriders. "They know the hill road. You ride east, through Libourne." He pauses. "They were told to ride south."

*Whether the outriders were told anything at all is a question the Fighter won't be able to answer until the forest road east of Libourne.*

He sets the safe-conduct on the table and waits for the Fighter to pick it up.

**ACT II — The Libourne Checkpoint**

*The Company's Safe-Conduct · sealed, in transit · held by the Fighter · English garrison checkpoint at the Libourne river crossing, midmorning*

The checkpoint is three men and a log across the road. The corporal who runs it is diligent — the kind who stays diligent twenty miles from his officer because diligence is a habit, not a performance. He stops the Fighter. He looks at the letter. He sees the rampant-wolf seal in black wax and a sealed correspondence that is not from any lord he recognizes.

"I need to log the contents before you cross," he says. He means: break the seal, read the letter, re-seal with his own counter-stamp. Standard procedure. He has his wax block and stamp ready on the post.

"Breaking a sealed safe-conduct voids it," the Fighter says. "The seal is the contract. If you break it, the document is worth nothing to anyone on either side of this crossing. The corporal who voided a routier safe-conduct in transit is not an interesting position to be in."

The corporal looks at the seal. He looks at his log. He looks at the seal one more time.

*He is deciding whether to be diligent in the way he knows, or in the way that actually works.*

**ACT III — The Forest Road**

*The Company's Safe-Conduct · sealed, inside the travel pack · held by the Fighter · forest road east of Libourne, entering Périgord, early afternoon*

The forest road narrows into a limestone defile. Two riders are waiting in the shadows — not barring the road, just present, in the way of men who have done this before and know that presence alone does the first half of the work. They wear no livery but their harness is the company's style: strapped-down, practical, nothing decorative. One carries a crossbow held at rest. "The captain sends his regards," the closer rider says.

**ACT IV — The Town Gate at Dusk**

*The Company's Safe-Conduct · sealed, intact, one edge damp from a stream crossing · held by the Fighter · main gate of Beaulieu-en-Périgord, evening*

**ACT V — The Council Chamber**

The man with the household near the wall is looking at the Fighter with the expression of someone deciding whether to believe what they need to hear.

*The captain counted forty-eight. He called it fifty. The Fighter was present. The question is what the Fighter says next, and why.*

## Cycle 3 — Angle: The Half-Entry and the Single-Party Record

*Prior cycles missed:*

LIL Cycles 1 and 2 found the mercy staged as political instrument, and the document that creates law between parties with no common authority. Neither cycle asked what happens when a payment has been made but only one party's records show it — when the French treasury has a certified receipt but the English registry has an incomplete countersignature because the official who was supposed to provide it died of plague before completing the entry.

Cycle 3 finds the ransom installment suspended in a documentary limbo: the payment is real, the debt is partially extinguished, but the English registry shows an incomplete entry — half of a double-entry bookkeeping process, waiting for a record that has not yet been created. The carrier brings the French proof to the English registry so the entry can be completed.

### LIL-03: "The Ransom Installment"

**Theme check:** LIL-1 (mercy staged as political instrument) and LIL-2 (document creating law between parties with no common authority) are not this. CRL-1 through CRL-8 are not this. BLQ-1 through BLQ-12 are not this. Cycle 3 takes the angle of the half-completed double-entry transaction — the payment made by one party, documented by one party, requiring the other party's acknowledgment to be legally operative; the single-party record as evidence waiting for its counterpart.

This is distinct from CRL-3 (strategic accounting misdescription — that document was complete but chose the wrong category; this document is a correct record of a genuine payment that cannot be credited because the receiving registry's entry is incomplete); from BLQ-12 (antecedent debt — that document was complete and served as alibi; this document is incomplete and waiting for completion).

---

**SOURCE HOOK:** *Chronicles of Froissart* (Berners translation), Book II: King John II of France was captured at Poitiers in 1356 and his ransom — three million gold crowns — was never fully paid. Installments were made for years; Charles V inherited the debt. Berners translates Froissart's account of John II's captivity and the ransom negotiations in their full institutional complexity. The specific mechanism of how installments were received and credited is the bureaucratic substratum beneath the chivalric surface.

---

**1367 SETTING:**
The tenth installment of John II's ransom — fifty thousand gold crowns, paid by Charles V's treasury in spring 1367 — was received at Bordeaux in April. The English registry official responsible for countersigning the receipt, Geoffrey de Montfort, died of plague on April 12, the same week the French treasury agent delivered the installment. The new registry official, Thomas Aldwick, arrived on April 19. Thomas has been given Geoffrey's registry book but not Geoffrey's session log; the April 12 entry shows the installment payment in Geoffrey's handwriting, with a blank countersignature line. The French treasury agent, Bertrand de Foix, has the French-side certified receipt but cannot return to Paris without the English countersignature that confirms the installment has been credited. He meets the carrier at Périgueux, gives them the French receipt, and asks them to complete the entry in Bordeaux.

*Bertrand de Foix, handing over the treasury certificate: "Geoffrey wrote the entry. Geoffrey died. The entry is real. The payment is real. Thomas Aldwick needs to know that completing Geoffrey's entry is not the same as inventing it."*

**TOKEN:** French Treasury Installment Certificate — a single vellum sheet, the French royal treasury seal in blue, signed by the Treasurer General; Bertrand de Foix's accompanying cover note attached: *Payment of the tenth installment, fifty thousand crowns, delivered April 10, 1367. English registry entry opened by Geoffrey de Montfort, April 12. Countersignature not completed due to death of said official. Certificate presented to complete the English record.* *Act I: received from Bertrand de Foix, Périgueux. Act V: surrendered to Thomas Aldwick, Bordeaux English Registry.*

**TOKEN granted:** Act I
**TOKEN taken:** Act V

**Act I — Périgueux Waystation (PER)**

*A waystation inn at Périgueux, morning. Bertrand de Foix is a compact French treasury agent in his forties who has been waiting in Périgueux for six days. He has the treasury certificate and the cover note and a very specific anxiety about the distinction between completing a dead official's entry and inventing one.*

"Thomas Aldwick has never met Geoffrey de Montfort. He cannot verify Geoffrey's handwriting from memory. What he can verify is that the entry exists in the registry book — Geoffrey's hand, Geoffrey's notation style — and that this French certificate matches the entry in every detail: the date, the amount, the French treasury seal." He spreads both documents on the table. "What he cannot do is add his countersignature without being certain he is completing a real entry rather than endorsing a forgery." He looks at the carrier. "Tell me the argument for why the entry and the certificate together are sufficient proof."

*The Fighter watches Bertrand's hands on both documents — the English registry extract he has brought from Bordeaux alongside the French certificate — the expression of a man who knows he is right and is not sure the person who matters will agree.*

Make an **Investigation check (DC 12)**.

**Fail:** "The certificate and the entry match. That is necessary but not sufficient. Tell me what makes their agreement evidential — what would be different if this were a forgery." *Bertrand waits. He has been thinking about this question for six days.*

**Success:** If the English entry were forged, the forger would need Geoffrey's registry book — which has been in English custody since April 12 and passed to Thomas Aldwick on April 19, seven days after Geoffrey died. The French certificate was issued in Paris before the English entry was made, under the French treasury seal, and does not mention Geoffrey by name — it could not have been coordinated with a forgery because the French treasury didn't know Geoffrey's name. The temporal and institutional independence of the two documents makes their agreement evidential. Bertrand exhales. "That is the argument." He hands over the **French Treasury Installment Certificate** and both notes.

---

**Act II — Périgord Forest Road (PER)**

*Two hours south of Périgueux, a Gascon toll collector's checkpoint — a timber gate, one collector, two soldiers. The collector checks transit papers and has a new rule: all French treasury documents transiting toward Bordeaux must be registered at the checkpoint before proceeding, per a recently issued Gascon administrative order dated May 3.*

"This is a French treasury document. It needs to be registered here before it reaches the English administration." He is applying the rule correctly. The rule creates a risk: a registered copy of the French certificate would be in the Gascon administrative log, which is accessible to French agents. The French treasury agent specifically did not want the installment certificate to pass through Gascon hands before it reached the English registry.

*The Fighter watches the collector open his registration book — the French treasury seal on the certificate is visible and the collector has correctly identified it.*

Make a **Persuasion check (DC 12)**.

**Fail:** "The rule is the rule. All French treasury documents. I don't have a carve-out for English-destined documents." *He opens his book to a new entry. Try again — about what "transit toward Bordeaux" means when the document has no French administrative relevance to the Gascon tollway.*

**Success:** "The registration requirement was issued for French treasury documents in transit for Gascon administrative purposes — documents relating to Gascon taxation, land claims, or property disputes. This document is in transit for the English registry's ransom accounting, which is a bilateral matter between the French and English crowns, not a Gascon administrative matter. Your order does not apply." He reads the May 3 order. He reads the relevant clause. He closes the registration book. "Transit documents for the English crown. Proceed." *He stamps the transit paper without opening the registration book.*

**Act III — Bordeaux English Registry Approach (BDX)**

*A law-court anteroom near the English registry, afternoon. A French royal notary named Pierre de Saintloup is waiting. He has heard that a French treasury certificate is being delivered to complete Geoffrey de Montfort's unfinished entry. He has been sent to observe and, if possible, to intervene — not to stop the delivery, but to ensure the entry is marked as "payment received under protest" since Charles V disputes the interest calculation on the previous nine installments.*

"The tenth installment should be credited with a notation that the interest dispute from installments one through nine remains unresolved. If Thomas Aldwick completes the entry without that notation, the credit extinguishes the associated interest claims." He produces a letter from the French royal council. "I have the authority to request the notation."

*The Fighter watches Pierre de Saintloup's letter — a real authority from a real source, with real implications for a six-year interest dispute worth significantly more than fifty thousand crowns.*

Make a **History check (DC 13)** to understand whether the notation request is procedurally correct at this stage and what crediting the installment without notation means for the interest dispute.

**Fail:** "I understand there is an interest dispute. Tell me whether this installment payment is procedurally separable from the interest calculation — whether completing the entry can be done without the notation, or whether the notation is technically required to preserve the French position." *Pierre waits. He is precise.*

**Success:** The installment and the interest dispute are separate transactions. The installment is a specific credited amount for a specific payment. The interest dispute is a separate accounting question that would require a separate proceeding to resolve. Completing the tenth installment entry without a notation does not extinguish the interest claim — the interest claim is established through different documentation. Pierre's request, while politically motivated, is procedurally incorrect: a notation on a registry credit entry cannot extinguish claims that are in a different registry section. "The interest dispute is not affected by this entry." Pierre looks at the letter. He knows this is true. He steps aside. "I will report that the carrier understood the distinction."

**Act IV — Bordeaux English Registry (BDX)**

*The English registry office, late afternoon. Thomas Aldwick is a careful young English official who has been in his position for four weeks. He has Geoffrey de Montfort's registry book open to the April 12 entry. He reads the French certificate. He reads the entry. He reads both again.*

"The entry is in Geoffrey's hand. I have worked with Geoffrey's session logs. The handwriting is consistent." He sets both down. "The certificate matches the entry in date, amount, and seal. They are independent documents that agree. I am inclined to complete the entry." He pauses. "I am twenty-six years old and I have been in this position for four weeks and completing a dead man's unfinished work is not something I was instructed how to do."

*The Fighter watches Thomas's hands on the two documents — the expression of a man who knows what the right answer is and is held back by the weight of the responsibility.*

Make a **Persuasion check (DC 11)** to give him the argument that completing Geoffrey's entry is an act of registry maintenance, not an act of creation.

**Fail:** "I understand the argument for completing it. I am asking for the argument about my authority. Does a new registry official have the standing to complete his predecessor's incomplete entries?" *Thomas is precise. He needs a specific answer to a specific question.*

**Success:** "The entry belongs to the registry, not to Geoffrey personally. Geoffrey was acting in his official capacity when he made the entry. His death did not create a new question — it left an administrative gap in a record that the registry is obligated to maintain accurately. Your authority to complete the entry derives from your position as registry official, not from Geoffrey's authorization. You are completing the registry's work, not Geoffrey's work." Thomas picks up his pen. "The registry's work." He writes his countersignature in the blank line. He stamps the completed entry. He hands you the **Registry Completion Receipt**.

---

**Act V — Bordeaux Diocesan Registry (BDX)**

*Guilhem de Castanet's registry — the same afternoon, at the carrier's request, to create a permanent parallel record. Guilhem reads the completed entry and the French certificate together.*

"I understand why you are here. If the English registry is ever disputed, the diocesan record gives the payment an additional independent certification." He thinks. "The classification does not exist. I have never received a French ransom installment certificate before." He looks at the carrier. "Tell me what this document proves beyond the specific payment — what category of document this is in terms of what institutional problem it solves."

*The Fighter watches Guilhem open his ledger to a new section — the expression of a man who takes classification seriously and is about to create a category that captures something precise about how installment payments work when the receiver dies mid-process.*

Make a **History check (DC 12)**.

**Fail:** "The payment is real. The registry entry is complete. I need the classification that names what problem this document solves — the category that makes this filing useful to whoever needs to understand how installment records work." *He waits.*

**Success:** Guilhem writes: *Ransom Records — Installment Payments Documented by Single-Party Certification; The French Treasury Proof as English Registry Credit; Debt Partially Extinguished When Both Parties' Records Agree; Completed After Death of Receiving Official by Successor's Authority.* He applies the diocesan seal. You hand over the **French Treasury Installment Certificate**. You receive the **Diocesan Ransom Record Reference**.

*The Fighter watches the certificate disappear into Guilhem's permanent file. Fifty thousand crowns, paid in April, credited in May. A dead official's blank line filled by his successor's pen. The debt is smaller now.*

### LIL-06: "The Chronicler's Draft"

**SOURCE HOOK:** Jean Froissart spent time at the English and Gascon courts in the 1360s and was in Bordeaux during the Black Prince's occupation. ## Cycle 7 — Angle: The Credential That Must Be Corrected Before the Act It Authorizes
