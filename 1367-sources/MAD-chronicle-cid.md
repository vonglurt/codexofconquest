## PASS 1 — The Arc

Rodrigo Díaz de Bivar earns his cognomen "El Cid Campeador" — the Lord Champion — not by birth or favor but by the unbroken maintenance of honor under every form of injustice his king can impose on him. The Chronicle opens with the young Rodrigo already the established champion of Castile: he forces King Don Alfonso to swear three times at Santa Gadea on the holy Gospels that he had no hand in the murder of his brother King Don Sancho. That oath-taking is the hinge of everything. From it forward Alfonso can never forgive Rodrigo the humiliation of having been made to swear, and twice he exiles him from Castile and all its lands. The first exile is the spine of the work. With sixty men Rodrigo rides out of Bivar, leaving empty mangers and locked doors; in Burgos a little girl of nine comes to her window to tell him the King has forbidden anyone to receive him on pain of losing their eyes and their property. The townspeople watch in silence and one line captures their feeling for all time: "God, how good a vassal if he had but a good Lord!" He prays at Cardeña, lashes his daughters and his beloved Ximena to him "as the nail to the quick flesh" in farewell, and rides east into banishment. He promises Ximena on his life that he will give their daughters away with his own hands from a position of honour, that she will be served by him again. Then he keeps every word of it.

What follows is nine books of conquest by a man who cannot go home. Rodrigo first tricks Jewish moneylenders in Burgos with coffers of sand weighed as gold, then takes town after town in the country of Moorish kings, always fighting with mathematical precision — feigned retreats, split forces, rear ambushes — and always under the moral discipline that his enemies later note as miraculous: he never breaks his word, never robs an ally, releases prisoners who behave nobly, and refuses to fight the King who exiled him even when Alfonso rides against him. He defeats Count Ramon Berenguer and keeps him a prisoner until the Count, who refuses to eat for shame, finally relents: Rodrigo offers him bread and wine saying "this is the chance of war," and when the Count eats and goes free he mutters that Rodrigo "never did a disloyal thing." The campaigns accumulate: Alcocer, Murviedro, Denia, Játiva. Alvar Fañez Minaya serves as his right hand throughout, carrying gifts to Alfonso and to Ximena, and the King relents enough to release Ximena and the daughters to join Rodrigo in the East.

The great achievement is Valencia. After a months-long siege that reduces the city to starvation — its people washing grape stones to eat, slaughtering dogs and cats, paying three maravedís for a pound of meat — Valencia falls. Rodrigo rides up to the high tower on his horse Bavieca, and leads Ximena up beside him: "You dear and honoured wife, and ye my daughters, my heart and my soul — enter with me into Valencia. This is the inheritance which I have won for you." He defeats two subsequent counterattacks: first King Yucef with 50,000 Moors ("the more Moors the more gain!"), then later King Bucar the Miramamolin. He governs with remarkable equity, keeping Moors and Christians in such accord that the Moorish Alfaqui Alfaraxi voluntarily converts to Christianity and is baptized Gil Díaz. He defeats the defiance of his enemies at home by demanding justice at the Toledo Cortes — the disastrous second marriages, to the craven Infantes of Carrión, had been arranged not by Rodrigo but by King Alfonso himself — and when the Infantes' outrage at Corpes is laid bare in open court, Rodrigo demands his swords Colada and Tizona back first, then the return of his daughters' dowry, then names the Infantes "dogs and traitors." Three judicial combats at Carrión settle the matter: all three of the Cid's champions win, the Infantes flee in shame, and the daughters are remarried to the Infantes of Navarre and Aragon.

Then comes the end. St. Peter appears to Rodrigo at midnight in Valencia and tells him he has thirty days left. Rodrigo uses them to make public confession, distribute his treasury, make his testament, and give his faithful men instructions for what must happen after he dies. He dies on the twenty-ninth day of May 1099, having drunk nothing in his final week but a spoonful of balsam and myrrh mingled with rose-water, his body and countenance growing fairer and fresher each day even as he weakens. Gil Díaz fits the embalmed body in a frame between two boards, sets it on Bavieca's saddle, and at midnight the entire garrison rides out of Valencia — silently, at a measured pace. Alvar Fañez attacks the Moorish host while the dead man on horseback, sword Tizona raised in his stiffened right arm, escorts Ximena to Castile. Twenty-two of Bucar's thirty-six kings die in the rout. The dead man rides all the way to San Pedro de Cardeña, sitting so upright on Bavieca that people come from Rioja and all Castile to see him and "hardly could they be persuaded that he was dead." He is enthroned in an ivory chair in the church, Tizona in his left hand, the strings of his mantle in his right, and remains there ten years — including one occasion when he draws the sword a palm's length from the scabbard when a Jew reaches for his beard. Ximena is buried at his feet. Bavieca lives two and a half years after his master and is buried before the monastery gate with two elms planted at his head and feet. The inscription Alfonso the Wise later orders cut into the stone sums Rodrigo entire: *Before Valencia I conquered them, on horseback, after I was dead, being the seventy and second battle which I won.*

## PASS 2 — Key Characters

**Rodrigo Díaz de Bivar / El Cid Ruydiez / the Campeador**
The pivot of every event. His defining quality — named by both enemies and the narrator — is that he never did a disloyal thing. Not loyalty as submission but loyalty as structural integrity: he holds his own word absolutely, demands others hold theirs, and neither begs nor dissembles. The Santa Gadea oath-taking is his first pivotal choice: he could let Alfonso's guilt slide and remain at court. He forces the issue, knowing the cost. His second defining choice comes during the siege of Valencia when he learns of the Corpes outrage — his immediate response is to control his rage and begin legal proceedings rather than ride to war against Castilian nobles: "Thanks be to Christ, by this beard which no one hath ever cut, the Infantes of Carrion shall not triumph in this!" He chooses justice over vengeance every time, and he chooses it knowing that justice will be slower and more painful.

**Doña Ximena Gomez**
Daughter of Count Lozano, who was killed by Rodrigo in a judicial duel before the Chronicle proper begins. She petitioned King Don Ferrando to give her in marriage to the man who had slain her father — a startling act of will framed as a demand for honor rather than surrender. In the Chronicle she is consistently Rodrigo's anchor: weeping but upright at the Cardeña farewell, governing the household in his absence, trembling on the high tower at Valencia with the first sight of Moorish armies (Rodrigo: "Fear not, honoured woman"), holding her daughter Sol's hand when Sol tears her golden hair at the sight of the embalmed father on horseback: "Daughter, you do ill, in that you break the command of your father, who laid his curse upon all who should make lamentation for him." She outlives Rodrigo by four years, spending them beside his ivory chair, then is buried at his feet.

**King Don Alfonso VI**
The man whose character the whole story judges. He accepts the Santa Gadea oath but never forgives it, and he exiles Rodrigo twice — the second time while Rodrigo is actively fighting his wars. His worst act is the Valencia marriages: he arranges his own champion's daughters to the Infantes of Carrión for political reasons, over Rodrigo's deep misgivings, and then has to live with what follows at Corpes. His partial rehabilitation comes at Toledo, where he convenes the Cortes and enforces justice on the Infantes — and at San Pedro de Cardeña at the end, where he travels from Toledo to do honour to the dead Cid, and stands looking at the fresh face and open eyes and "marvelled greatly."

**Alvar Fañez Minaya**
"My right hand." The steady professional soldier where Rodrigo is the inspired strategist. After Alcocer, when his horse is killed under him and he fights on foot, Rodrigo gives him his own horse: "Mount, Minaya, for you are my right hand." He is the political emissary — three times carries tribute and gifts to Alfonso, manages the court relationships, pleads for Rodrigo's reinstatement. He is also the man who sets the final battle order at Valencia, commanding the attack on Bucar's host while the dead man rides beside the body-guard.

**Pero Bermudez ("Pero Mudo")**
Called Dumb-ee or the Silent because he has a stutter and speaks slowly, which creates a perpetual comedy of frustration: he is always the first to act and the last to finish explaining himself. His defining moment is at Alcocer when, unable to hold himself back, he breaks formation and carries the Cid's banner straight into the Moorish host before any order is given. The Cid shouts after him in fury — then the whole army charges to cover him and wins the battle. At Toledo, Pero Bermudez is the one who finally punches Count Garcia Ordoñez to the floor when the mockery becomes insufferable, and he is the first of the three champions to fight at Carrión, driving his lance through Ferrando González's triple breastplate until the man confesses conquest to avoid the sword Tizona.

**Martin Antolinez**
"The good Burgalese." He is the man who arranges the gold-chest trick with the Jewish moneylenders at the very start of the exile, and who stands by the Cid in Burgos when the whole town is too afraid to help. At Carrión he faces Diego González with the sword Colada: "the brightness of which flashed over the whole field, for it was a marvellous sword." One blow shears off the crown of Diego's helmet, cuts away hood and coif and hair and skin. Diego González, still holding his own sword, is too terrified to use it: "Great God, help me and save me from that sword!" He rides away; Martin Antolinez drives him from the lists.

**The Infantes of Carrión — Diego González and Ferrando González**
Cowards and courtiers who accept a marriage their pride cannot sustain. The lion episode establishes them: when the Cid's lion escapes its cage and paces the hall, Ferrando hides under the Cid's own bench, tearing his mantle and doublet at the shoulders; Diego leaps into a wine-press and comes out drenched in lees, crying "I shall never see Carrión again!" Rodrigo simply takes the lion back by the mane. The Infantes nurse the humiliation until their resolve to revenge themselves becomes the Corpes outrage — stripping and flogging Doña Elvira and Doña Sol with saddle-girths and spurs in the oak forest, leaving them for the wolves. Their logic is legible even while monstrous: "Badly were we matched with them, and now for the after-feast he hath made this mockery of us."

**The Daughters — Doña Elvira and Doña Sol**
Never given much interior life in Southey's rendering but two moments of their own remain: in the Corpes forest, even being beaten to unconsciousness, they appeal to the Infantes' honor — "Don Diego and Don Ferrando, ye have strong swords of sharp edge; the one is called Colado and the other Tizona; cut off our heads and we shall become martyrs!" — that is, they would rather a clean death than a shameful survival. And they write their letter home to Rodrigo in their own blood.

**Gil Díaz (formerly Alfaraxi)**
The Moorish Alcalde of Valencia who converts voluntarily in Rodrigo's final years. He becomes Rodrigo's most intimate servant at the end: it is Gil Díaz who fits the boards to the embalmed body, saddles Bavieca, leads the horse through the night gates of Valencia, leaves the Arabic inscription on the Alcazar wall. He tends Bavieca for the rest of the horse's life, and at his own death requests burial beside the horse he had loved.

## PASS 3 — Elaborated Scene: The Dead Man Rides from Valencia

The directive came from Rodrigo himself, on the twenty-ninth day before he died. He told Ximena and the Bishop Hieronymo and Alvar Fañez and Pero Bermudez and Gil Díaz: wash my body with rose-water three times, anoint it with the balsam from the Soldan of Persia's gift — "from head to foot, so that every part shall be anointed." Make no cries, no lamentation. Sound the trumpets and tambours from the walls every day so the Moors know nothing has changed. And then: "saddle my horse Bavieca, and arm him well; and ye shall apparel my body full seemlily, and place me upon the horse, and fasten and tie me thereon so that it cannot fall; and fasten my sword Tizona in my hand."

He died on a Sunday, at the hour of sexts, having received the host on his knees and said his prayer: "Lord Jesus Christ, thine is the power and the kingdom, and thou art above all Kings and all nations... I beseech thee therefore pardon me my sins, and let my soul enter into the light which hath no end." His countenance did not change. They washed the body twice with warm water and once with rose-water, anointed it with the Persian balsam, carried it to the Church of St. Mary of the Virtues to keep vigil. Three days later King Bucar arrived at the port of Valencia with thirty-six Moorish kings and a Moorish queen who was a negress, commanding two hundred horsewomen archers with their hair shorn in token of holy war. Fifteen thousand tents went up around the walls.

The company of the Cid played their part exactly. They sounded trumpets and tambours from the walls each morning. They fought off the daily assaults, killing Moors who came blind up to the masonry. All this time Gil Díaz was working. He took the embalmed body — "the flesh remained firm and fair, having its natural colour, and his countenance as it was wont to be, and the eyes open, and his long beard in order, so that there was not a man who would have thought him dead if he had seen him and not known it" — and placed it on a noble saddle fastened to a wooden frame. He dressed the body in a gambax of fine sendal next to the skin. He took two boards and hollowed them out to fit perfectly against breast and back, one board reaching from shoulders to pole, the other from breast to chin, and bolted them through the saddle so the body sat as if vertebrate and willed. He dressed it in painted hose of black and white, cunningly painted to resemble greaves and cuisses, and a surcoat of green sendal with Rodrigo's arms blazoned on it, and a helmet of parchment painted to look like iron. He hung the shield around the neck. He placed Tizona in the right hand and raised the arm and fastened it at the angle of a man about to strike.

They loaded every sumpter beast in Valencia with everything worth taking. They told the Moorish population in the suburbs nothing. At midnight, on the twelfth day after Rodrigo's death, they went out through the gate of Roseros, which faces toward Castile, so quietly and at so measured a pace that even with the whole garrison moving "it seemed as if there were only a score." Pero Bermudez went first with the banner and five hundred knights. Then the baggage train. Then — in the center of the column, with a hundred chosen men around him — the Cid on Bavieca. The Bishop Hieronymo rode on the dead man's left, Gil Díaz on his right, leading the horse by the bridle as Rodrigo had commanded. Behind them Ximena with her women, and six hundred knights in the rear.

Alvar Fañez had set the battle order. The moment the column was clear of the gate he attacked the nearest Moorish encampment — the camp of the Moorish negress queen, the Star of the Archers. The onset was so sudden they killed a hundred and fifty men before anyone reached a horse. The queen herself got mounted and led fifty archers against the attackers and did "some hurt" before they killed her. Then the Moorish host broke. What the Moors saw — or what Southey reports they saw — was "full seventy thousand knights, all as white as snow," and before them a knight of great stature on a white horse with a bloody cross, carrying a white banner in one hand and a sword of fire in the other. They ran for the sea. Of the thirty-six kings, twenty-two died in the water or in the field. King Bucar himself hoisted sails and never looked back.

The Cid's column did not stop to watch. They were already nine leagues east, halting for the night at a village called Siete Aguas — the Seven Waters. The dead man was still on Bavieca. At each halt Gil Díaz lifted the saddle and frame off the horse and set it on a trestle, and when they moved again mounted it back. They went by short stages to Salvacañete, then Osma, then Santesteban, then San Pedro de Cardeña. Riders went ahead with letters. People came from Rioja and all Castile, from all the country round about, to see the Cid coming in that guise, and "when they saw him their wonder was the greater, and hardly could they be persuaded that he was dead." Doña Sol came out with a hundred knights, shields reversed, weeping in grey cloaks and linsey-woolsey, and when she saw her father upright on Bavieca half a league out from Osma she tore off her headdress and began pulling out her hair — it was like threads of gold — and Ximena held her hand and told her quietly: your father left his curse on anyone who made lamentation for him. Doña Sol put her headdress back on and kissed his hand.

King Alfonso rode from Toledo taking long stages. He found the Cid at Cardeña, still on Bavieca, and stood looking at the open eyes and fresh countenance, greatly astonished. He gave order for the ivory chair from the Toledo Cortes to be set on the right of the altar of St. Peter, covered with a cloth of gold and a cushion of tartarí. He himself, with the King of Navarre and the Infante of Aragon, helped lift the body from between the boards. It would not bend: the flesh was still firm, the color still natural. They dressed it in purple cloth from the Soldan of Persia's gift, seated it in the ivory chair with Tizona in the left hand and the mantle strings in the right. In that posture the Cid remained for ten years.

The tomb inscription carved at Alfonso the Wise's command says it plainly: *Before Valencia I conquered them, on horseback, after I was dead, being the seventy and second battle which I won.*

## 1367 CONNECTION

The terrain of the Cid's early campaigns is the terrain of the Black Prince's greatest victory. Southey's text names Logroño and Nájara (Nájera) explicitly as Riojan towns whose territory passed between Moorish and Christian hands in Rodrigo's time — Book I records a Moorish raid that "entered the lands of Castille as far as Logroño and Najara" which Rodrigo was sent to avenge. Later, when Alfonso exiles Rodrigo a second time, Rodrigo himself burns and raids Logroño and Alfaro as a direct challenge to the King's authority. These are the same towns — the same valley of the Najerilla meeting the Ebro, the same bridgehead ridge at Nájera — where in April 1367 the Black Prince and Pedro of Castile broke Enrique of Trastámara's French-backed army. Three centuries separate the two campaigns. The ground does not change: the Cid fought over Riojan soil to define what Castile meant; the Black Prince fought over the same soil to define who would hold Castile's throne. A player character in the 1367 game walks through a landscape still named by the Cid's passage.

## PASS 4 — Seven Storyline Ideas

**1. The Chest of Sand (Exile's First Night, Book I)**
The Cid needs funds to equip sixty men for a campaign in banishment. Martin Antolinez brokers a deal with two Jewish moneylenders, Raquel and Vidas, using two locked chests of sand presented as Rodrigo's gold — pledged against repayment with interest. The moneylenders, forbidden by the Cid's command to open the chests until he returns, never discover the deception until the loan has been repaid in full with honest conquest-gold. Quest seed: a player must obtain supplies under false pretenses in enemy territory, with the moral weight that full repayment is the only way to keep the original lie from becoming a crime.

**2. The Lion and the Bench (Valencia, Book IX)**
The Cid is asleep when his caged lion escapes. Knights freeze; the Infantes of Carrión disgrace themselves spectacularly. Rodrigo wakes, walks into the hall, takes the lion by the mane, and leads it back to its cage. The lion does not resist. Quest seed: an encounter with a predator that will yield to unafraid authority — tests whether a player character chooses fight, flight, or a third option.

**3. The Oath at Santa Gadea (Book I)**
Rodrigo forces King Alfonso to swear three times on the Gospels, touching the Gospels with twelve knights in witness, under a curse that echoes Vellido Dolfos's murder of King Sancho — "may you die even such a death as your brother, by the hand of a villain whom you trust, one who is not a hidalgo." The King swears. Rodrigo tells him: "Since you have sworn it, King, I wish you well of the oath; but from this day forward I would fain not be in your court." Quest seed: forcing a powerful figure to swear a binding oath in public — the diplomatic courage required, and the cost of succeeding.

**4. The Famine of Valencia (Books VI–VII)**
Before Valencia falls, the Cid enforces a siege so total that the city's defenders eat their own horses, then dogs and cats, then wash grape stones and sell them as food. A pound of meat costs three maravedís. Rodrigo watches from the hills. Quest seed: siege logistics as a player problem — managing supply lines, preventing relief columns, holding nerve through the moral weight of the suffering visible inside the walls.

**5. Corpes — The Oak Forest (Book IX)**
The Infantes lead their wives deep into the forest, send all company ahead on pretense, strip and beat Doña Elvira and Doña Sol with saddle-girths, and leave them "to the mountain birds and to the beasts of the forest." Felez Muñoz, the Cid's nephew, doubled back out of instinct and finds them: "Cousins! Cousins! Doña Elvira! Doña Sol! for the love of God rouse yourselves." Doña Sol asks only for water. Quest seed: a rescue in enemy wilderness, arriving too late to prevent harm, early enough to prevent death — carrying wounded people through hostile terrain to reach the Cid.

**6. The Toledo Cortes — Three Demands (Book IX)**
Rodrigo enters with a hundred armored knights, his long beard bound with a cord, and takes his ivory seat beside the King's. Garcia Ordoñez mocks it. Rodrigo makes his three demands in sequence: first the swords, then the dowry gold, then the naming of the Infantes as traitors and dogs. The third demand causes uproar — Pero Bermudez punches Garcia Ordoñez — and requires the King to impose order and decree the three combats. Quest seed: a legal proceeding in open court where the player must sequence demands correctly to prevent the proceedings from collapsing into violence before justice is obtained.

**7. The Dead Man's Ride (Book XI)**
The full exit from Valencia — loading the city, deceiving the Moorish suburbs, timing Alvar Fañez's attack to cover the column's departure, escorting a dead man in armor on horseback through two weeks of road to a monastery in Castile without breaking formation or triggering panic among the people who come to see him along the way. Quest seed: a long escort of a sacred cargo under a deadline, where the cargo's true nature must be concealed, and the emotional cost of the mission falls on those who know.

## PASS 5 — 5-Act Vignette Play: "The Cord and the Beard"

*A Fighter has been hired as a personal escort for an unnamed nobleman traveling from Burgos to a Toledo court appearance. The nobleman carries a sealed deposition — evidence against two powerful Counts who wronged a woman in his household. The TOKEN ITEM is a knotted cord of plaited black horsehair — the nobleman binds his beard with it each morning. The cord is visible to anyone who knows what it means: it is a vow-cord, worn until the vow is discharged.*

*The Thing · black horsehair cord · The Nobleman · tied in his beard*

---

**ACT I — Departure from Burgos**

*Morning. The inn yard at Burgos. The Nobleman's servants load three mules. One servant whispers to the Fighter that their employer has not cut his beard in four months and will not until he has spoken in open court. The Fighter is given no explanation. The Nobleman comes out, binds the cord himself, mounts without a word.*

*The Thing · black horsehair cord · The Nobleman · tied in his beard*

*The Fighter observes: the Nobleman's hands do not shake when he ties the cord, but his jaw does.*

The road south from Burgos crosses open country before it reaches the pass. Three riders appear on the ridge above the road — retainers of the Count whose behavior is the subject of the deposition. They do not attack openly; they follow at a distance that could be called coincidence. The Fighter must decide whether to challenge them, ignore them, or find a third road.

---

**ACT II — The Waystation at Nájera**

*The road passes through Riojan country — the valley where Logroño stands beside the Ebro, where three centuries earlier the Cid burned and raided in defiance of his king. The Nobleman knows the history. He stops at the bridge at Nájera and stands looking at the water for a long time.*

*The Thing · black horsehair cord · The Nobleman · tied in his beard, one end fraying*

*The Fighter observes: the cord has begun to fray at the tying end. The Nobleman notices, too, and tucks the fraying piece under the main knot without comment.*

At the waystation inn a local knight recognizes the Nobleman and takes offense at the vow-cord — he is a client of the Count's family, and the cord's meaning is an insult in his county. He demands the Nobleman remove it publicly or answer for it. The Nobleman will not remove it. He stands perfectly still and says nothing. The Fighter must manage the confrontation without the Nobleman's help.

---

**ACT III — The Mountain Pass**

*The road climbs toward Toledo through a narrow pass. Snow on the upper rocks. Three days from the city.*

*The Thing · black horsehair cord · The Nobleman · tied in his beard, fraying badly, held together by a careful second knot the Fighter tied last night at the Nobleman's request*

*The Fighter observes: it is the first time the Nobleman has asked for anything.*

The pass is held by a toll-gate operated by a third party with no allegiance — they serve whoever pays. A rider has paid them to delay all traffic for two days on grounds of "road inspection." This is not a fight the Fighter can win by force; the toll guards are twenty and within a fortified post. The delay will cause the Nobleman to miss his court date.

---

**ACT IV — Toledo, the Night Before Court**

*The city. The inn near the court building. The Nobleman spends the evening in his room. He sends his servant for wine and does not drink it. At midnight he asks the Fighter to come in.*

*The Thing · black horsehair cord · The Nobleman · in his hand, unbound from his beard, being re-examined*

*The Fighter observes: the cord has been unbound for the first time since Burgos. The Nobleman is re-reading the deposition by candlelight.*

He tells the Fighter only this: the woman in his household was his daughter. The Counts who wronged her are powerful. He will name them publicly in court tomorrow, in sequence — the evidence first, then the witnesses, then the formal accusation. He has rehearsed the order carefully because if he names them too early the proceedings can be ended on procedural grounds. He needs to know the Fighter will be in the room, visible, armed. Not to threaten anyone. Just to be there.

---

**ACT V — Open Court**

*The court hall. The Nobleman stands and makes his three demands in sequence: first the evidence, then the witnesses, then the naming. At the naming, one of the Counts rises. The room goes loud. The Nobleman does not raise his voice.*

*The Thing · black horsehair cord · The Nobleman's hand · held loosely, ready to be removed*

*The Fighter observes: when the King's clerk confirms the Nobleman's right to proceed, the Nobleman reaches up and unties the cord from his beard for the last time. He does not throw it down or make a gesture. He folds it once and puts it in his pocket.*

When the Count's men make their move — not violence, but a procedural challenge designed to force the Nobleman to respond before the King has spoken — the Fighter's visible stillness is the answer. No sword drawn. No step taken. Simply present, and demonstrably unintimidated. The King's clerk looks at the Fighter, looks at the Nobleman, and rules in favor of proceeding.

## Cycle 2 · The Pledge and the Sand

**New angle:** Cycle 1 followed the honor of endurance — a vow-cord worn for months, grievance carried in silence through Riojan country to a Toledo court. Cycle 2 enters the financial architecture of exile: the specific moral logic by which a deception becomes an absolute obligation. The chest-of-sand gambit is not dishonest in Rodrigo's universe because he has always intended full repayment in honest conquest-gold; the lie is provisional, the debt is permanent. What this reveals that cycle 1 missed: the Cid's honor is not only about patience and public procedure — it includes a willingness to enter necessity's arrangements and then discharge them more completely than anyone required.

---

### PASS — The Chest of Sand: New Lens

The episode appears early, in Book I, at the very first night of exile: sixty men, no funds, Burgos locked against them. Martin Antolinez goes into the city and returns before dawn with 600 marks against two locked chests of the Cid's goods. The chests contain sand — but the moneylenders Raquel and Vidas have been told the contents are gold and silver plate, sealed under oath and too precious to move. They are forbidden to open them until the debt is repaid or one year has passed. What Martin Antolinez and the Cid know that the moneylenders do not: there is nothing in the chests. What everyone in the scene understands implicitly: if the Cid does not return and repay the loan with real gold, the chests will be opened, the fraud discovered, and Raquel and Vidas will be ruined.

Rodrigo takes this seriously. The repayment, when it comes, is made with honest conquest-gold: principal plus interest, more than the chests ever held. Raquel and Vidas are paid in full, open the chests, find sand, and — having been paid more than any gold that was ever their due — receive the proceeds without complaint.

The quest lives in the gap between those two counting-house meetings. Someone must carry the obligation from the night of exile to the morning of settlement.

---

### Token Doctrine

**TOKEN: The Counterpart Loan Receipt**
A sealed folio bearing all three signatures and the loan terms: the amount, the collateral, the prohibition against opening the chests. Martin Antolinez retains this counterpart. It is the only document that proves the debt was entered honestly — and the only document that, if seized by the Cid's enemies, could be used to argue the transaction was fraudulent. The Fighter carries it from the counting house through the night to Cardeña, holds it through the six months of campaign, and returns it to the flame at settlement.

**ACT III — The Bridge (Six Months Later)**

*Open Castilian country. The Cid's first campaign has yielded conquest-gold. Martin Antolinez has assembled the repayment. The Fighter is the courier, six months into campaign, riding back toward Burgos.*

A count's road agent stops the party at a bridge. He has been watching for Cid-connected couriers for weeks. He asks about the document in the pack. He knows what he's looking for: a receipt bearing two Burgalese names and the Cid's mark. If he takes it to the crown magistrate and argues the transaction was fraudulent, the repayment becomes a confession instead of a discharge.

**ACT IV — The Poplars**

*Two leagues from Burgos. Afternoon. A stand of poplars beside the road.*

*The Thing · counterpart loan receipt · sealed folio · Fighter's pack · targeted*

*The Fighter observes: three riders in the poplars. No livery. Waiting for something specific.*

The count's men are not authorized for this. They're acting on private instruction: if the receipt never reaches Burgos, the transaction never closes, and the open loan becomes a standing fraud that can be reported at any time. They want the document, not blood — but they'll take what they need to get it.

**ACT V — The Second Meeting**

*Morning. Raquel and Vidas' counting house. The repayment on the table. Martin Antolinez presents it.*

*The Thing · counterpart loan receipt · sealed folio · Fighter's hands*

*The Fighter observes: Vidas counts the coin twice. He counts it a third time. He nods to Raquel. Raquel takes the key from her belt and opens the first chest.*

Sand. The second chest: sand. Vidas looks at the coin for a long time. He looks at Martin Antolinez. He says nothing. He takes his copy of the receipt — the moneylenders' original — and holds it to the candle flame. It burns quickly. Martin Antolinez says: the counterpart. The Fighter puts the sealed folio on the candle. It goes the same way. No one says anything else. The chests are taken to the back room. The transaction is closed.
