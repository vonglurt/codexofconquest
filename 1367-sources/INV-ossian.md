# INV — Fragments of Ancient Poetry (Ossian)
*Source read: full text (2,299 lines, 87 KB). James Macpherson, pub. 1760. Gutenberg ID 8161.*

---

## Four Sentences

James Macpherson published these fifteen prose-poem fragments in 1760, claiming to have translated them from ancient Gaelic manuscripts composed by the legendary Highland bard Ossian — a claim that ignited the most ferocious literary controversy of the eighteenth century, with Samuel Johnson calling Macpherson a fraud to his face and the Scottish literary establishment defending the texts in kind. Whether Macpherson invented Ossian wholesale or distilled genuine oral tradition remains unsettled, but the cultural detonation was total: the fragments created Romantic primitivism, gave Napoleon his campfire reading, made Goethe weep through *The Sorrows of Young Werther*, and proved that a single invented voice could reshape European taste for a century. The fragments are set in a legendary Highland past corresponding roughly to the 3rd–5th centuries AD, and their fifteen episodes cover a narrow band of human experience — warriors leave for battle, women die waiting, lovers kill the wrong person in the dark, brothers destroy each other over a woman, and aged survivors sit by cold fires unable to name what they have lost. What endures past every controversy about authenticity is the grief: these prose-poems are the purest elegiac voice in English before Keats, and every fragment ends the same way — the hall is cold, the grass grows between the cairn-stones, and the last person left alive is blind.

## Long Summary — Heroes, Decisions, Romance, Human Condition

### The World

The world of Ossian has no towns. It has hills, glens, sea-rocks, oak trees, and moors. The great king Fingal rules from somewhere off-page; his son Oscian is the collection's narrator — blind, ancient, already the last of Fingal's race by the time he tells these stories. His warriors Oscur (Oscian's own son) and Dermid fight under Fingal; his bard Ullin will mourn them all when it is over. This is not a political world. It is a world of private grief wearing the costume of heroic action. The weather is always wrong — storms arrive when someone must not sail; mist covers the hill when a signal is waited for; mid-day silence descends when a ghost appears.

### Fragments I and II — Shilric and Vinvela

The first two fragments form a diptych. Fragment I is a dialogue across distance: Shilric the warrior speaks to Vinvela his love from the mustering-field; she answers from the hill above. He must go to war with Fingal. She will wait. He asks her: raise my grave with grey stones if I fall. She says she will remember him. In Fragment II, the narrator sits by a mossy fountain and sees someone approach — bright as the moon in autumn. She speaks: she heard of his death; she mourned him; she died. "Shilric, I am pale in the tomb." She fleets away like grey mist before the wind. He calls after her. She does not stay. No burial scene. No explanation. She simply ceased while he was away, and now she appears only when mid-day is silent.

### Fragment III — Carryl and Malcolm

The grey-haired Carryl sits alone in a hollow rock and mourns Malcolm, drowned at sea returning from war. His widow waits on the shore; the promised ship never came. Carryl sees Malcolm's ghost on a meteor of fire — green-limbed, aerial — then loses it in the trees. He resolves to sit by the stream of the plain and preserve Malcolm's name in song. This is the Ossian world's answer to death: the bard sits down and composes. It is not much. It is everything.

### Fragments IV and V — Connal and Crimora

This pair is one of the most devastating sequences in the collection. Crimora loves the warrior Connal and has followed him to battle against the enormous Dargo, arming herself in her father's gear. She draws her string on Dargo — and the arrow misses. It pierces Connal instead. He falls like an oak on the plain. Fragment V is the aftermath: Crimora tends his mortal wound and describes, in real time, the double tomb she imagines for them both. She arranges the cairn-stones in her mind. With grief the sad mourner died. The final line: "Undisturbed you now sleep together; in the tomb of the mountain you rest alone." She used her grief to make them permanent.

### Fragment VI — Oscur and the Daughter of Cremor

Fingal's family, returning from the hunt, encounters a woman fleeing the warrior Ullin, who has killed her father and seeks her. Fingal's sons vow protection. Ullin arrives with a ship — monstrous, binding Fingal's three sons and killing the girl before Oscur can reach her. Oscur descends in his armor; they fight all day; at evening, his blade finds Ullin's heart. Both the pursued and the pursuer die the same day. Oscian, telling it now as a blind old man: "forgive the tears of the aged."

### Fragment VII — Oscur, Dermid, and Dargo's Daughter

This is the collection's most intricate tragedy, and the one that carries the most weight as a human document. The narrator is Oscian — he is telling the story of his own son's death.

Dermid and Oscur, whose "friendship was strong as their steel, and death walked between them to the field," kill the chief Dargo in battle. Dargo's daughter is fair as the morning. Both warriors love her. Her soul is fixed on Oscur — she has forgotten her father's blood; she loves the hand that slew him.

Dermid cannot live with this. He asks Oscur directly: "Here, pierce this bosom, Oscur; relieve me, my friend, with thy sword." Oscur refuses. "My sword, son of Morny, shall never be stained with the blood of Dermid." Dermid presses: "Who then is worthy to slay me? Let none but Oscur slay me. Send me with honour to the grave."

"Dermid, make use of thy sword; son of Moray, wield thy steel. Would that I fell with thee!"

They fight by the brook of the mountain, by the streams of Branno. Dermid the graceful fell — and fell smiling in death. He got what he asked for.

Oscur returns to Dargo's daughter. He is already weary of life, carrying his friend's death. He tells her: his aim has gone wrong — he can no longer hit the shield of Gormur fixed on a tree by the brook. She offers to try: "My hands were taught the bow; my father delighted in my skill."

She went. He stood behind the shield. Her arrow flew and pierced his breast.

"Blessed be that hand of snow; and blessed thy bow of yew! I fall resolved on death: and who but the daughter of Dargo was worthy to slay me? Lay me in the earth, my fair-one; lay me by the side of Dermid."

She understands everything at once — what he told her, what he concealed, what he gave her. She pierces her white bosom with steel. She fell; she trembled; and died.

Three graves by the brook. A birch's unequal shade covers their tomb. Oscian, the blind father, telling this story: "my eyes are blind with tears; but memory beams on my heart."

The footnote Macpherson appended: *"Nothing was held by the ancient Highlanders more essential to their glory than to die by the hand of some person worthy or renowned. This was the occasion of Oscur's contriving to be slain by his mistress, now that he was weary of life."*

### Fragment VIII — The Last of Fingal's Race

Ossian sits on the moss under aged trees, sightless, alone. He is the last. He cannot see, but he feels the tombs with his hands. He names Fingal, Oscur, Fillan — the river murmurs below. He mourns and names them before the names can disappear.

### Fragment IX — Ronnan, Rivine, Connan, and Euran

Ronnan leaves for a year in Norway, entrusting his beloved Rivine to her brother Connan. A rival named Durstan threatens; Connan drives him off. Then a servant named Euran — the only villain in the entire collection, named once and never punished — tells Ronnan separately, and Connan separately, that Durstan will come through the gate of the north that night to take Rivine. Both men arm themselves and go to the gate in total darkness. They meet each other. Ronnan kills Connan — "And is it my friend, cries Ronnan, I have slain? O Connan! I knew thee not." Ronnan then finds the real Durstan and fights him; both fall at dawn. Rivine finds all of them. She opens the grave for the warriors and falls into it herself before it is closed, like the sun snatched away in a storm. Euran the servant received no punishment. Euran is not mentioned again. One lie. An entire family.

### Fragment X — The Woman at Shalgar's Rock

A woman waits alone on the hill of storms at night for her lover Shalgar, who comes from an enemy clan. She has come in secret. She finds two men dead at the appointed rock — Shalgar and her own brother, who killed each other in the dark without knowing. She announces she will die there. She does not ask permission. "My life flieth away like a dream: why should I stay behind?"

### Fragment XI — Armyn, Daura, Armor, and Arindel

The most elaborate single narrative. Daura, daughter of Armyn, is beloved by the warrior Armor. Earch, whose brother Armor had killed, disguises himself as a sailor and rows Daura out to a sea-rock on the pretense that Armor waits there. He abandons her, stranded as the tide rises. Her signal fire draws Arindel, her brother — who descends from the hill with his bow and dogs and, seeing a ship approach, fires at it. The grey-feathered shaft sinks into Arindel's own boat. No — into Armor's chest. Arindel drowns in the storm trying to reach his sister. Armor drowns trying to save Daura. Daura cries from the rock — Armor's name, Arindel's name, her father's name. She dies before morning. Armyn stands all night on the shore listening. He outlives all of them, old and blind in the cold hall: "my soul is a flame that has spent its force."

### Fragment XII — The Elegy for Morar

A pure elegy. Morar was swift as a deer, terrible as a meteor, gentle in peace. He is dead. Four gray stones. A moss-covered mound. A blind father who cannot find the grave. "Thou wert swift, O Morar! as a roe on the desert; terrible as a meteor of fire. Thy wrath was as the storm of December. Thy sword in battle, as lightning in the field." And now: grass between cairn-stones and silence.

### Fragments XIII–XV — Cuchulaid, Duchommar, Lamderg

Fragment XIII: The warrior Cuchulaid holds a mountain pass against Garve's fleet; his scout Moran brings intelligence. The focus is the moment before battle — knowledge the numbers are wrong, the choice to stand anyway.

Fragment XIV: Duchommar loves Morna; Morna loves Cadmor; Duchommar kills Cadmor and offers Morna his sword so she may grieve in the traditional way. She takes the sword and stabs him with it. He pulls it free and stabs her back as she approaches. With her last effort she rolls a stone between their bodies so their blood will not mingle. The stone is her final refusal — even dead, she will not be his.

Fragment XV: Lamderg fights Firchios over Gealchossa. Lamderg returns wounded; Gealchossa learns Firchios is dead — and dies of grief in the same moment, because she loved Firchios too, and would not have chosen between them.

### The Human Condition

Three structural tragedies repeat in variation across all fifteen fragments:

1. **The Waiting Woman.** She does not survive what she waits for. Vinvela dies. Daura dies crying names. Shalgar's love arrives to find two corpses. The men who caused them to wait come back to find only graves or air.

2. **The Dark Meeting.** Two people who should not fight meet where they cannot see each other and destroy each other. Connal and Dargo at the battlefield. Ronnan and Connan at the northern gate. Dermid and Oscur by the brook of Branno. In every case, someone wanted the meeting — grief arranged it, or love's impossible math, or one treacherous servant's lie.

3. **The Last Survivor.** One person is left with full knowledge of everyone who is gone, and their role is only to name the dead before the names are lost entirely. Ossian himself is the ultimate last survivor — blind, cold, feeling tombs with his hands, speaking their names into the wind.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Ghost at Noon** — Shilric has returned from Fingal's wars to find only Vinvela's ghost on the hill where she promised to wait. A Perception check DC 13 determines whether the player recognizes her for what she is before speaking to her — and whether the conversation held with the dead brings peace or breaks the speaker.

2. **Crimora's Arrow** — Crimora has armed herself and will enter the battle where Connal fights Dargo. The player must stop her without force or lose the chance entirely. Holding her back saves Connal; letting her through means arriving at the battlefield to find the wrong man dead and the wrong woman weeping over him.

3. **The Shield of Gormur** — Escort Dargo's daughter from the battlefield to Fingal's hall, guarding the rival warriors Oscur and Dermid who both love her. Witness Dermid's chosen death and Oscur's final trick at the oak by the brook. A quest where the Fighter's only power is to stand still, witness correctly, and carry what is left of the dead to their graves.

4. **Euran's Message** — Euran the servant has sent the same lie to both Ronnan and Connan: that Durstan will come through the gate of the north at night. Both men are armed and moving toward the same gate in the dark. The player has intercepted one of Euran's messages and has a single night-sprint to reach one man before they meet. Failing to arrive in time means finding two bodies at dawn and a third fighting for his life against Durstan.

5. **The Rock at Sea** — Earch has stranded Daura on the sea-rock. The player must escort the rescue boat through Arindel's patrol — who will fire on any approaching vessel — while the tide rises and Daura's signal fire dims. The window to reach her closes with the storm.

6. **The Blind Father's Stone** — Armyn cannot see to find Morar's grave. He sends the player with a hearthstone from the hall floor to place at the grave's head. Navigate between the hill's many unmarked cairns — the dead leave only grass and moss, and the living left no record of where they buried the greatest among them.

7. **Morna's Stone** — Duchommar and Morna have mortally wounded each other. The player arrives in time to carry one message between them — but Morna refuses any message. She asks only that the player place a stone between the two bodies so their blood will not mingle. A service quest: honoring a dying woman's last refusal costs nothing except the willingness to understand it.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Shield of Gormur*

*Storyline 3 — Escort Quest. After Oscur and Dermid slay Dargo in battle, his daughter must be escorted north to Fingal's hall. The Fighter is tasked with her safety. The two warriors who both love her ride with the column.*

**THE TOKEN: THE SHIELD OF GORMUR** — a battle-shield of old iron, rim-banded in dark bronze, won by Oscur from the warrior Gormur in single combat years before this story. Oscur carries it on his back as a trophy of his reputation — it is the most conspicuous thing in the column. The Fighter has been told: "Guard the girl. Guard the column. If Oscur needs both hands, you carry his shield." By Act V the shield will be laid over Oscur's grave and covered with stone.

---

### ACT I — *The Field of Dargo*
*The shield: on Oscur's back, iron-grey, dented from many battles. Location: the plain where Dargo fell.*

The ravens are already working. Dargo's men have raised a quick mound over their chief and retreated north. His daughter stands at the mound's edge. She does not weep. She does not look at the sky. She looks at the two warriors who killed her father.

Oscur and Dermid stand at thirty paces from each other, not speaking. Dermid has removed his helm. Oscur has not.

**Dermid,** approaching the girl, quietly: "Your father fought as one man should. His name will not be forgotten."

She does not answer. She turns north and begins to walk, toward Fingal's hall.

Oscur falls in behind her, the iron shield on his back. Dermid falls in last. The Fighter takes the middle position — between the girl and the two men who orbit her like moons held in place by something they will not name.

*The Fighter thinks: they killed her father this morning. They are in love with her this afternoon. I do not understand this world.*

---

### ACT II — *The Moor Road*
*The shield: on Oscur's back, catching torchlight when clouds part. Location: the high moor, three hours from the field.*

They march in near-silence. The girl walks ahead of everyone. Oscur and Dermid have not spoken since the plain. The Fighter watches the gap between them widen and close, widen and close, as though measuring something.

At the stream-ford, a war-party of six appears on the near bank — not Dargo's men, not Fingal's. Opportunists drawn by the sounds of the day's battle, hoping to take the shield and anything else worth carrying.

Oscur's hand goes to his sword. Dermid's is already drawn.

**The lead man:** "Set down the iron shield, friend. Set it down and walk on."

The shield comes off Oscur's back. He holds it on his left arm, sword in his right, and says nothing. He simply moves toward the water.

They break before he reaches the bank.

*The Fighter thinks: the shield is not his armor. The shield is his name. Any man who wanted it would have to kill him first.*

---

### ACT III — *The Brook of Branno*
*The shield: set against an oak by the Fighter, Oscur's hands now free. Location: a glade beside the stream, dusk.*

They make a cold camp above the brook. When the girl sleeps — or pretends to — Dermid speaks.

**Dermid:** "Her soul is fixed on Oscur. You know this."

**Oscur:** "I know."

**Dermid:** "Then one of us must go. Pierce this bosom, Oscur. Relieve me. You are the only one worthy."

**Oscur:** "My sword will never be stained with the blood of Dermid."

Long silence. The brook runs below them.

**Dermid:** "Son of Oscian — wield your steel. I will wield mine. Let death come from whoever it comes from."

Oscur removes the shield from his back and sets it against the oak beside the Fighter. He does not look at the Fighter. He looks at Dermid, and his voice is very quiet.

**Oscur:** "Would that I fell with thee."

They go into the trees, toward the brook. The sound of steel comes through the oaks — brief, certain, the way decisive things are. Then silence.

Dermid the graceful fell; fell, and smiled in death.

Oscur returns alone. His face is unchanged. He goes to the oak above the brook and hangs the shield from a high branch by a length of cord from his pack — the iron shield swaying slowly over the water. He does not explain. He walks back to camp and sits by the dead fire.

*The Fighter thinks: he has just killed his oldest friend. And he is hanging the shield in a tree. Something about the angle of it is wrong.*

---

### ACT IV — *The Oak Above the Stream*
*The shield: hanging from the branch, swaying in the morning air. Location: the brook of Branno.*

At morning, Oscur goes to the girl. He has prepared his face carefully.

**Oscur:** "Though once renowned for the bow — I have lost my aim. Fixed on that tree by the brook is the shield of Gormur the brave, whom I slew in battle. I have tried and cannot pierce it. My arm is wrong today."

She looks at him. Her father taught her the bow.

**Dargo's daughter:** "Let me try, son of Oscian. My hands were taught the bow. My father delighted in my skill."

She takes her bow and walks to the brook. Oscur walks with her, slightly ahead, positioning himself beside the oak. The shield hangs over the water, swaying. She sets her stance — her father's daughter, form perfect, the way he taught her.

The Fighter is twenty paces back. Something about the angle is wrong. Oscur is not standing where a man watches someone shoot. He is standing where a man stands if he is the target.

The Fighter opens their mouth.

She draws. She fires.

Oscur falls.

**Oscur,** from the ground: "Blessed be that hand of snow; and blessed thy bow of yew. I fall resolved on death. Lay me by the side of Dermid."

She stands with the empty bow. She looks at the shield still swaying. She looks at the angle. She understands everything at once — what he told her, what he didn't tell her, what he gave her.

She pierces her own breast with her knife. She falls by the stream. She trembles, and dies.

*The Fighter thinks: I almost called out. Almost. He had chosen this. Calling out would have been my comfort, not his honor. I do not know if I chose right. I will never know.*

---

### ACT V — *The Birch's Unequal Shade*
*The shield: taken from the branch, laid over Oscur's grave. Location: the brookside, then Fingal's hall.*

Three graves by the stream of Branno. Dermid. Oscur. Dargo's daughter. The Fighter and two surviving column-guards dug through the night. A birch stands unequally over all three mounds — its shade will cover them in summer, miss them in winter, as all shade eventually does.

At morning the Fighter climbs to the oak branch and unties the cord. The shield comes down. It is heavier than it seemed when Oscur carried it.

The Fighter lays it face-up over Oscur's grave before the final stones are placed. Iron on stone. It will rust slowly over years until the mound and the shield are one thing.

The column walks north without its warriors. The Fighter walks at the front.

At Fingal's hall, the bard Ullin asks: "Where is Oscur? Where is Dermid?"

The Fighter tells him. Everything — the field, the moor, the brook, the angle, the arrow.

Ullin listens without interrupting. He stands at the fire for a long time.

**Ullin:** "Oscur my son was brave. But Oscur is now no more." He begins to compose. "Son of Alpin, the woes of the aged are many: their tears are for the past."

The quest is done. The shield is in the earth. The song begins.

*The Fighter thinks: I carried his shield for three days and gave it to his grave. Now Ullin will carry his name. That is all that remains of great men — a song composed by someone who was not there.*

## Cycle 2 — Angle: Morna's Stone

**What this angle reveals that Cycle 1 missed:** Cycle 1 (The Shield of Gormur) showed the Ossianic world through its largest register — the escort of Dargo's daughter, the rival warriors, the chosen death of Oscur, the shield as monument. It was a story of male heroism witnessed by the Fighter. Cycle 2 shows the book's quietest act: a dying woman's last refusal. Morna does not want to be reconciled to Duchommar. She does not want his message. She wants a stone placed between them so their blood will not mingle. Cycle 1 missed entirely: the Ossianic elegy for women — the specific dignity Macpherson gives to the women who refuse what the men around them insist on; Morna's story as the refusal of the consoling narrative; and the specific service the Fighter can render that is not intervention but execution — not comforting, not saving, but carrying out an instruction exactly as given.

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling (Morna's Stone Angle)

Duchommar comes to Morna, the dark maid of the brook of Cona. He loves her. He tells her he has gone to Temora and killed Cormac — the man she loved, the man who was her rival's rival. He killed Cormac for her sake: this is how he presents it. He killed someone she loved and presents the killing as a gift.

Morna takes his own sword.

She does not weep, she does not argue the logic of what he has told her, she does not negotiate. She uses his sword on him. Then she is wounded — whether in the struggle or by her own hand or by a blow before he falls, Macpherson leaves slightly ambiguous — and they are both dying. Both on the ground in the vale of Cona with the stream nearby and the autumn light coming through whatever trees are left standing after years of war have stripped the highlands.

Macpherson's Ossian-fragments are full of this — the double death, the man and the woman, the grief that arrives in both directions simultaneously. But Morna's scene is particular because of the stone. She does not ask for revenge, does not ask for comfort, does not ask for her death to be reported to anyone. She asks for a stone between them. So their blood will not mingle on the ground. She refuses his narrative — she refused it with his sword — and she refuses it once more with the stone, and the refusal costs her nothing extra because she is already dying, and the request costs the witness only the willingness to understand what is being asked and why.

The why is: she loved Cormac. Duchommar killed Cormac and brought her the news as a gift. She will not lie down in the same blood as the man who did this. The stone is not a punishment. It is a boundary. She draws it because she can, because she still has the capacity to make one choice, and she makes it.

The Ossianic world is full of men who die well and whose deaths are remembered in song. Morna is a woman who dies well and whose final act is a refusal that will not be in any song — because refusals are not the material of Ossianic elegy, which prefers the reconciled dead lying together under cairns on the hill. Morna insists on something different. The stone is the only document of her insistence.

### Chapter Notes

**The Carric-Thura fragments:** Ossian's remembrance of Dargo, Shilric and Vinvela, battles and ghosts. The general atmosphere of autumn light and multiple grief-layers — every Ossianic fragment takes place after a battle and before a burial.

**Croma:** An old blind man asks Ossian to sing of Croma. Ossian sang of it. The song is for the benefit of the dead and the living simultaneously. The Ossianic bard's function: to make the dead present again in the form that living grief requires.

**Duchommar and Morna:** The short episode of the killing of Cormac and its immediate consequence. One of the fragments where Macpherson's pacing is at its most compressed: the story fits in a page, and the page contains two deaths and one request.

**The Shield of Gormur / Oscur / Dermid episodes:** The larger narrative fragments that gave Cycle 1 its material — the escort, the two warriors, the chose death, the burial.

**Colna-dona:** The princess who loved the warrior from an enemy tribe. Ossian's elegy for the love that the war made impossible — and for the warrior who died before the love could become anything except its own elegy.

---

### Pass 2 — Slower

**Morna:** Macpherson's description is "dark maid of the brook of Cona" — dark meaning not the valley but something that does not deflect. She is specific. She loved Cormac. She knows this. Duchommar kills Cormac and presents the act as love, and Morna receives this presentation correctly — not as love but as destruction of what she loved. She acts on the correct information. She dies with the correct information. The stone is her last act on the correct information.

**Duchommar:** He is not a villain in any simple sense. He loved Morna and killed Cormac because he loved Morna and could not see that killing Cormac was destroying what he was trying to win. He does not understand this. He brings the news as a gift. He cannot understand why the gift becomes the sword in her hand. He dies not understanding. The Ossianic tragedy is often this: the man acts from love and produces destruction and never quite sees the connection between the two.

**The Stone as Instrument:** Flat river stones from the brook of Cona. Morna specifies this not because the stone is magical but because the request is specific. She has a clear vision of what she wants. The stone is the material form of the distinction she is drawing: we are not the same story. She will not lie down in the same blood as the man who killed Cormac.

**The Fighter's Role:** The Fighter is not a mourner, not a judge. The Fighter is an executor — a person who arrives at the moment when someone makes a final request and is willing to carry it out exactly. No more. The Fighter does not comfort Morna, does not argue with Duchommar, does not attempt to reconcile them. They are both dying. One of them has made a specific request. The Fighter's service is to execute it.

### Pass 3 — The Scene: The Vale of Cona

The brook of Cona is low in autumn. Its sound is the sound of water over stones, which is one of the most neutral sounds in the world — it does not change for the quality of the event it accompanies. Duchommar is on the bank, sitting, his hand on his side. Morna is two feet from him, lying on the grass, her hand in the brook's edge. Her hand is open. The water runs through her fingers.

They are not looking at each other.

Duchommar is explaining something. He has been explaining something since before the Fighter arrived — it is the kind of explanation that is a continuation of something that has been going on a long time, the argument that was not really an argument because only one person was making it. He killed Cormac. He tells this again. He killed Cormac for her. He would kill anything for her. His voice is the voice of a man who has done everything he could do and cannot understand why it has come to this.

Morna looks at the water over her fingers.

The Fighter crouches beside her. She turns her head. She looks at the Fighter with the very clear eyes of someone who has been in pain long enough that the pain has become familiar.

**Morna:** "He will keep speaking."

**Fighter:** *(listening)*

**Morna:** "When we are both gone — put a stone between us. From the brook. Flat. I know which ones." She moves one finger in the water — pointing to a specific stone just below the surface, pale, flat, smoothed by years. "That one. Between us. So the blood does not mingle."

She does not explain why. She has already done everything that needed explaining. She looks back at the water.

Duchommar is still speaking behind the Fighter. His voice is becoming less clear. The explanation is moving toward its end.

*The Fighter reaches into the brook and lifts the stone.*

It is cold and heavy and smooth and the correct stone. Morna watches the Fighter lift it and closes her eyes.

It is not about grief-in-an-object, not about the witness holding still for someone else's heroism, not about love given away. ### 5-Act Vignette Play: *Morna's Stone*

**THE TOKEN: MORNA'S STONE** — A flat pale stone from the brook of Cona, smooth from years of water. Morna pointed to it from the brook's edge while she could still point. The Fighter lifted it from the water. It is cold and heavy and wet and the correct stone. By Act V it will be placed between two bodies so their blood will not mingle. The commission is specific. The stone is specific.

---

**ACT I — The Brook of Cona**
*Morna's stone: in the water, not yet lifted. Location: the vale of Cona, the brook's edge — new node CNA.*

The brook is low. The autumn light is the color of grass that has been warm all summer and is beginning to remember what cold means. Duchommar is on the bank. Morna is near the water, lying in the grass. Both are dying — the wounds are clear and the time is short.

Duchommar is explaining something. He has been explaining for a while.

Morna turns her head toward the Fighter.

**Morna:** "He will keep speaking." Her voice is clear and low and precise. "When we are both gone — put a stone between us. From the brook. Flat. I know which ones."

She moves one finger in the water. A specific stone, pale, smooth, just below the surface.

**Morna:** "That one. Between us. So the blood does not mingle."

She looks back at the water.

*The Fighter reaches into the brook and lifts the stone. It is cold and heavy and exactly as she described.*

*The Fighter thinks: she has already done everything that needed explaining. The stone is what remains.*

---

**ACT II — Duchommar's Account**
*Morna's stone: in the Fighter's hands, cold, dripping. Location: the brook bank.*

Duchommar has heard the Fighter lift the stone. He turns toward them. His face is the face of a man who has done everything he could do and cannot understand why it has come to this.

**Duchommar:** "She does not hear me. She has not heard me since — did she say — what did she ask you?"

*The Fighter holds the stone and does not answer.*

**Duchommar:** "Tell her — tell her I did not understand what I was taking from her. Tell her I would undo it if I could. She will not hear me. You can carry it."

*The Fighter thinks: he is asking me to change her instruction. The instruction was: no message.*

The brook runs. Duchommar's voice is becoming quieter — not softer, but the volume that indicates the end of what he has to say.

**Duchommar:** *(after a long silence)* "She will not have it."

**Fighter:** *(one word)* "No."

---

**ACT III — The Waiting**
*Morna's stone: held in both hands, warming slightly. Location: the vale, the bank.*

The afternoon passes slowly in the way that afternoons pass in Ossianic poems — too bright, the light too exact, the birds in the wrong trees. The Fighter sits between Duchommar and Morna on the bank and holds the stone and waits.

Duchommar sleeps — the final sleep that comes like sleep. His breathing changes.

Morna's eyes are open. She is watching the water.

*The Fighter thinks: she is waiting for me to be ready. She has been ready since she lifted his sword.*

At some point Morna says, without looking away from the water: "You have it."

Not a question.

**Fighter:** "Yes."

**Morna:** *(after a time)* "Good."

Her hand closes in the brook. The water runs over it.

---

**ACT IV — The Moment**
*Morna's stone: in the Fighter's right hand. Location: between the two dying figures.*

Morna's breathing changes. The change is small and complete.

The stone is in the Fighter's hand. The space between the two figures is two feet of autumn grass. The Fighter moves to the space and kneels and places the stone.

Flat. Pale. Between them.

*The Fighter holds their hands in their lap after placing it. The stone is the correct stone. The placement is correct. The blood from Duchommar's wound reaches the stone's edge and is stopped there.*

The vale is quiet. The brook runs. The stone is between them.

*The Fighter thinks: this is what she asked for. This is all she asked for. I have done it exactly.*

---

**ACT V — The Road from the Vale**
*Morna's stone: placed, remaining, belonging to the ground. Location: the vale, departing.*

The Fighter rises. They look at the stone once and then do not look at it again.

The brook continues. The autumn light continues. The vale of Cona is the same vale it was before the Fighter arrived — same trees, same water, same particular quality of Highland air. Two cairns will be built here eventually, by whoever comes next.

The stone will be between them.

*The Fighter walks the road out of the vale and does not look back. The commission was specific. The stone was specific. The execution was exact. That is all a service like this can be — exact — and exactness is its own form of honor.*

The road runs north toward Fingal's hall. The Fighter does not carry the stone anymore. The Fighter does not carry anything that belonged to this place. The place has what it needs now.

*The Fighter thinks: she knew which stone. She had been watching the water a long time.*

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Ghost at Noon*

**THE TOKEN: VINVELA'S CARVED TABLET** — A small bone tablet, palm-sized, carved at the edges with knotwork in the Highland tradition. On one face, scratched in the old letters: *I will be here when you come back. On the hill. At noon. This is the promise.* On the other face: her device, a running deer, completed in two lines. She made it before Shilric left for Fingal's wars. She left it at the stone on the hill where she said she would wait. She died before he came back. Her ghost appeared there at noon on the day he returned — he spoke to her, and she answered, and told him she was not there for long, and she was not afraid. He picked up the tablet after she was gone. He has held it for three months and cannot put it down.

**ACT I — "Three Months"**
*Node: CNA — Vale of Cona.*

Shilric is sitting at the base of the hill stone. He has been here, on and off, for three months. He is not mad. He is a man who spoke to a ghost at noon and does not know what to do with the fact that the promise was kept by someone who was already dead when she kept it.

He holds the tablet out without looking up.

> **Insight DC 11.** He is not asking the Fighter to validate his experience or explain it or comfort him. He is asking the Fighter to carry the proof somewhere that it can be documented — not so that anyone will believe it, but so that the promise exists in a record somewhere beyond his own memory. Understanding this distinction is the act of taking the tablet correctly.

**Fail:** You reach for the tablet and say something. He pulls it back. You wait. He offers it again. You take it without speaking.

**Pass:** You understand before you reach for it. You take it in silence. He nods once.

TOKEN GRANTED: Vinvela's Carved Tablet.

**ACT V — "The Promise Category"**

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *Crimora's Arrow*

**THE TOKEN: CRIMORA'S RESTRICTION ORDER** — A small folded parchment from the Highland clan elders: Crimora of the clan of Connal's father is forbidden to enter the battlefield at Lena before the engagement closes, on grounds that her grief for Connal could cause her to loose an arrow at a figure she cannot identify clearly in the fighting. The elders wrote this order not from cruelty but from knowledge — Crimora is a skilled archer, and skilled archers loose when they see their target, and in a battle where Connal fights Dargo both men are in the field and Crimora does not know what Dargo looks like in his war-kit. The order is correct. The problem is it arrived too late for Crimora to hear it calmly.

**ACT I — "The Field Edge"**
*Node: HLD — Scottish Highland.*

Crimora has her bow. She is at the field's edge in the grey early morning. She is not mad or reckless; she is prepared. She has been watching the field from the hill and she knows where Connal is — she thinks. She is wrong: the figure she has been watching is Dargo.

> **Insight DC 12.** She does not know she has the wrong figure. She is watching with complete precision. The problem is not her aim; it is that she has been watching the field for an hour in poor light and the two figures have moved, and the one she has been tracking since they were at distance has changed position with the one she did not recognize. Before giving her the restriction order, the Fighter must understand that the order alone will not work — she will resist it because she believes she knows what she is doing. The only approach that can reach her is acknowledging her precision and then asking her to hold it for five more minutes while the light improves.

**Fail:** You give her the order directly. She reads it and stands still. "I know where he is." She does not lower the bow.

**Pass:** You acknowledge the precision first. She pauses. The five minutes create the light. She sees the switch.

TOKEN GRANTED: Crimora's Restriction Order.

## Cycle 8 — Angle: *The Disputed Fragment — Authenticity That Cannot Be Settled*

*All prior cycles reviewed. Source knowledge used directly.*

**What prior cycles missed:** The Ossian text itself is disputed — Macpherson claimed to have translated ancient Gaelic originals; critics (including Samuel Johnson) argued he wrote them himself. The controversy has never been finally resolved. The "original" manuscript fragments Macpherson showed to supporters exist in various forms. A fragment presented as the source of one of the poems — old vellum, Gaelic script, faded — survives in a collector's hands. It may be genuine. It may not. The archive cannot authenticate it and cannot dismiss it.

**This cycle introduces:** *The contested original — a document presented as genuine whose authenticity cannot be finally established, making the contest itself its primary archival characteristic.*
