## Pass 1 — Full Telling

The Three Musketeers was published in 1844, serialized in a Parisian newspaper, and it has not stopped running since. Alexandre Dumas and his collaborator Auguste Maquet wrote it as entertainment in the most honest sense: a book you could not stop reading, each installment ending at exactly the moment you needed to know what happened next. The setting is 1625 France — Cardinal Richelieu versus the King's Musketeers, Paris and London and the road between them, love affairs at the level of state politics. The thing that makes the novel extraordinary is not its plot, which is a series of magnificently engineered complications, but its friendships. The plot is the engine. The friendships are the destination.

**The World and Its Stakes**

Louis XIII is King of France. His chief minister is Cardinal Richelieu — historically accurate, and the novel does not make him purely villainous; he is intelligent, politically necessary, dangerous. Queen Anne of Austria is the French queen who is in some form of love with the Duke of Buckingham, the English favorite. This is the political scandal the Cardinal exploits: if he can prove the Queen has been indiscreet with a foreign enemy of France, he destroys her standing, weakens the King's trust, and consolidates his own position.

The Musketeers are the King's elite guard. Athos, Porthos, and Aramis are the three; D'Artagnan is the Gascon provincial who arrives in Paris with nothing but his horse, his sword, and his father's advice, manages to insult all three Musketeers in one afternoon through a comedy of errors, is challenged to three separate duels, and then when Richelieu's guards arrive to break up the illegal dueling, the four men fight side by side and become inseparable.

This is Dumas's founding premise: the friendship was not chosen; it was made by necessity and confirmed by blood and stays because it cannot be otherwise. No one decides to love Athos and Porthos and Aramis. D'Artagnan simply discovers that he does, in the middle of a sword fight, while outnumbered.

**The Diamond Studs**

Queen Anne was given twelve diamond studs by King Louis XIII as a personal gift. She gave them secretly to the Duke of Buckingham during a private meeting at a garden — a political and romantic gesture whose consequences she could not entirely predict. Cardinal Richelieu learned of the gift through his agent Milady de Winter. He arranged for Milady to steal two of the studs during a court ceremony in England.

His plan: persuade the King to organize a ball and request that the Queen wear the diamond studs. She cannot refuse without explanation. When she appears with ten studs instead of twelve, the King will know she gave them away. The scandal will be public, actionable, and complete.

The Queen, in desperation, sends for D'Artagnan through her lady-in-waiting Constance Bonacieux (who is also D'Artagnan's love). He must go to London, reach Buckingham, get the studs (or replacements for the stolen ones), and return before the ball. Buckingham has the studs; he also has a goldsmith on hand who can make replicas.

**The Race**

This is the novel's central narrative machine: the race against a deadline through a series of obstacles that do not need to defeat the carrier — they only need to delay him.

The four ride together from Paris. Athos is shot and wounded at Chantilly. He tells D'Artagnan to leave him. D'Artagnan leaves him. Porthos is forced off the road by a fight at Crevecœur. He tells D'Artagnan to leave him. D'Artagnan leaves him. Aramis takes a musket ball at Amiens. He tells D'Artagnan to leave him. D'Artagnan leaves him. D'Artagnan arrives in Calais alone, with his servant Planchet.

At Calais, Richelieu has ordered that no one leave for England without specific authorization. D'Artagnan has no authorization. He engages the officer who has authorization in a duel, wounds him, takes his authorization, crosses to Dover.

He reaches Buckingham. Buckingham is delighted — he is in love with the Queen and the diamond studs are the proof of her love. He checks the studs: two are missing. His goldsmith makes replacements in twenty-four hours. D'Artagnan carries all twelve studs back across the Channel, back to Paris, hands them to Constance, who delivers them to the Queen, who wears all twelve to the ball. The King sees twelve studs. Richelieu says nothing. He presents the Queen with two more diamond studs as a "gift" — making fourteen — because he knows and she knows and everyone knows, and nothing can be proven.

**Milady de Winter**

The novel's great creation. She is Richelieu's agent, a spy with almost supernatural ability to read people and make them love her. She has a fleur-de-lis brand on her left shoulder — the mark of a criminal, specifically a convicted thief. She has multiple identities: Lady de Winter, Charlotte de Winter, other names. She is also Athos's wife.

Athos, before the novel's events, was the Comte de la Fère. He married a beautiful young noblewoman, deeply in love. He discovered the brand on their wedding night. In the assumption of the time — a branded woman was an escaped criminal, therefore not a noblewoman, therefore the marriage was obtained by fraud — he hanged her from a tree. She survived. She became Milady. He became Athos, erasing his identity, becoming a musketeer without a past.

This backstory governs the second half of the novel. Milady appears, manipulates, works, and is eventually recognized. When the Musketeers finally capture her on the banks of the river, Athos presides over a formal judgment. He presents himself as the witness to her first crime; others present the evidence of subsequent crimes (the poisoning of Constance Bonacieux; the assassination attempt on Buckingham). The Executioner of Lille — whose wife Milady had seduced and driven to suicide in a convent — appears and identifies himself as the original brandings officer. He beheads her.

The scene is simultaneously the novel's moral climax and its most troubling moment. The men conducting this trial are outside the law; the woman they condemn has committed genuine horrors; the trial itself is a kangaroo court. Dumas does not resolve this tension — he lets it stand.

**Constance Bonacieux**

D'Artagnan's love. She is the Queen's lady-in-waiting, married to the landlord Bonacieux (who is a Richelieu informer without her knowledge), brave, clever, completely loyal to the Queen. She is kidnapped by Richelieu's agents. She is recovered. She is placed in a convent for safety. She is poisoned by Milady, who poisons the convent's wine. She dies in D'Artagnan's arms after a few minutes of recognition. D'Artagnan, who has been racing back with Athos to save her, arrives thirty minutes too late.

The novel does not soften this. It is Dumas's declaration that the machine of adventure has costs: the woman who loves D'Artagnan pays the price for his adventures, for Milady's grudge, for all of it. He holds her as she dies and cannot do anything else.

**The Musketeers' Friendship**

The novel's thesis is simply that four men can love each other completely, without embarrassment, and that this love is among the most reliable things in the world. Athos is the ideal — he is braver, more noble, more ruined, and more loyal than is quite plausible, and the novel knows this and leans into it. Porthos is enormous fun and is ultimately reliable. Aramis is brilliant, evasive, and complicated in ways the novel hints at but does not resolve (he gets his own sequels: Twenty Years After, The Vicomte de Bragelonne). D'Artagnan is the point-of-view: the one who is not quite as good as the others yet, who is learning, who is all emotion and speed and honest love.

All for one, one for all.

### Chapter Notes (Major Movements)

**Chs 1–4: D'Artagnan Arrives** — The young Gascon reaches Paris, insults all three musketeers, is challenged to three duels, the duels merge into a brawl with Richelieu's guards, the friendship is born. Narrative function: establish the protagonist and the central friendship in one kinetic sequence.

**Chs 5–12: The Bonacieux Affair** — Constance Bonacieux is introduced; her husband is a Richelieu informer; Constance serves the Queen; D'Artagnan falls in love; the diamond studs situation begins to take shape. Narrative function: introduce the political stakes and the personal ones simultaneously.

**Chs 13–21: The Race to London** — The four ride; three are disabled one by one; D'Artagnan reaches London alone; Buckingham produces the replacement studs; the return. Narrative function: the novel's central action set-piece, demonstrating both the friendship's cost and D'Artagnan's individual capability.

**Chs 22–29: The Ball and Its Aftermath** — The ball; the Queen wears twelve studs; the Cardinal's maneuver fails; the political situation stabilizes temporarily. The Cardinal offers D'Artagnan a commission in the Musketeers as acknowledgment of his quality — the villain who recognizes the hero's worth. Narrative function: resolve the diamond studs plot; establish D'Artagnan's standing.

**Chs 30–45: Milady and the Siege of La Rochelle** — The historical siege; D'Artagnan encounters Milady; she tries to have him killed, fails; he learns pieces of her history; she is sent to England to assassinate Buckingham. The Milady plot thickens. Narrative function: shift the novel from political comedy to something darker.

**Chs 46–57: The Poisoning of Constance** — Milady reaches England, manipulates a Puritan gentleman, writes to Lord de Winter (her brother-in-law) about the assassination, is imprisoned; she escapes by manipulating her guard. She returns to France and goes to the convent where Constance is hiding. She poisons Constance's wine. Narrative function: close the friendship plot violently; establish the final stakes.

**Chs 58–67: The Pursuit and Trial** — The Musketeers and Lord de Winter pursue Milady; they arrive too late to save Constance but in time to capture Milady; the riverbank trial; the execution. Athos presides. The Executioner of Lille arrives. She is beheaded. Narrative function: resolve the Milady arc with a moral ambivalence the novel acknowledges but does not resolve.

**Chs 68–Epilogue: The Aftermath** — D'Artagnan is offered a position by the Cardinal; he accepts it. The four friends go their separate ways temporarily. The tone is elegiac: the adventure is over, something has been lost with Constance's death, the world goes on. Narrative function: close the novel while suggesting that the friendship, not the adventure, is what will last.

## Pass 2 — Characters, Choices, Tone

**D'Artagnan** — Speed, love, and impulsive brilliance in a nineteen-year-old body. He is not yet as good as the others — his swordsmanship is excellent but not Athos's, his wisdom is zero but growing. What he has is the capacity for absolute loyalty and the energy to act on it immediately. His love for Constance is not a subplot; it is the personal stake that makes the political adventure matter.

**Athos** — The novel's ghost. He is the most capable, most noble, most ruined of the four. His backstory (the marriage, the branding, the hanging) is revealed in stages and it explains everything: his drinking, his stoicism, his cold efficiency in a crisis, his complete unwillingness to discuss his past. He is the novel's elegiac register — the man who has already paid the highest price and is still here, still loyal, still exact. His presiding over Milady's trial is the most morally complex thing he does: he is judge, husband, and victim simultaneously, and he performs the execution with perfect neutrality.

**Porthos** — Comedy and heart. His pretensions are transparent and entirely forgiven. He borrows money from Madame Coquenard (his married mistress) under the cover of elaborate fictions, spends it on clothes, and is still completely reliable in a fight. The novel loves him for his uncomplicated appetites and his uncomplicated loyalty. He is not subtle. He does not need to be.

**Aramis** — The complicated one. He is always on the verge of taking religious orders and never quite does; he is always quoting theology and then flawlessly killing someone. His actual motivation and allegiances are deliberately kept murky. In the sequels he turns out to be the most politically dangerous of the four. The Three Musketeers gives you enough to see the complexity without resolving it.

**Milady** — The novel's great dark achievement. She is a villain whose crimes are real and numerous and whose intelligence and capability are presented without condescension. She manipulates men not because men are stupid but because she is a master of her instrument — she understands desire and uses it with precision. Her backstory (Athos's wife, branded, hanged, survived) gives her a specific grievance, but the novel is clear that the grievance does not excuse what she does. She is the most fully realized character in the book and she dies on a riverbank at night, which is wrong in several ways simultaneously.

**Constance Bonacieux** — The novel's conscience. She is braver than her situation allows, more capable than her marriage permits, and she dies because she trusted the wrong cup of wine in a place where she was hidden for safety. The novel does not forgive the world for this.

**Tone of the world** — High, fast, warm, precise. Dumas knows exactly what he is doing: he is manufacturing pleasure of the highest-grade kind, which requires absolute technical control. The action scenes are choreographed with the specificity of stage directions; the emotional scenes land because the action scenes have earned them. The world is unfair (Constance dies; the trial is extrajudicial; the Cardinal, who is smarter than almost everyone, is the antagonist by virtue of which side he is on) but it is not hopeless. The four friends make it livable.

## Pass 3 — D'Artagnan Leaves Aramis in the Rain

He had left Athos at Chantilly bleeding from a musket ball. He had left Porthos at Crevecœur with his horse shot under him. He had left Aramis at Amiens with a wound in his shoulder that was not mortal but was not nothing.

He is alone now, except for Planchet, his servant. The road to Calais is straight and long and he has been riding for two days without stopping except to change horses. He knows what the deadline is. He does not know if he can make it.

The thing about leaving his friends is that they told him to. Athos gripped his arm and said: go. Porthos looked up from the ditch and said: I'll be fine, go. Aramis, leaning against the side of the inn with his hand pressed to his shoulder, said: D'Artagnan, for the love of God, go. Each one spoke to him the same way — not as an order but as a gift, the gift of being released from the obligation to stay.

He went.

This is the thing about the friendship that the adventure stories usually leave out: loyalty sometimes means going rather than staying. It means trusting that the people you love can manage what is happening to them, even when you can see that it is bad. He trusted each of them — correctly, as it turned out; all three survived — and rode on alone.

At Calais the harbor officer had Richelieu's orders. No one leaves for England without written authorization. D'Artagnan looked at the officer and assessed the situation with the cold speed he was developing that year, which was the year of becoming the man he would be for the rest of his life. He said: sir, I challenge you to a duel for your authorization papers. 

It took eleven minutes. He left the officer alive and slightly diminished and took the papers.

He crossed to England in weather that made the Channel seem personal. He reached Buckingham's residence in London and was shown in by a man who recognized the Queen's seal on the letter D'Artagnan carried. He stood in the Duke's study and watched Buckingham count the diamond studs. Two were gone. The Duke called for his goldsmith.

Twenty-four hours later D'Artagnan stood at the quay at Dover with twelve diamond studs in a velvet case and the tide working in his favor and the knowledge that he had done the impossible thing in the available time, which was the only kind of thing worth doing.

He was nineteen years old. He had left three friends bleeding on the road from Paris, and they had all told him to go, and he had gone, and he was going back now with what the Queen needed, and none of it felt like heroism. It felt like what you do when the people you love are counting on you.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **Before the Ball** — D'Artagnan returns from London with all twelve diamond studs in a velvet case; the road to Paris is full of Richelieu's agents who only need to delay him by two hours for the ball to begin without the studs; the Fighter is his escort for the last thirty miles.

2. **The Convent Letter** — Constance Bonacieux, hidden in the convent at Bethune, sends an urgent letter to D'Artagnan warning him that Milady has found her; the Fighter must carry it from the convent to the nearest relay point before Milady arrives and poisons the wine — the only chance to avert her death.

3. **The Cardinal's Sealed Order** — Richelieu sends a sealed order to his agents in London authorizing Milady's assassination of Buckingham; the Fighter must intercept the courier carrying the order between Paris and Calais — not to stop the Cardinal but because Lord de Winter, Buckingham's ally, needs the order as evidence of the conspiracy.

4. **The Goldsmith's Receipt** — Buckingham's goldsmith has a receipt showing he made two additional diamond studs in 1625; this receipt proves the Queen's studs were replaced and that the Cardinal knew; it is the evidence that could finally expose Richelieu's manipulation; the Fighter carries it from London to Richelieu's political enemy, the Duke of Beaufort.

5. **Athos's Past** — A document exists in the Comte de la Fère's abandoned estate confirming that Milady was legally hanged for her crimes; the hanged woman being formally dead means her subsequent crimes cannot be prosecuted as the same person's — unless this document survives to establish legal continuity; the Fighter carries it to Paris before the Cardinal's agents who want it destroyed.

6. **Planchet's Loyalty** — D'Artagnan's servant Planchet, riding alone after being separated from his master, carries the Queen's seal (the document that opened Buckingham's door) through a Richelieu checkpoint; the seal must reach D'Artagnan's hands in London before the Buckingham meeting or the meeting fails.

7. **The Musketeers' Billet** — The Musketeers are ordered to the Siege of La Rochelle, officially as soldiers; actually, Athos has information the Cardinal needs suppressed; the Fighter carries Athos's sealed report to the Musketeer captain before the Cardinal's agents can intercept — the report names names and the names include people still alive and in power.

## 5-Act Vignette Play

**Theme Check**

Existing themes to avoid (24 entries, LHR through OST):
- Loyalty under the long watch (LHR, STN, TBS)
- Grief carried in an object (INV, BHD, GDN)
- Honor vs. shame (MAD, GDN, BHD)
- Love and fate intertwined (MSE, INV, BHD)
- Truth delivered posthumously (LGW)
- Healing carried through hostile law (MAN)
- The carrier becomes the hunted (SEN)
- The token's journey (ALL)
- Ancient witnesses and the relay of questions (KIR)
- Corruption wears the face of mercy; the genuine article has no price (FCO)
- Fate moves through the broken thing (FRO)
- The greatest man alive has no house (VBY)
- The invitation as the weapon (LBC)
- The poem that ransoms a life (RIX)
- The one who will not weep (BOO)
- The tool of reassembly (ALF)
- What the martyr commands from death the living cannot refuse (KSU)
- The prophecy that must be spoken though it changes nothing (RKV)
- Reparation requires the original fire (HFT)
- Pride that cannot confess returns the stolen thing anonymously (MOL)
- The man fate wrote for betrayal is still the man who betrayed (CPH)
- The institution silences its victim so the diplomat can speak for him (ARN)
- The relic embodies both the glory and the error (OST)

**Avoiding**: "Loyalty under the long watch" (LHR, STN, TBS) — the Musketeers' loyalty is real but the quest theme is not about sustained patience; it is about speed and the temporal structure of opposition. "The carrier becomes the hunted" (SEN) — similar surface but SEN is about possession of a valuable object marking the bearer as prey; this is about a timed race where the obstacles are designed to create delay, not destruction.

**Chosen Quest: Storyline 1 — "Before the Ball"**

**New Theme**: *The obstacle doesn't need to win — it only needs to cause delay: the enemy's strategy is temporal, and the carrier races not against an army but against the clock the enemy controls.*

This is structurally distinct from all 24 existing themes. It names the specific mechanic of this kind of adversity: the opposition is designed for delay, not destruction, and surviving every encounter is still a loss if the encounters collectively consume the available time.

### The Vignette: "Before the Ball"

**NODE ROUTE**: LON (London — Buckingham's residence) → CDV (Calais harbor) → TL (Tilbury — the harbor arrival) → FR (Frankish road south) → WM (Weimar — standing in for the royal court)  
**TOKEN**: The Diamond Stud-Case — a velvet-lined box containing all twelve of the Queen's diamond studs, sealed with the Duke of Buckingham's personal wax; two of the studs are replacements made by the Duke's goldsmith in twenty-four hours; it must reach the Queen's hands before the ball begins.

---

**ACT 1 — The Duke's Goodbye**  
*Node: LON (London — the Duke of Buckingham's private study)*

Buckingham gives you the case himself. He has counted the studs twice. He holds the case a moment before giving it — the Queen gave these to him as proof of something he is now returning, because she needs them more than he needs the proof. He hands the case over. He says: tell her I counted every one.

*Skill Check: Insight (WIS) DC 12*

**Pass**: You read the weight of the gesture — he is not being asked to give back the studs but to give back the version of himself that held them. He is doing it anyway. You take the case and understand what you are carrying.  
**Fail**: You take the case and understand only that you are carrying twelve diamond studs in a velvet box and that this is urgent. The rest you will learn on the road.

*Grant item: The Diamond Stud-Case (velvet-lined, sealed with Buckingham's wax; the seal marks you as a carrier with the Duke's authorization)*  
*Activate mission bit: CDG_questActive*

---

**ACT 2 — The Port at Calais**  
*Node: CDV (Calais harbor)*

The harbor is locked. Richelieu's order: no vessel departs for France without written authorization from the Cardinal's office. The officer at the quay is polite, firm, and entirely aware that every hour he holds you here is an hour closer to the ball's opening. He has been told to hold anyone carrying sealed correspondence from London. He has a smile that says: I don't need to fight you. I just need to keep you here until this afternoon.

*Skill Check: Persuasion (CHA) DC 13*

**Pass**: You find the departure authorization in his command post — the document he uses to clear legitimate vessels — and argue that your papers are in order under a different clause of the Cardinal's own standing order, which permits emergency royal correspondence. He does not entirely believe you; he believes you just enough to avoid the institutional risk of being wrong. He lets you through.  
**Fail**: He will not be moved by argument. You wait three hours for a merchant vessel whose captain has a private grievance against the Cardinal's harbor authority and is willing to depart on a technicality. Three hours lost.

*Grant item: A harbor departure stamp (the quay officer's mark on a travel strip, extracted in the course of persuasion or obtained separately; proves legal departure from Calais)*

---

**ACT 3 — Tilbury Landing**  
*Node: TL (Tilbury — the harbor)*

Richelieu's agent at Tilbury is not the subtle kind. He has four men and a description of what the carrier from London looks like. He has been waiting since yesterday. He moves when you step off the boat.

*Combat: Richelieu's harbor agents (×4)*  
**AC**: 13 | **HP**: 16 each | Professional operatives: two swordsmen, one grappler, one man stationed at the road to close it if you run. They work as a unit. They are trying to take the case, not necessarily kill you — a dead carrier creates paperwork; a robbed carrier is simply an incident.

*Grant item: A Cardinal's agent's letter of instruction (found on the leader; signed with a code-name, not the Cardinal's seal, but the code-name is known — it opens other doors later)*

---

**ACT 4 — The Road South**  
*Node: FR (the road from Tilbury toward the court city)*

The relay station at the fifteen-mile post has no horses. The stablemaster apologizes elaborately: the horses were all purchased this morning by a party of travelers, very legitimate, nothing irregular, he has the receipts. The elaborateness of his apology tells you everything. You are two hours from the court, the ball begins in three hours, and you have no horse.

*Skill Check: Athletics (STR) DC 13 OR Persuasion (CHA) DC 14*

**Athletics Pass**: You run the last two miles to the next village yourself, flag down a traveling merchant's empty cart horse, and make up the time.  
**Persuasion Pass**: You show the Cardinal's agent's letter (from Act 3) to the stablemaster. The code-name on it means he was already paid once today to cooperate with your opposition. You offer him the choice between his employer knowing he cooperated with both sides, or his employer thinking he cooperated with only one. He finds a horse quickly.  
**Fail**: You lose forty-five minutes sourcing a horse from a farmstead a quarter-mile off the road. You arrive at the court just as the first guests are entering. You have minutes, not hours.

*Grant item: A clay road token (the merchant's cart-marker — a numbered clay disc that permits use of the royal road without toll challenge; useful for the final approach)*

---

**ACT 5 — The Court Entrance**  
*Node: WM (Weimar — standing as the royal court, the night of the ball)*

The court entrance is bright and loud and full of people who are all going to the same place at the same time. Constance is inside somewhere, near the Queen's antechamber. You need to get to her before the King requests that the Queen wear the studs. There is no one to fight here — only the crowd, the protocol, and the narrowing window.

*Skill Check: Persuasion (CHA) DC 15*

**Pass**: You find a palace servant who recognizes Buckingham's seal on the case (he was at the court when the Duke visited; he knows what the seal means) and gets you through to the Queen's antechamber in four minutes. Constance is there. She takes the case. She does not say thank you — there is not time. She disappears through the inner door.  
**Fail**: You get to the antechamber door but a guard has his instructions and will not pass you without the right recognition phrase. You send a message in with a servant; it reaches Constance; she comes out to collect the case herself. Five additional minutes, which are almost not available. She gets the case and runs.

*Take item: The Diamond Stud-Case*  
*Grant item: Constance Bonacieux's ribbon — a small piece of blue ribbon she pulls from her hair and presses into your hand as she takes the case; not payment, simply the gesture of someone who has been afraid for three days and is now, briefly, not afraid*  
*Activate mission bit: CDG_questComplete*
