## Pass 1 — Full Telling

La Chanson de Roland is a poem that knows exactly what it is: a text written for people who are about to go on crusade, by someone who wants them to know what dying for the faith should look like. It was composed around 1100, in the immediate aftermath of the First Crusade — which had just delivered Jerusalem to Christian hands in 1099. Its subject is technically Charlemagne's Iberian campaign of 778, but the transformation from history to myth is complete: the Basques who actually ambushed the Frankish rearguard have become Saracens, the tactical failure has become a martyrdom, and Roland's death has been made into a template for how a Christian warrior should die.

The historical Roland was probably an officer of the Breton March. The historical Battle of Roncevaux was a minor ambush in the Pyrenean pass, obscure enough that Einhard — Charlemagne's biographer — barely mentions it. The poem takes this footnote and turns it into the founding myth of French chivalry. It is not interested in historical accuracy; it is interested in what it means to be a knight, and what it costs.

What it costs is Roland's life, Oliver's life, Archbishop Turpin's life, and twenty thousand Frankish soldiers who died because Roland refused to blow the horn.

**The Setup: Ganelon's Treachery**

Charlemagne has been fighting in Spain for seven years. He has reduced every Saracen stronghold except Saragossa, held by King Marsile. Marsile sends a peace embassy offering tribute and nominal conversion; Charlemagne's council debates whether to accept. Roland argues for continuing the war. Ganelon — Roland's stepfather, a great lord in his own right — argues for accepting. Roland nominates Ganelon as the dangerous envoy to Marsile; the previous ambassadors were killed.

Ganelon is furious at the nomination. He goes to Marsile's court seething with resentment. He makes his deal: the Franks will leave Spain; Roland will command the rearguard at Roncevaux; Marsile will attack with his full force. The trap is simple. The cause is private: Ganelon wants Roland dead.

The poem gives Ganelon a full moral presence — he is not a mustache-twirling villain but a man whose wounded pride has curdled into something that requires twenty thousand dead to satisfy. The poem does not excuse this, but it understands how it happens.

**Roncevaux: The Decision**

The rearguard at Roncevaux Pass. Roland commands it; Oliver is with him; Archbishop Turpin is with them; twelve Peers of France are with them. Twenty thousand Franks. Charlemagne's main army has already passed north.

Oliver climbs a high rock and sees the Saracen army coming: hundreds of thousands. He comes down and tells Roland: blow the horn. Roland refuses. It would be shameful — a knight does not call for help against any odds. Oliver pleads again. Roland refuses again. They will fight.

They fight magnificently. The paladins are extraordinary — each kills hundreds. But they are twenty thousand against hundreds of thousands, and the mathematics are not wrong. They are dying.

Oliver confronts Roland again: "You could have blown the horn. You didn't. Now it is too late." He says this clearly, with grief and anger mixed, in a way that the poem treats as completely accurate. Roland is not wrong that his decision expressed honor. He is also not wrong that his decision killed twenty thousand men. The poem holds both of these things together and does not resolve them.

Finally, Roland blows the olifant.

He blows it three times. The sound carries thirty miles to Charlemagne's ears. Charlemagne weeps at the sound of it and says to his council: "Roland is in danger." He turns the army around.

The price of blowing too late: the force of the blow ruptures Roland's temples. His brain leaks from his ears. He is dying as he blows. When he stops, the horn has a crack in it.

The poem renders this detail precisely. It is the physical record of when the decision was finally made. The crack in the ivory is the error's mark.

**The Deaths**

Oliver, already wounded, is struck blind by blood loss. Fighting blind, he swings and accidentally strikes Roland. The blow is enormous — it would have killed anyone else. Roland asks: "Did you mean to do this, my friend?" Oliver says he cannot see; he doesn't know who he struck; forgive him. Roland forgives him immediately, completely. They embrace. Oliver dies.

Archbishop Turpin gives last rites to the dying — he himself is dying, four lance-wounds through the body, but he moves among the fallen and blesses them. Eventually he falls.

Roland is the last man standing. He tries to break his sword Durendal on a dark rock — he strikes three times, but the blade is indestructible; only the rock cracks. He speaks to the sword, naming all the relics sealed inside its golden hilt: a tooth of Saint Peter, the blood of Saint Basil, a hair of the Virgin, cloth from Saint Denis's robe. He says: you should not be in pagan hands. He hides Durendal and the olifant under his own body.

He arranges himself facing Spain — the direction of the enemy, which is also the direction of the fight. He raises his glove to God. The angel Gabriel takes it. He dies.

Charlemagne arrives. He finds the bodies. He weeps in a way the poem describes with full detail — he faints, his barons hold him up, he cannot speak for a long time. He has Roland's body prepared. He gives orders for burial with full honor for all twenty thousand.

He pursues the Saracen army to the Ebro River, which they try to cross; most drown. He takes Marsile's queen, Bramimonde, back to France — she will be baptized not by force but by love of the faith, the poem says, demonstrating Christianity's claimed superiority to forced conversion.

**The Trial of Ganelon**

At Aix-la-Chapelle, Ganelon is tried. He argues his case: he publicly declared enmity toward Roland before the army set out; this is not treason, which is secret betrayal of the emperor — this is personal feud within the proper forms. His argument is technically elegant. Thirty of his kinsmen swear to its truth and back it with combat.

Charlemagne's champion Thierry fights Ganelon's champion Pinabel — the greatest warrior alive. Thierry is smaller, less experienced, and loses the technical exchange. But at the moment Thierry is about to be killed, God intervenes (the poem is not subtle about this): Thierry's blow lands, Pinabel falls dead. Divine judgment has spoken. Ganelon is condemned. His thirty kinsmen are hung. Ganelon is torn apart by four horses.

**Aude**

At the very end — four stanzas, brief and devastating. Aude, Roland's betrothed, comes to Charlemagne. She asks for Roland. Charlemagne tells her Roland is dead. He offers his own son Louis in marriage instead. Aude says: this means nothing to me. She falls dead immediately. The poem does not explain what she died of. She simply stopped when the news arrived. She is buried with honor.

**Gabriel's Command**

The night after the trial, while Charlemagne sleeps, the angel Gabriel appears in a dream and commands him to march to relieve the city of Imphe, besieged by a new king who wants him at war. Charlemagne is old. He has been fighting for years. He does not want another campaign. He says — in the poem's final, most human line: "Dieu, si penuse est ma vie!" God, how hard is my life.

Then he rises and buckles on his armor.

The poem ends there. It is a poem about duty as something that does not stop, even for grief, even for exhaustion, even for the specific wish to be left alone for a while. The answer to Roland's death is: the war continues. The answer to Aude's death is: the war continues. The answer to everything is: buckle on your armor and go.

### Chapter Notes (By Major Laisses Groups)

**Laisses 1–14: The Embassy Debate** — Marsile's peace offer, the council debate, Ganelon's nomination, his departure. Establishes the political mechanism of the poem and the private resentment that drives it. Ganelon's treachery has a human cause; the poem explains the cause without excusing it.

**Laisses 15–27: Ganelon and Marsile** — The deal made; the trap set; Ganelon returns to the Frankish army with Marsile's promised gifts. Roland cannot read the betrayal in his stepfather's face. The poem maintains the dramatic irony throughout: the reader knows what Ganelon did; Roland does not.

**Laisses 28–78: The Battle Begins** — The rearguard takes its position; Oliver's warning; the horn debate; the first wave of the battle. The paladins' individual combats, each with its own vivid texture. The poem is not abstract about violence — it describes exactly how each man dies and who killed him.

**Laisses 79–130: The Horn** — Oliver's final plea; Roland's refusal; the battle turning; Roland finally blowing; the rupture; Charlemagne hearing and turning back. The poem's moral center. Oliver's accusation and Roland's silence are the most honest exchange in the poem.

**Laisses 131–176: The Deaths** — The paladins dying one by one; Oliver's accidental blow and Roland's forgiveness; Archbishop Turpin's last rites; the twelve Peers all dead; Roland's final acts (Durendal, the olifant hidden under his body, the arrangement facing Spain, the glove raised to God).

**Laisses 177–213: Charlemagne Arrives; the Pursuit** — The grief at the pass; the pursuit to the Ebro; the supernatural darkness God sends to hold the light while the Franks chase the fleeing army; Marsile's defeat; Bramimonde taken.

**Laisses 214–268: The Trial and Execution of Ganelon** — The trial; Ganelon's defense; the judicial combat; God's verdict; the execution. Aude's appearance and death. The poem resolves the treachery thread completely.

**Laisses 269–291: Gabriel's Command** — The dream; the new campaign commanded; Charlemagne's exhaustion and his obedience. The poem's coda: duty that does not end.

## Pass 2 — Characters, Choices, Tone

**Roland** — The poem's problem and its glory simultaneously. He is brave in a way that is not performance — it is genuinely who he is. He is also proud in a way that kills twenty thousand men. The poem does not choose between these facts; it holds them together. The theological reading is that Roland died in a state of grace because he repented and offered his glove to God; the practical reading is that he made a catastrophic decision and people died for it. Both readings are in the text. Both are true.

**Oliver** — The voice of prudence in a poem that loves courage. His "blow the horn" plea is completely correct; Roland's refusal is completely Roland. The poem respects Oliver's judgment without suggesting Roland should have been someone else. The accidental blow — Oliver dying, striking his friend blind, being forgiven in the same moment — is the poem's most technically precise scene. The friendship absorbs the blow and the blow does not change the friendship; this is one of the things the poem is about.

**Archbishop Turpin** — The fighting priest who gives last rites to his companions while also dying of four wounds. The poem offers him as the synthesis that the medieval church wanted to project: the man who can wield a sword and administer sacraments with equal facility. He is also simply courageous. Whatever the institutional meaning, the poem's Turpin is a man who keeps working past the point where a sane assessment of his own condition would tell him to stop.

**Ganelon** — The most complicated character in a poem that is not primarily interested in complication. His argument at the trial — that he declared personal enmity publicly, that this is not treason — is not insane; it is technically within the codes of the time. The poem still kills him for it, by divine judgment. The suggestion is that the private codes of honor (I am allowed to destroy my personal enemy within the proper forms) fail when applied at the scale of an entire army's death. Some acts are too large for personal justification.

**Charlemagne** — The emperor who weeps. This is the detail that humanizes the poem's political theology: the great king cannot stop crying at what he has lost. He weeps at the horn's sound. He weeps at the bodies. He faints. His barons hold him up. Then he buries the dead and pursues the enemy. The emotion is real; the duty does not wait for the emotion to finish. The poem's final image of him — old, exhausted, commanded again by Gabriel, saying "God, how hard is my life" — is the most human thing in a poem that otherwise runs on inhuman scale.

**Aude** — She dies in four stanzas and is never forgotten. She is Roland's betrothed; we know almost nothing about her except that when she learns Roland is dead, she stops. The poem gives her dignity: she refuses Charlemagne's offer of his son as consolation; she knows what she is refusing. Then she falls. The abruptness is not callousness — it is the poem saying: this is what love like this looks like. It doesn't negotiate.

**Tone of the world** — The world of Roland is large and clear and morally bright. There is no ambiguity in the Saracens (they are wrong), in the faith (it is right), in the order of battle (clear). The only genuine ambiguity is Roland's decision at the horn, and the poem spends that ambiguity very carefully. The emotional register is high — grief is enormous, pride is enormous, courage is enormous. The poem is not interested in small feelings. It is a monument, and it knows it, and it tries to be worthy of what it is commemorating.

## Pass 3 — The Horn

He had been watching the army die for two hours.

Oliver came down from the high rock the first time and said: blow the horn. Roland said no. They fought for a long time — the paladins against the hundreds of thousands, and the paladins were extraordinary, and the mathematics were wrong. Oliver came back and said again: blow the horn. Roland said no. A knight does not call for help against any odds. His voice was steady. It was completely true and it was killing everyone.

The last time Oliver came to him there was no point in asking. He just said: you could have called them back. Now it is too late. You made this choice. His voice was not angry, exactly — it was past anger, at the place where a man states a fact about someone he loves because there is nothing left to do but state it. Roland heard him. The poem does not say Roland answered.

The moment came. The grounds for refusing were still there — a knight does not call for help, it would be shameful, the code was the code — and the grounds had been the same for two hours while people died for them. Something shifted. Roland put the horn to his lips.

He blew.

The sound went thirty miles. You have to understand what kind of sound that requires. Not volume — something else. The horn's material is ivory (the tusk of an elephant, the poem specifies, and gives the object that name: olifant, elephant), and what Roland made it do was not within the human range. When Charlemagne heard it, thirty miles north, he knew immediately. He said: Roland is in danger. He turned the army around. He wept.

What the poem records, in the same line: Roland's temples burst. The force of the blowing ruptured something in his head. He blew three times. Each time: the army turned south; Roland's brain was moving outward through his ears.

When he stopped, the horn had a crack in it at the bell end.

He looked at it. He could not have told you whether the crack was from the force of the blowing or from the rock when he put it down. He picked up his sword and kept fighting.

The men he could have saved, if he had blown hours earlier, were dead. The men he was saving now, by making Charlemagne turn the army, were Charlemagne's men. The rearguard was already past saving. The horn was for the army. He had blown it when the only thing left it could do was vengeance.

He fought until everyone around him was dead. Then he tried to break his sword and couldn't. Then he arranged himself facing Spain and offered his glove to God.

The crack in the horn remained.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Horn of Roncevaux** — Charlemagne recovers the cracked olifant from Roland's body at the pass; it must be carried to the chapel at Aix-la-Chapelle and installed there as a memorial; Ganelon's surviving kin want it before it reaches the shrine, because a horn displayed in a royal chapel fixes Roland as a martyr and Ganelon's guilt as permanent.

2. **Ganelon's Letter** — Before the battle, Ganelon sent a private letter to a Frankish lord named in the deal, confirming the arrangement; the letter survived the trial but was not presented as evidence; the Fighter carries it from the trial records to the man it implicates, giving him the chance to testify to the full conspiracy.

3. **Bramimonde's Baptism** — Marsile's queen is brought to Aix as Charlemagne's prisoner; the poem says she will be baptized by love not compulsion; someone must carry her correspondence to the church that will receive her — but the letters she wrote contain her own private record of what the Saracen court did and why, which is not the version the official baptism narrative requires.

4. **Aude's Ring** — Roland's betrothed died the moment she learned he was dead; she had sent him a ring weeks earlier that he never received; the ring is still with the messenger who was carrying it when the battle happened; the Fighter must deliver it to Roland's grave-marker at Roncevaux before the grave is formalized without it.

5. **Oliver's Accusation** — A Frankish lord who survived the battle's aftermath (a courier, not a fighter) was close enough to hear Oliver's final accusation of Roland; he has written it down and is unsure whether to present it at court, where Roland's official memorial is being shaped as pure heroism; the Fighter is asked to carry the document one direction or another.

6. **Turpin's Blessing List** — Archbishop Turpin, dying, walked among the fallen and blessed each by name; a monk who followed the archbishop after the battle recorded the names in order; the list is the complete roll of the dead; it must reach the families before the official count (which has political omissions) is sealed.

7. **The Embassy before the Embassy** — The previous two envoys Charlemagne sent to Marsile were killed; their families want the diplomatic records showing the mission was authorized and the deaths were murders, not military casualties; the Fighter carries the authorization documents to the families before the crown can reclassify the deaths as combat losses.

**Avoiding**: "Grief carried in an object" (INV, BHD, GDN) — the olifant carries grief but that is not the theme; the theme is about what the relic establishes as permanent truth. "What the martyr cannot command in life he commands from death" (KSU) — KSU is about a saint's miraculous authority; this is about a morally ambiguous legacy being fixed into memorial form; the horn is not a saint's relic but an object that carries both the courage and the error. "The man fate wrote for betrayal is still the man who betrayed" (CPH) — CPH is about self-testimony versus institutional simplification; this is about the physical object as dual-truth memorial.

**Chosen Quest: Storyline 1 — "The Horn of Roncevaux"**

**New Theme**: *The relic embodies both the glory and the error: the crack in the horn is when it was blown too late, and installing it in the shrine fixes both truths permanently — because a memorial that keeps only the good half is a lie, and one that keeps only the failure is another.*

This is distinct from all 23 existing themes. It addresses the specific problem of memorial-making: what a physical object carries in its damage, and the ethical weight of choosing to display that damage as part of the monument rather than concealing it.

### The Vignette: "The Horn of Roncevaux"

**NODE ROUTE**: RON (Roncevaux Pass — the battlefield) → PYR (Pyrenean road, high pass) → Frankish lowland road → AIX (Aix-la-Chapelle — Charlemagne's chapel)  
**TOKEN**: The Olifant of Roland — an ivory war-horn, cracked at the bell from the force of Roland's final blowing; the crack is the visible mark of when the decision was made; carried by Roland at Roncevaux; its sound reached Charlemagne thirty miles away.

---

**ACT 1 — The Recovery**  
*Node: RON (Roncevaux Pass — the pass-road and the burial ground)*

The bodies are still being counted. Charlemagne stands at the place where Roland's body was found — arranged facing Spain, the glove already taken, the sword and horn recovered. He gives you the olifant himself. He says: take this to the chapel at Aix. It belongs on the altar there. He does not explain why he is not carrying it himself — you understand: he is not done counting the dead, and he will not be done for a long time.

*Skill Check: Insight (WIS) DC 12*

**Pass**: You understand the weight of what you are being given. The crack is visible — it runs along the lower bell of the horn like a line of dried blood. Charlemagne is not giving you a symbol of pure glory. He is giving you the whole object, damage and all. You take it.  
**Fail**: You take the horn without fully reading why Charlemagne does not carry it himself. Later, on the road, you will understand: he cannot carry it yet. He is not finished with the grief.

*Grant item: The Olifant of Roland (ivory horn, cracked at the bell; still smells faintly of blood from the pass; wrapped in the cloth of a dead paladin's cloak)*  
*Activate mission bit: OST_questActive*

---

**ACT 2 — Ganelon's Kin**  
*Node: PYR (Pyrenean high road — the northern pass)*

Three men on the high road. They are Ganelon's kinsmen — two of the thirty who took the oath at the trial, who survived the executions by being absent at the wrong moment. They know where the horn is going. A horn in the royal chapel at Aix is a horn displayed in front of every court gathering, every diplomatic reception, every feast; it is a permanent accusation in ivory. They would prefer it lost on the Pyrenean road.

*Combat: Ganelon's surviving kinsmen (×3)*  
**AC**: 14 | **HP**: 20 each | Frankish knights, fully capable, carrying the specific anger of men who have lost thirty of their family in two days. They are not going to be talked out of this.

*Grant item: A Frankish knight's cloak-brooch (silver, engraved with a family device — taken from the leader of the three; heavy and specific)*

---

**ACT 3 — The Lord's Hall**  
*Node: PYR → lowland road (a Frankish manor at the road's edge)*

Lord Bertrand de Ventoux received his knighthood at Roland's hand fifteen years ago. He heard the horn at Roncevaux from thirty miles away and wept. He wants the olifant. Not to suppress it — he wants to display it in his own hall, where he will describe Roland to every visitor for the rest of his life. He makes the case earnestly: I was there. I heard it. No one will honor it better than I will.

*Skill Check: Persuasion (CHA) DC 14*

**Pass**: You tell him: Charlemagne gave it to me for Aix. It belongs on the altar there, where it will be seen by everyone who comes to the chapel — not just everyone who visits a single lord's hall. His claim is real. The chapel's claim is larger. He lets you go, unhappy and honest about being unhappy.  
**Fail**: He is not satisfied by this argument and his household does not make it easy to leave. You find a way out — a side door, a stable route — before he formally orders you stopped. His honor prevents him from pursuing you once you are clearly on the road; a man of his code cannot rob a royal courier in open view.

*Grant item: Bertrand's road-blessing (a small wax seal with his device, given as grudging acknowledgment of your mission; marks you as a legitimately-traveling royal courier on the Frankish lowland road)*

---

**ACT 4 — The Cleric's Question**  
*Node: AIX approach (Aix-la-Chapelle — the chapel's outer porch)*

The head cleric of the chapel is a serious man. He has been preparing the chapel's reliquary space for the horn for two days. But he has also heard two versions of Roland's death: the martyr's version (Roland fell fighting the enemies of God, unblemished) and the soldiers' version (Roland blew the horn too late and twenty thousand men died for his pride). He wants to know which horn he is receiving. He asks, carefully: what is the crack from?

*Skill Check: Persuasion (CHA) DC 14*

**Pass**: You tell him the truth, completely: the crack is from the blowing. Roland blew three times, too late, and his temples burst. The crack is when he finally decided. The horn carries the courage and the error in the same piece of ivory. The cleric thinks for a long time. He says: then it is the true relic, not a cleaned one. He opens the porch.  
**Fail**: You give him the simpler version first — battle damage, the horn was struck — and he does not quite believe it. He knows what ivory breaks from. He asks again. You tell him the truth. He takes longer to open the porch, but he opens it.

*Grant item: The chapel door-token (a small carved key-charm in wood, hung on the inner door; indicates authorized entry to the reliquary — kept by the cleric, given to you as acknowledgment of the delivery's authenticity)*

---

**ACT 5 — The Altar**  
*Node: AIX (Aix-la-Chapelle — the chapel interior)*

The chapel is small and high and very still. The altar holds the other relics of the campaign — Charlemagne's gifts over decades. The cleric places the olifant in the prepared space. The crack is visible from the nave. He does not turn it so the crack faces inward. He leaves it facing out, toward whoever walks through the door.

*Skill Check: Insight (WIS) DC 12*

**Pass**: You understand the cleric's choice. He is not displaying the failure; he is displaying the whole truth. The crack says: here is when he finally decided. The ivory says: here is how far the sound carried. Neither fact cancels the other. Anyone who comes to this altar gets both, whether they want them both or not.  
**Fail**: You see only the placement. The horn is on the altar. The mission is done. What it means in twenty years when pilgrims touch it is not something you were hired to consider.

*Take item: The Olifant of Roland*  
*Grant item: The chapel's pilgrim token — a small pressed-tin medallion with the Aix chapel mark; given to authenticated donors of relics; it opens gates in Charlemagne's royal circuit and marks the bearer as known to the palace*
