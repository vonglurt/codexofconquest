## Cycle 1 — Angle: The man in the champion's shadow

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

Amadis of Gaul is the great founding romance of the Iberian chivalric tradition — the book that defined what a knight was supposed to want, fear, feel, and do, and which haunted the imagination of the Spanish-speaking world so completely that Cervantes had to parody it in order to clear the air. The story was compiled and expanded across generations; the text we have is Garci Rodríguez de Montalvo's four-book Spanish edition of 1508, which translates and expands a Portuguese or Castilian original probably composed in the early 1300s. Southey's English translation (1803) is faithful, stately, and a little solemn — it reads the way a cathedral sounds.

The plot follows Amadis from before his birth. His father, King Perión of Gaul, and his mother, the Princess Elisena of Brittany, conceive him outside of marriage. Elisena, terrified of shame, puts the infant in a locked wooden chest and sets him adrift on the river. The chest reaches the sea. A Scottish knight finds him. He is raised without knowing his name. His father's sword is in the chest with him; his father's ring is on his finger; he is already a knight without knowing why. He grows up as "the Child of the Sea."

He meets Oriana — daughter of King Lisuarte of Great Britain, the most powerful Christian king in the romance world — when they are both children, and their love is instantaneous and total. It is not a courtly pretense: they are ten years old and they know. Amadis spends the next twenty years proving himself worthy of her, which is to say he spends twenty years being the finest knight alive, performing impossible feats, and remaining secretly devoted to a woman who cannot publicly acknowledge him.

Volume 2 (the Southey translation we have) opens mid-story. Amadis has already been named, his parentage discovered, his place at Lisuarte's court established. He is the best knight alive and everyone knows it. The volume moves through three phases:

**Phase One: The Union and the Court.** Amadis rescues Oriana from the enchanter Arcalaus, who had trapped her in a magical prison. After the rescue, alone in a forest, they consummate their love in a scene the author writes with extraordinary care — it is tender, mutual, and presented as a covenant rather than a conquest. Amadis is reverent; Oriana is willing; the text gives both of them dignity. This is the emotional center of the whole romance, paid for by twenty years of patient devotion and immediately complicated by the world closing back in around them. Back at court: Galaor (Amadis's brother) rescues King Lisuarte from an ambush. Barsinan, Lord of Sansuena, attempts a coup in London while Lisuarte is away; Amadis returns, defeats Barsinan's forces, restores order, and is celebrated. The Madasima subplot involves a delicate public covenant about chivalric courtesy that Amadis discharges through negotiation rather than raw force, establishing that he thinks as well as fights. Agrayes (Amadis's cousin) wins a trial by combat against the Duke, gaining honor.

**Phase Two: Poor Rock.** This is the novel's psychological core. Oriana receives a false report suggesting Amadis has been intimate with another woman. She writes him a letter of dismissal — the cruelest possible letter, thorough in its repudiation. Amadis reads it and is destroyed. Not wounded. Destroyed. He rides to the coast, gives up his armor, takes the hermit name "Beltenebros" (Beautiful Darkness), and retreats to an island hermitage called Poor Rock (Peña Pobre), where an elderly holy man named Andalod keeps a chapel. What follows is a medieval portrait of clinical depression: Amadis barely eats, barely speaks, would die if Gandalin — his squire, who followed him secretly — were not keeping him marginally alive. The court searches; Don Guilan carries Amadis's shield to tournaments in his honor. Corisanda, a noblewoman, comes to Poor Rock and provides female friendship without demand. The turn comes when Durinda, the Damsel of Denmark, arrives after weeks of searching. She recognizes Amadis by a scar on his neck — he has kept his identity hidden — and delivers Oriana's second letter, the retraction, written after Oriana learned the false report's source. Amadis reads it. The life returns. He emerges. He returns to court. The whole arc is about grief as physical dissolution and recovery as something that can only be triggered by the right voice from outside.

**Phase Three: Ardan Canileo and the Aftermath.** Urganda the Unknown — Amadis's patron enchantress, who appears at key moments to frame narrative in prophecy — arrives by ship. She reads the past and future in riddles comprehensible in parts and opaque in others; her arrival signals that something large is coming. The night before the great combat against Ardan Canileo — a massive Moorish knight who has challenged any knight at Lisuarte's court to single combat — Matalesa, a malicious damsel called the "Insolent Damsel" throughout the text, steals Amadis's sword Flamberge while he sleeps. Gandalin, his squire, does not wake. Matalesa rides off with it, motivated by a personal grudge against Oriana. In the morning, Amadis finds himself without his principal weapon on the day of a life-or-death duel. He borrows a sword and enters the combat.

The fight against Ardan is extraordinary. Ardan is enormous, experienced, and fully armed. Amadis bleeds from two shield-notch wounds. The crowd goes silent in the way crowds go silent when they understand they might be watching someone die. Then Amadis's borrowed sword breaks — not at the hilt, cleanly, but sheering at the mid-blade under a counter-block. He throws what remains. He looks at what Ardan is holding. He steps in rather than back, drives his forearm into Ardan's shield to turn it, picks up a broken lance fragment from the ground, uses it as a lever against Ardan's sword arm until the sword rotates in Ardan's grip, and tears it from him. He now has Ardan's own sword. He decapitates him. Don Bruneo simultaneously defeats Madaman (Ardan's second) in a parallel combat. Matalesa, watching from the stands, sees Ardan dead and kills herself. In the aftermath, two counselors — Gandandel and Brocadan — begin privately poisoning Lisuarte's mind against Amadis, suggesting that Amadis's fame is becoming a threat to the king's own authority. The volume ends with the court celebrating and the poison already working.

**What the book argues:** That love is not a reward but a test, and that the test never ends. That the finest knight alive can be destroyed by a single letter from the right person. That the institutions around great men tend to produce the very conditions that corrupt and eventually destroy them — not through malice necessarily, but through the ordinary mechanisms of court politics: the counselor who flatters the insecure king, the damsel who carries a grudge too long, the record-keeper who writes what the room wants rather than what happened. The book is sentimental but not naive. It knows that Amadis's world will not hold. Books 3 and 4 will make that explicit. Volume 2 is the last moment before the political disintegration begins.

**What stays with you:** The scene in the forest with Oriana. The Poor Rock sequence — specifically Gandalin's silent, secret vigil, feeding his lord by hand in the dark. The moment the borrowed sword breaks in Ardan's combat and Amadis does not run. And the last image: Gandandel and Brocadan at the back of the celebrating hall, whispering.

### Pass 2 — Slower

**Amadis** is constructed as the perfection of chivalric virtue — brave, loyal, reverent, patient — and the book works by putting exactly that perfection under pressure. His greatness is not in doubt. What is tested is whether the man behind the greatness can survive it: survive the love that depends on another person's continued belief in him, survive the court politics that inevitably form around someone too good to be comfortable, survive the moment when his sword breaks and he has nothing left but the will to step forward rather than back. The Poor Rock sequence is the author's most honest moment: the finest knight in the world, reduced to refusing food in a hermit's cell because a letter said the wrong thing. The recovery is not heroic — it is ordinary, dependent, human. Gandalin feeds him. Oriana writes again. The right words arrive from outside. None of it is Amadis doing it himself.

**Oriana** is given more interiority than most chivalric heroines. She wrote the cruelest letter and wrote it because she believed a false report and was humiliated by what she imagined. The text does not excuse her. It also does not condemn her. It lets her be wrong in a specific, human way — the way someone who has waited twenty years for a love they cannot publicly claim is wrong when they finally crack. Her second letter is the retraction of someone who knows exactly what she has done. She does not ask forgiveness; she states what happened and leaves the rest to him. This is more psychologically complete than most of the romance tradition.

**Gandalin** is the figure the angle of this cycle opens up. He is present in virtually every scene involving Amadis — the squire who follows him to Poor Rock in secret, who keeps him eating, who watches from behind the barrier fence at Ardan's combat when the borrowed sword breaks. He is Amadis's shadow. His failure to prevent Matalesa's sword-theft is the one moment in the entire romance where he is visible as himself rather than as Amadis's extension — and he is visible through failure. He is twenty-two years old. He has served since he was twelve. The combat register, unless corrected, carries a note under his name for an event that was not his fault.

**Matalesa (the Insolent Damsel)** is a figure of malice who cannot think past the grudge. She takes the sword not to kill Amadis — she could not manage that — but to humiliate him, to make him fight Ardan with diminished tools, to watch him fail under whatever shame attaches to being weaponless on the day of a life-or-death combat. When Ardan dies anyway, she cannot survive what she has become. Her suicide is the text's way of writing the ending of a person who defined themselves entirely by opposition to something greater than themselves, and then watched that something survive them.

**Urganda the Unknown** functions as the romance's structural conscience. She arrives when the major movements shift; she speaks in riddles that the reader can partially parse with the benefit of knowing what happens next; she names the arc without changing it. Her appearance before the Ardan combat signals not only that Ardan will be defeated but that the political estrangement — Gandandel, Brocadan, Lisuarte's growing discomfort with Amadis's fame — is the real coming crisis.

**Gandandel and Brocadan** are the novel's most quietly frightening figures. They are not enchanters, not enemy knights. They are courtiers who have correctly identified that Amadis is too famous for Lisuarte's court to hold indefinitely, and who have decided to accelerate the fracture for their own position. They begin the campaign that will drive Books 3 and 4. They do it in whispers at the back of a celebration. This is the book's final image, and it is the right one.

### Pass 3 — Elaborate One Scene: The Borrowed Sword Breaks

The morning after the theft, Amadis stands in the armor room. Flamberge is gone. Gandalin stands behind him not saying anything. Outside, Ardan Canileo is already at the combat field, oiling his sword in front of the crowd, which is the specific kind of public ease that belongs to a man who expects to kill someone today.

Amadis borrows a sword from Don Bruneo's armorer. The armorer holds it out without comment. It is a good sword — adequate weight, decent edge, a blade that has seen honest use. It is not Flamberge. The hand knows the difference immediately: the grip is a finger's width too wide; the balance point is an inch too far forward. These are small differences that become large over two hours of fighting.

Amadis carries the borrowed sword onto the field. The crowd is enormous. Lisuarte is in the judge's chair. Oriana is in the upper gallery with her women. Gandalin is behind the barrier fence at the field's edge, standing very still.

Ardan Canileo is what the text says: huge, experienced, contemptuous. He opens with heavy overhead strikes meant to test the borrowed sword's quality — he can tell from the sound of the first block that it is not Amadis's usual weapon. He pushes. For an hour, Amadis holds ground and not much more. He bleeds from a notch at the left shoulder, from a glancing cut at the right forearm. The crowd goes quiet in the particular way crowds go quiet when they begin to believe what they are watching.

Then the sword breaks.

Not dramatically. Not with a ring and a slow fall. It sheers at the midpoint under a counter-block — there is a sound like a stick snapping — and Amadis is left with a hand's-width of edge and eight inches of steel below the hilt. He throws what remains at Ardan's face, not because it will do damage but because Ardan has to move his shield to deflect it, and in the quarter-second of shield movement Amadis steps forward.

He steps forward. Not back. Not sideways. Into the fight.

The broken lance fragment is on the ground at the field's edge, left from the crowd's opening ceremonies, point down in the churned grass. Amadis gets to it before Ardan reorganizes. He puts it against Ardan's sword arm at the elbow and levers. Ardan is strong. Amadis is stronger in this specific moment, because he has already decided that the only direction available to him is forward. The sword rotates in Ardan's grip by a quarter-turn, by a half-turn; Amadis catches the blade with his bare hand, taking a cut across the palm that will scar, and completes the rotation. The sword comes free.

Ardan Canileo stands in the combat field holding a shield with no sword. He takes two steps back. Amadis walks toward him with Ardan's own sword and a bloody hand and the particular expression of a man who has stopped calculating.

The decapitation is swift. The text is not lavish with it. Ardan falls.

In the upper gallery, Oriana makes no sound. In the armorer's area, Don Bruneo looks at his armorer. At the barrier fence, Gandalin watches his lord turn and scan the crowd, and understands that what Amadis is looking for is not applause.

What Amadis is looking for, in the first moment after the combat, is whether anyone is already writing it down.

They are. Three scribes, two herald's assistants, one combat register already open on a field desk under the judge's banner. They are writing down: the outcome, the weapons, the combatants — and in the section for the challenger's preparation, one note that reads: *sword absent at commencement; squire's accounting pending.*

Gandalin can read from the fence that something is being written down. He cannot read what it says. He does not need to.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **What the Register Omits** — The night before the Ardan Canileo duel, Matalesa stole Amadis's sword Flamberge from the armor room while Gandalin slept; Gandalin now carries a "squire's accounting pending" note in the combat register; three witnesses were present and have had their account notarized; if the Fighter carries the attestation to the master herald before the wax is poured on the register, Gandalin's record is amended; if not, the note stands for twenty years.

2. **Beltenebros's Deeds** — For six months, Amadis fought under the assumed name "Beltenebros" during his exile at Poor Rock; every tournament victory, every rescued dame, every defeated knight is entered in the combat register under that false name; unless an authenticated identity declaration connecting "Beltenebros" to "Amadis of Gaul" reaches the herald before the annual register is sealed, those six months of deeds belong permanently to a man who does not exist.

3. **Durinda's Passage** — The Damsel of Denmark found Amadis by a scar on his neck, delivered Oriana's reconciliation letter, and saved his life; she has been traveling home for three weeks; a faction at court is now claiming she fabricated the letter to ingratiate herself with Oriana; the Fighter carries the original seal-authenticated draft — evidence of Oriana's authorship — to the court's herald before the slander is entered into the court chronicle as the official account.

4. **Corisanda's Notarization** — Corisanda came to Poor Rock and kept Amadis's grief company for weeks at personal cost to her reputation; the official account of Amadis's recovery names Durinda and Gandalin and does not name her; a notarized witness statement from Andalod the hermit — who watched her come and stay and go — sits unsigned at the hermitage; the Fighter must ride to Peña Pobre, get Andalod's mark, and return it to the court record-keeper before the chronicle cycle closes.

5. **Urganda's Suppressed Verse** — From her ship's bow, Urganda delivered a riddle-prophecy in twelve stanzas; a court scribe transcribed all twelve; the eleventh stanza named an event that Gandandel's faction does not want in any archive; Gandandel has had the scribe arrested and the original transcription confiscated; the Fighter must retrieve the copy the scribe hid in the harbor district and deliver it to the master archivist before the arrest is formalized into an official suppression.

6. **The Madasima Covenant Dispute** — Amadis discharged the Madasima public covenant through negotiation rather than combat; Madasima's faction is filing a claim that the manner of discharge was technically deficient and honor was not satisfied; the signed covenant-discharge document sits with a neutral notary who will release it only to an acknowledged court agent; the Fighter must obtain that designation from Lisuarte's chamberlain and carry the document through Madasima's agents to the neutral arbiters before the dispute is entered into the record.

7. **The Letter in the Chest** — When Amadis was set adrift as an infant, his father's sword and ring were sealed in the chest with him; there was also a letter, from Elisena to Perión, that was never delivered; the letter still exists, held by the family of the Scottish knight who found the chest; it predates the marriage, names the circumstances, and changes the question of whether Amadis is legitimate; the Fighter carries it to the court chamberlain before the question is formally settled the wrong way.

### Theme Check

Reviewing the 27 existing Theme Threads Active:

- "Truth delivered posthumously" (LGW) — covers a dead woman's words carried through silence. **Not the same as Idea 1:** Gandalin is alive, not dead; the record is still open, not closed.
- "The institution silences its victim's tongue" (ARN) — covers a victim whose testimony is suppressed. **Not the same as Idea 1:** Gandalin is not silenced by force; the record simply does not register the theft as something he reported.
- "The man fate wrote for betrayal is still the man who betrayed" (CPH) — covers a man writing his own account against the court's simplified version. **Not the same as Idea 1:** Gandalin is not writing anything; the evidence comes from third parties; and the subject is not a divided life but a service record falsely shadowed by one event.
- "The relic embodies both the glory and the error" (OST) — covers a memorial that holds both truth and failure. **Not the same as Idea 1:** there is no relic; the subject is an administrative record, not a physical monument.
- "The obstacle only needs to cause delay" (CDG) — covers the temporal strategy of the enemy. **Similar in structure but not theme:** CDG's theme is the clock as weapon; BRU's theme is the margin of the record as the space where blame accumulates invisibly.

**Chosen quest:** Storyline 1 — "What the Register Omits"

**Theme:** *The champion's triumph erases the near-disaster that preceded it, and only the squire who served in the dark is left visible in the record's margin, blamed for what was stolen from him: the evidence that absolves him must arrive before the wax is poured, while the world looks at the victor*

This is distinct from all 27 existing themes. No previous quest centers on a secondary figure blamed in the margin of a primary figure's celebrated success. The fighter is not carrying testimony for the wronged person themselves (as in ARN, VIE, ERF) but for the person wrongly made accountable for the circumstance that preceded a famous triumph. The near-disaster is already over; the triumph is being celebrated; the injustice is happening quietly in the administrative aftermath.

### 5-Act Vignette Play — "What the Register Omits"

**TOKEN:** The Theft Attestation — a sealed notarized document, three witnesses' marks pressed in wax, tournament notary's seal; addressed to the Master Herald of Lisuarte's Court; the paper inside names Matalesa, names Flamberge, names the date, and concludes: *no negligence on the squire's part can be concluded from the circumstances of the theft*

---

**ACT I — At the Notary's Booth**

*The Theft Attestation · sealed, in a locked iron box · held by Renaud the Tournament Notary · Lisuarte's Tournament Precinct, the field's edge, afternoon after the combat*

The tournament field is empty. The crowds left with Amadis's name in their mouths — the name, the severed head, the story of the borrowed sword and the improvised lance and the moment the man who should have run did not run. The grass is still torn from it. The dark stain where Ardan fell is already drawing crows.

Renaud the notary sits in his canvas booth with a small iron box in his lap. He has been sitting here since the crowds dispersed. He has three signed statements in the box — two tournament stewards and one stable-hand who were in the armor room when Matalesa took the sword — and a wax seal ready, and he is not moving.

Gandalin is across the road. He is twenty-two years old and he has been Amadis's squire since he was twelve. He is not looking at the notary's tent. He is looking at the ground in front of him, the specific stillness of someone who has decided not to hope for anything in particular.

The Fighter enters the tent. Renaud looks up.

*He has been waiting for someone. He is not certain whether this is the right kind of someone.*

"Take it then," Renaud says finally, and puts the box on the table. "The seal is mine. The witnesses signed under their own names. I want no part of what comes after that door."

He opens the box. The Theft Attestation is inside, rolled tight, three pendant wax seals on the cord. The Fighter takes it.

It is heavier than paper should be.

---

**ACT II — The Record Clerk's Toll**

*The Theft Attestation · sealed, rolled tight, in the Fighter's hands · who holds it: the Fighter · Herald's Lodge Vestibule, the herald's precinct, late afternoon*

The master herald has a door. Between the Fighter and the door there is an anteroom with four clerks at long tables and one man at a standing desk with a submission log, and the man at the standing desk has been told to be thorough today.

"Forty-eight hour review," he says, not looking up. "All post-combat submissions. Standard procedure."

He stamps the log for a different document. He stamps it again for another. He does not look at the Fighter.

*The submission log is open. The list of names on his desk is handwritten. None of them are names of fighting men.*

The Fighter cites the Madasima covenant. Filed four hours after its associated combat; accepted without review; entered into the record same day. The clerk remembers the Madasima covenant. He has to remember it — it was three weeks ago and the whole court was watching.

He looks at the standing desk. He looks at the submission log. He looks at the stamp in his hand.

He stamps the log.

---

**ACT III — The Corridor**

*The Theft Attestation · stamped for submission, still sealed · who holds it: the Fighter · the inner corridor of the herald's lodge, between vestibule and master's chamber*

Two men in Gandandel's livery stand in the corridor. Not barring it. Present.

One holds a rolled document in a relaxed way — the way a man holds something official when he wants the official quality to be visible. "Writ of challenge," he says pleasantly. "Regarding a forged attestation currently in transit. The writ requires the document be held pending verification."

"The writ names Gandandel's household court," the Fighter says. "The attestation is addressed to the Master Herald of the King's Court. Gandandel's household has no jurisdiction here."

The man with the writ smiles. The man beside him, who has not spoken, moves his hand to his sword.

*The corridor is narrow. The answer to the jurisdiction argument is in the man beside him who has stopped listening to arguments.*

The Fighter moves first. The corridor is too close for anything careful. The first man goes down against the wall; the second man draws and swings in the same motion but the Fighter is already inside the swing. It is not a long fight. It is the kind of fight that is over before the principals have time to wonder how it went.

The Fighter picks up Gandandel's challenged writ from the floor. The door is close.

---

**ACT IV — Before the Wax Is Poured**

*The Theft Attestation · sealed, one corner slightly torn from the corridor · who holds it: the Fighter · the Weimar civil archive floor, the master herald's chamber*

The master herald is at his writing table. He is an old man with the hands of someone who has been holding a pen for forty years. The wax pot sits on a small brazier to his left. A page stands to the right with the seal-stamp, watching the pot. Around the floor: long clerk benches, an open document chest, a column, a low stool, stacks of the day's final submissions distributed across every available surface.

The wax is nearly ready. The page is watching the pot.

The Fighter is at the far door. Thirty steps. Six obstacles.

*This is not a fight. This is a different kind of problem.*

The Fighter goes through the room at the speed that the room allows: vault the bench, redirect off the column with one hand, catch the falling document stack with the other, do not stop. The wax pot is tilting on the brazier from the vibration of the floor — the page grabs it by reflex, which means the page's hands are occupied for three seconds.

The Theft Attestation goes on the table before the stamp comes down.

---

**ACT V — Into the Record**

*The Theft Attestation · on the table, unsealed now, the paper visible · held by the moment itself · the Master Herald's writing desk, Weimar*

The master herald reads it slowly. He reads all of it — the witnesses' descriptions, the notary's certification, the conclusion about negligence. When he finishes he sets the document down and looks at the Fighter with the expression of a man who has been filing records for a very long time.

"This creates a note in a record that everyone has already closed," he says. His voice is neither warm nor cold. "The combat is over. Amadis won. The story is finished. No one is asking about the sword."

He picks the document up again. He sets it down.

*He is not refusing. He is deciding what history needs, not what history wants.*

"Gandalin is twenty-two," the Fighter says. "He will be Amadis's squire for twenty more years. Every herald in the circuit will see this note."

The master herald looks at the document on his table. He opens the combat register. He finds the Ardan Canileo entry, already inked, already complete. He turns to the Exhibit section — the page at the back where supporting materials are filed.

He writes: *Exhibit C — Theft Attestation re: Flamberge. Received same day.*

He stamps it.

He files the Theft Attestation as Exhibit C in the Ardan Canileo combat record. He closes the register.

He does not look up when he says: "Tell the boy the record is complete."

## Cycle 2 — Beltenebros's Deeds

**What cycle 1 missed:** Cycle 1 took the squire's angle — the person in the champion's shadow, wrongly blamed in the record's margin through another's act. That angle is about correcting a false negative already written into the register. Cycle 2 takes a different structural problem: not a wrong entry but an absent connection. For six months Amadis fought under a name the register accepted as a different fighter's. Every deed that happened under "Beltenebros" happened. The register did not lie. It recorded what it was given. But the annual sealing of the record will permanently sever those six months from the man who lived them — not through malice but through the neutral operation of a system that cannot look behind a name. The identity declaration is the only mechanism that can retrospectively join the man to his months, and it must arrive before the annual seal.

---

### Pass 1 — Token

**The Incomplete Identity Declaration** is a half-signed parchment — two columns: one naming "Beltenebros of Poor Rock" and every tournament entry and deed registered under that name across six months; the other linking that name to "Amadis, son of Perión of Gaul, Knight of King Lisuarte's Court" by physical identification and dated testimony. Durinda the Damsel of Denmark has already pressed her mark to the first column — she recognized the scar at his neck, she knows both names, she was there at the transition point. The declaration needs a second witness: Andalod the hermit at Peña Pobre, who knew him as Beltenebros throughout the exile and knows the name he returned under. With both marks it is complete. Without Andalod's countersignature it is insufficient — a single witness attesting to an identity split between a public name and a pseudonym is not legally binding in the court's evidence standard.

---

### Pass 2 — Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **"What the Register Omits"** — USED (cycle 1): Gandalin's theft attestation; squire's record amended before the wax is poured
2. **"Beltenebros's Deeds"** ← **CHOSEN / USED (cycle 2)**
3. "Durinda's Passage" — Damsel of Denmark accused of fabricating Oriana's reconciliation letter; carry original authenticated draft to court herald before the slander is entered as official account
4. "Corisanda's Notarization" — Corisanda's presence at Poor Rock unrecorded; Andalod's witness statement at the hermitage; her name missing from the official recovery story
5. "Urganda's Suppressed Verse" — eleventh stanza of Urganda's prophecy politically suppressed; scribe's hidden copy recovered from harbor district and carried to master archivist
6. "The Madasima Covenant Dispute" — manner-of-discharge challenged; signed covenant-discharge document at neutral notary; Fighter obtains court agent designation and carries document through Madasima's agents
7. "The Letter in the Chest" — Elisena's original letter to Perión, sealed in the infant's chest, predates the marriage; held by the Scottish knight's family; delivery now changes what the past officially is

---

### Pass 3 — Theme Check

No existing thread covers: the anonymous combatant whose deeds accumulate correctly under a name that cannot survive the official record; the annual sealing as the neutral mechanism of permanent estrangement; the identity declaration that retroactively joins a man to six months he lived under a name the world accepted as someone else. The closest is *the man fate wrote for betrayal is still the man who betrayed* (CPH cycle 1) — but that is about a divided life the actor lived consciously through multiple treacheries; here the pseudonym was survival, not division, and the deeds were not in tension with the man's identity. Distinct thread: **The anonymous combatant whose six months of deeds were correctly entered under a false name; the annual seal as the neutral mechanism that permanently separates the man from his acts; the identity declaration as the only instrument that can retrospectively join them before the record closes.**

## 5-Act Vignette Play

*Lisuarte's court, late autumn. The annual combat register seals in three days. Durinda the Damsel of Denmark is back in court with a half-signed parchment and no way to reach the second witness herself — she has a court obligation she cannot leave. Andalod lives at Poor Rock, off the coastal headland, reached by sea. He is old and will not come to court. The Fighter has been sent to get his mark and return before the seal date.*

---

**ACT 1 — Herald's Lodge Vestibule (CHT)**

*The room smells of tallow and fresh ink. Durinda is at a side table with the incomplete declaration unrolled between her hands. She is not young; she has traveled far and fast to get this far; she has Amadis's scar description memorized from the day she found him in a hermit's cave, and the day she found him has been in her face ever since. She pressed her mark to the first column two days ago. The second column is blank.*

"Andalod will not come here," she says. "He has not left the island in forty years. He witnessed the whole of it — he knew the name Beltenebros from the first day, and he was standing at the chapel door when Amadis rode away under his own name. He knew both." She rolls the parchment carefully. "He will sign it if someone brings it to him in the right way. He will not sign it for someone who wants something from the archive."

She does not say what the right way is. That is the first thing.

**TOKEN GRANT:** The Incomplete Identity Declaration — Durinda's mark on the first column; second column blank; to be completed by Andalod's countersignature; addressed to the Annual Register Herald; seal date in three days.

**SKILL CHECK:** Insight DC 12
- *onPass:* Fighter understands what "the right way" means before Durinda finishes: not carrying the document as an administrative errand but as a recognition of what the six months were. Andalod will sign for someone who understands that the exile was real, not a legal fiction requiring cleanup. The Fighter takes the declaration and says nothing about the register.
- *onFail:* Durinda adds it plainly: "Do not speak to him about the register. Speak to him about Beltenebros. He will know what you need when you have told him what you understand." Fighter takes the declaration.

---

**ACT 2 — Coastal Road to the Harbor (CHT → PBK)**

*The coastal road runs three hours south to the fishing harbor where one man owns a boat large enough to reach Poor Rock in daylight and old enough to have carried Durinda out and back six months ago. He is mending a net when the Fighter arrives. He looks at the Fighter. He looks at the direction the Fighter came from.*

"Not this season," he says. He does not explain what season means. There are men in Gandandel's household who have been at the harbor for four days — just watching. Not doing anything. Watching the road from court and watching the sea road to Poor Rock.

**SKILL CHECK:** Persuasion DC 12
- *onPass:* Fighter does not try to explain the register or the urgency. Pays the full rate, and then pays the same again, and then says: "You carried the Damsel of Denmark six months ago." The boatman looks at the Fighter for a long moment. He puts down the net. He says: "High tide is in two hours."
- *onFail:* The boatman needs more than money. He needs the Fighter to say something that makes Gandandel's men irrelevant to the decision. Fighter names what the journey is for — not the archive, but Beltenebros — and the boatman nods slowly and puts down the net.

**ITEM GRANT:** The Boatman's Crossing Note — a brief line on oilskin recording the passage date and direction; the boatman's mark. Useful if Gandandel's watchers report the departure and someone later asks where the Fighter went.

---

**ACT 3 — Peña Pobre / Andalod's Hermitage (PBK)**

*Poor Rock at morning: a small island of grey stone and salt-grass, a chapel at the high point, a garden that has been tended for forty years by the same hands. Andalod is in the garden when the Fighter arrives by the stone path up from the landing. He does not look up immediately. He has been expecting someone since the sea went quiet an hour ago.*

"He came here with no name," Andalod says, kneeling beside a winter herb. "He called himself Beltenebros. I did not ask why. He stopped eating. He would have died." He stands, slowly. He looks at the Fighter. "He started eating again when the right words arrived. I did not write that down anywhere. I did not think it needed to be written down."

He looks at the incomplete declaration in the Fighter's hands.

**SKILL CHECK:** Composure DC 13
- *onPass:* Fighter says nothing about the register. Says: "Beltenebros happened. The register doesn't know it was him." Andalod looks at the first column — Durinda's mark, the list of deeds — for a long time. Then he says: "I know both names. I was present for both. That is what you need me to say." He takes the quill from his writing table inside the chapel. He signs the second column with the same care he brings to the garden. "Tell him Peña Pobre remembers him by the right name."
- *onFail:* Andalod will not sign for the register's sake. Fighter must find a different framing — not correction, not administrative necessity, but Andalod's own knowledge bearing witness to something that happened while he watched. The second try, more carefully given, lands.

**TOKEN STATE CHANGE:** The Incomplete Identity Declaration → now fully signed; Durinda's mark and Andalod's countersignature; the second column complete; legally sufficient.

**ITEM GRANT:** Andalod's Message — a verbal line to carry back: "Peña Pobre remembers him by the right name."

---

**ACT 4 — Sea Return (PBK → CHT)**

*The return crossing is rougher than the outbound — the autumn wind has come around to the south and the boatman is working hard. Gandandel's watchers were at the harbor when they left. Two light boats are on the water a quarter-mile back, running with the wind. They have been following since the island.*

*They do not want to kill the Fighter at sea — too visible, no control over the body or the document. They want to board the boat before it reaches the harbor, take the declaration, and be gone before anyone on shore knows what happened.*

**COMBAT:** Gandandel's Sea-Agents ×2, AC 13, HP 24
*The boats are close and closing. The boatman is working the tiller, saying nothing, which is a form of help. The declaration is in the Fighter's inner coat.*

---

**ACT 5 — Annual Register Herald's Desk (CHT)**

*The Herald's Lodge is lit by multiple lamps at late evening — the seal ceremony begins at the morning bell. The Annual Register Herald is at his long table, the register spread open, the day's final filings stacked beside him. A clerk is sorting. Two other heralds are copying the supplemental index. The seal date is tomorrow. This is the last evening it can be received.*

*The herald looks at the declaration. He reads both columns. He looks at the marks — Durinda's and Andalod's. He checks the Beltenebros entry in the register: seventeen combat entries, six tournament months, one note from the prior herald reading "independent traveler, confirmed knight by deed record."*

"Seventeen entries," he says. He is not objecting. He is calculating.

**SKILL CHECK:** Composure DC 11
- *onPass:* Fighter does not rush. The herald needs time to calculate — he is going to have to cross-reference each Beltenebros entry and annotate it with the identity link. Fighter holds the position at the desk without adding urgency. The herald works. After thirty minutes he looks up: "Filed. Beltenebros of Poor Rock equals Amadis of Gaul. Seventeen entries." He closes the declaration into the supplemental index. He stamps the index.
- *onFail:* Fighter says something about urgency; the herald sets the declaration to the side while he finishes other work. "I'll reach it before the bell." Fighter has to hold position and wait, which is the correct action the Fighter failed to choose. The declaration is filed. The seal date is met.

**TOKEN TAKE:** The Incomplete Identity Declaration → now fully signed and filed; the declaration enters the supplemental index as cross-reference 17B to the annual register; Amadis's six months reclaimed. Fighter leaves with empty hands and Andalod's message.

**ITEM GRANT:** The Register's Cross-Reference Receipt (annual supplement, 17B notation, dated and stamped).

## Cycle 3 — *Oriana's Dismissal Letter*

**Archive category:** Dismissal Records — The Letter That Said What the Writer Believed and Made It Permanent

**What this angle reveals:** Cycles 1-2 were Gandalin's clearing and Amadis's identity reclaimed. This angle enters the cause of the Poor Rock sequence: the letter Oriana wrote. It is precise, thorough, complete in its repudiation — twenty years of covert devotion rendered as evidence of betrayal, because Oriana received a false report and believed it. The letter is not wrong in its form. It says exactly what Oriana meant. It is wrong about its premise. She wrote it anyway because she had no other instrument available. The archive wants it because without this letter the Poor Rock sequence has no comprehensible cause, and because it is the only document that says what twenty years of faithful, hidden love looks like at the moment it believes itself betrayed.

---

**THE TOKEN: `orianas_dismissal_letter`** — Oriana's letter of dismissal to Amadis, written after receipt of the false report: the full repudiation, thorough and exact; twenty years of devotion written as evidence of betrayal; Oriana's personal seal broken when Amadis received it at the coastal inn. The letter that made him Beltenebros. The archive holds it because the cause should be in the same building as the consequence.
