## Pass 1 — Overview

The Tale of Genji is the world's first novel by any conventional measure — Murasaki Shikibu completed it at the Heian imperial court of Japan around 1008 CE, and Arthur Waley's 1925 translation calls it without qualification "the greatest novel of the East." It follows Genji — son of a Japanese emperor and a low-ranking lady, denied succession but granted a princely title — through his loves, alliances, and losses across decades of court life. The novel is not plot-driven in the modern sense; it is mood-driven, a sustained inquiry into the texture of feeling. Genji's world is one of layered obligations — to rank, to season, to aesthetic expectation — and the novel's subject is what it costs to be fully alive inside those layers.

For the roll2hit.com quest pipeline, the Genji's most useful elements are:
- **Objects as communication**: fans, scarves, letters, and music all carry meanings that direct speech cannot
- **The anonymous gift**: the poem without a sender's name is the purest form — it creates response without creating obligation
- **The thing left behind**: Utsusemi flees Genji and leaves her scarf; the abandoned object is the more honest part of her farewell
- **Handwriting as character testimony**: the rainy night discussion classifies women by the quality of their correspondence — the hand reveals the writer
- **The impossible letter**: Fujitsubo's notes to Genji make him tremble; once she becomes Empress, they cannot be acknowledged; they exist as a closed file
- **The shaped pupil**: young Murasaki is taught calligraphy, music, and taste to be Genji's ideal; the education is also a kind of creation
- **Jealousy that survives death**: Lady Aoi dies possessed by a rival's spirit; the jealousy that could not be expressed in life finds its final form in possession

Unique features as quest material:
- The first novel gives us not plot mechanics but relational mechanics — how social obligation generates documents (poems, letters, records)
- Anonymous address as a system — the fan poem requires response but permits the receiver to choose whether to pursue the sender
- The pedagogy of desire — to teach someone to be exactly what you need is a form of commission
- The possessed corpse as witness — the spirit's claim is the clearest statement of what the love was worth

## Pass 2 — Structure

### Part 1 Summary

**Chapter I — Kiritsubo**: The Emperor favors a low-ranking lady (Kiritsubo) so much that the court grows jealous; the jealousy kills her through accumulated social cruelty before Genji is old enough to remember her. Genji grows up beautiful, talented, and slightly displaced — neither heir nor common noble. A Korean fortune-teller predicts he has imperial destiny but that claiming it would endanger the realm; the Emperor makes him a commoner with a great name: Genji the Shining. He marries the Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal's daughter (Lady Aoi), obligatorily, without interest. The chapter establishes: Genji's mother's death as founding grief; his resemblance to Fujitsubo (the Emperor's new consort, whom the Emperor chose partly because she resembles Kiritsubo); and the court as a place where too much imperial favor kills the object of it.

**Chapter II — The Broom-Tree**: A rainy night. Genji, his friend Tô no Chûjô, and two others take shelter and pass the time classifying women. The classification has three tiers: high-born women (too formal, too constrained — no surprises), low-born women (no cultivation worth talking about), and the middle class — most interesting, most varied, most dangerous, most alive. Uma no Kami tells of a devoted, jealous lover who drove herself to death over small things; Tô no Chûjô tells of a secret lover who disappeared (taking their child) after he stopped writing; a third man tells of a garlic-scholar's daughter whose letter-writing was impeccable but whose breath was intolerable — and that he could not have one without the other. The chapter establishes: handwriting as the primary evidence of a woman's quality; the middle class as the novel's true subject; and the fatal taxonomy as comedy until it isn't.

**Chapter III — Utsusemi**: Genji visits a provincial governor's young wife (Utsusemi — cicada) twice. First visit: he enters her room; she receives his attention without warmth but does not refuse. Second visit: she hears him coming and flees, leaving behind a thin silk scarf. He ends up with the Lady of the West (Nokiba no Ogi), who was sleeping beside Utsusemi. He is left holding Utsusemi's scarf. The chapter's image: the cicada shedding its husk — she escaped him but left her outer form behind. Genji wears the scarf under his robe for months. The abandoned object is more present than its owner.

**Chapter IV — Yugao**: Genji visits his nurse in a poor district. He notices a humble house with a wooden fence covered in white evening-face flowers. A girl from the house approaches his servant with a white fan — on it a poem: the flower is the evening-face, strange in its dress of shining dew. No signature. The fan smells of a rare perfume. Genji replies with a poem; a correspondence begins; he eventually visits. The woman (Yugao — evening face, a name given her by the flowers, not her real name, which Genji never learns) becomes his lover. He takes her secretly to a deserted mansion. She dies there — possessed, suddenly, by a spirit that speaks through her before she goes silent. Genji holds her body in the dark, unable to call for help without exposing the secret. Koremitsu arranges the funeral quietly. Genji falls ill returning home.

### Part 2 Opening Content (offset 1-30)

Confirms: Yugao has died; Genji has fever; Koremitsu manages the funeral logistics secretly. Genji visits her body. The cause is spirit possession — a jealous spirit from elsewhere reached her in the deserted house. The chapter continues with Genji's extended illness and subsequent recovery.

### Part 3 Opening Content (offset 1-30)

Murasaki scenes: Genji at home with young Murasaki (his child ward, who resembles Fujitsubo), playing a thirteen-stringed zithern together. She learns to accompany him on flute. Domestic teaching scenes. Fujitsubo is named Empress. Genji's torment: she is now beyond reach in a new register; the letters he has from her are closed documents. The chapter continues with Genji's court conduct and growing political complications.

## AMS-01 Full Vignette Spec: "The Scarlet Fence Poem"

### Source Hook

In Chapter IV of the novel, Genji's servant returns from a neighbor's house with a white fan bearing a poem — sent anonymously, over a hedge, by a woman Genji has not yet met. The flower at the hedge is the evening-face (yugao); the poem uses the flower as pseudonym; the sender's real name never appears in the chapter. Genji replies. A correspondence begins. The woman becomes his lover. She dies possessed by a spirit in a deserted mansion before Genji learns her real name. The fan is the first moment of a love that ends in possession and death — and the fan carried no name.

The quest hook: the anonymous poem is the purest form of address because it creates an obligation that belongs entirely to the receiver. The sender has removed herself from the exchange. Whatever happens next is the receiver's decision, conducted in full knowledge that they are responding to no one they can name.

### 1367 Setting

**Ahmad ibn Ibrahim** — scholar and manuscript dealer at the Nishapur madrasa, eastern Khorasan. Ahmad acquired a cedarwood writing box from a Nishapur caravanserai keeper who purchased it from the estate of a Japanese Zen monk (name: Tesshu Ryokan) who died of illness near Nishapur while traveling with a Mongol-era diplomatic correspondence mission. Inside the box: a silk scroll bearing brushwork verse in Japanese kana, followed by two stanzas of partial Persian translation in a second hand. Ahmad cannot read the Japanese; he has read the Persian translation enough to understand it is a scholarly commentary on anonymous address in poetry — a practice across traditions — and he believes the Greek scholar **Anastasios of Trebizond** (currently resident in Tabriz, working on a comparative Eastern poetics for the Byzantine court) will be able to complete the translation and has the right audience for the material.

Ahmad wants to send the box to Anastasios. The complication: the box bears a chrysanthemum wax seal on the exterior, a mark of Eastern (Mongolian-era, Japanese diplomatic) provenance that Jalayirid customs officers have been logging under a current order to inventory foreign state property passing through Khorasan and Tabriz territory. Ahmad is afraid the box will be seized before it reaches the scholar.

**TOKEN:** A cedarwood writing box, interior lined in dark brocade, containing a rolled silk scroll (brushwork verse in an Eastern script, with partial Persian translation beneath in a second hand). The exterior bears a chrysanthemum wax seal. The box smells of cedar and old silk. No name on the outside.

**TOKEN granted:** Act 1 (Ahmad places the box in the carrier's hands)
**TOKEN checked:** Acts 2, 3, 4
**TOKEN taken:** Act 5 (the deacon receives it on Anastasios's behalf, or Anastasios himself)

**Commission city:** Nishapur (NIS) — scholarly city, eastern Khorasan, 1367 under fragmenting Muzaffarid-adjacent rule
**Delivery city:** Tabriz (TBZ) — Jalayirid Persian court city, scholar quarter near the court precinct

### Act 1 — Nishapur (Ahmad's study at the madrasa)
**DC 12 | Insight or Persuasion**

Ahmad has the box on his desk and will not stop giving conflicting instructions. He says: "Tell no official what it contains." Then: "Announce it at every checkpoint as scholarly property — it is exempt from tariff." Then: "But do not draw attention." He is circling something he will not name.

The problem is real: the chrysanthemum seal will attract customs officials who are inventorying Eastern diplomatic property under a current Jalayirid order. If the carrier announces the box as scholarly property, inspectors will want to open it; if hidden and found, it looks like smuggling. Ahmad needs to choose one policy and explain the actual risk so the carrier can navigate the route.

**Skill check purpose:** Press Ahmad into a single clear directive and extract the actual reason for his anxiety.

- **Success (Insight/Persuasion DC 12):** The carrier gets Ahmad talking about the customs order specifically. Ahmad admits: the box's chrysanthemum seal is the problem, not the contents. He advises: announce it as scholarly property at the Maragha junction (Act 3) — the officers there know the madrasa seal on the letter he's including; they will accept scholarly exemption. At other checkpoints, keep it in the saddlebag. He also gives the carrier Anastasios's authentication phrase: "She had no name he could repeat, and he never learned it until she was gone."
- **Failure:** The carrier sets out with both instructions unresolved. At Act 3, having no clear policy, the carrier improvises — the Deception route becomes the only option, at higher difficulty.

**Flavor:** Ahmad's study is deep in the madrasa's south wing, past three locked doors. The box is already packed. He has been ready for a week but keeps finding reasons not to send it. When the carrier finally presses him, he is quiet for a moment, then says: "The man who owned it died here. I did not know him. His student came to ask about it and I said I hadn't seen it. That was a lie." He hands over the box.

### Act 2 — Mountain Road West of Nishapur (Khorasan Highway, spring pass)
**DC 12 | Athletics or Survival**

Forty miles west of Nishapur, the main road passes through a low mountain cut. A winter rockfall — still present, not yet cleared — has narrowed the road to single-file foot traffic. A crew of six road-workers is clearing it; they say two days' more work. A caravanserai thirty yards back from the blockage is doing good business charging storage fees. The keeper is offering to hold luggage "for safety" while travelers wait — with the implication that the box stays in his hands overnight as surety for the room.

The carrier needs to move through without surrendering the box.

**Three options:**
- **Goat track (Athletics DC 12):** A narrow goat track skirts the rockfall on the uphill side — loose shale, one bad step possible, passable with good footing and the box secured. Success: through in an hour. Failure: the box is dropped (no damage, but a brief confrontation with a road-worker who found it).
- **Bypass road (Survival DC 12):** Navigate the local terrain to find the farmers' bypass that locals use. Success: three hours but avoids the caravanserai and the customs pressure entirely. Failure: get turned around and arrive at the caravanserai after dark.
- **Wait (automatic):** Two days. No roll. The caravanserai keeper will ask about the box twice more before morning.

**Flavor:** The road-workers have been here four days. They are not hostile; they are tired and cold and eating cold bread at the margin of the cut. The caravanserai keeper is watchful in the professional way — he notices what everyone carries. The box is small enough to look like documents or trade goods; he has not identified it specifically. He just knows the carrier is reluctant to leave it.

### Act 3 — Maragha Junction (Post-Station, Road Checkpoint)
**DC 12 | Persuasion or Deception**

The Jalayirid post-station at the Maragha junction has two functions: mail routing and customs check. The customs officer on duty today — a man named Farhad Bayani, third-generation descendant of Ilkhanid administrative families, now operating under Jalayirid authority — has read the current inventory order. He sees the chrysanthemum seal. He identifies it (incorrectly but plausibly) as a mark associated with Yuan Chinese state gifts — the category being inventoried. He wants to open the box, record the contents, and hold it pending authentication.

He is not corrupt. He is doing his job.

**Skill check purpose:** Get the box past the customs officer without inspection.

- **Success (Persuasion DC 12, easier if Ahmad's letter from Act 1 is presented):** The madrasa seal on Ahmad's accompanying letter is recognized. Scholarly property: exempt from the inventory order under a carve-out established three years ago for academic correspondence. Farhad accepts the exemption but notes the box in his ledger as "examined and cleared, Nishapur madrasa, scholarly property." The carrier passes with a one-hour delay and a note in the log.
- **Success (Deception DC 12, harder without the Act 1 policy):** The carrier claims the seal is a merchant guild mark, not a state mark — Eastern silk merchant, Tabriz-registered. Farhad is uncertain. He accepts with visible reluctance and notes "uncertain provenance, passed on carrier's attestation."
- **Failure:** Farhad insists on opening the box. The silk scroll is visible; the brushwork is clearly not a trade document. He is uncertain what to do with it — he doesn't want to hold a scholarly manuscript; he wants Eastern state property. He agrees to let the carrier proceed but copies the chrysanthemum seal into his ledger for the Tabriz customs office to follow up. This creates an Act 5 complication: a Jalayirid customs note arrives at the scholar quarter the same morning the carrier does.

**Flavor:** Farhad's desk is covered in route ledgers. He has a cup of tea he has not touched. He looks at the chrysanthemum and looks at the carrier and says: "I have six of these to log this week. This one does not look like the others." He is right. He is not sure in which direction he is right.

---

### Act 4 — Village Road East of Tabriz (Late Afternoon, Roadside)
**DC 12 | Medicine or Insight**

A Japanese monk is lying in the shade of a stone wall at the village edge, ten miles from Tabriz. He is Tesshu Ryokan's student — the same monk mentioned by Ahmad in Act 1, the one Ahmad lied to about the box. He recognized the carrier on the road and has been following since Maragha by a faster track. He has been pushing himself for two days; now he has fever and road-sickness. He is conscious but barely.

He is not hostile. He is genuinely ill. But if he reaches Anastasios before the carrier, he will make a competing claim: the box belongs to his master's lineage; Ahmad took it without proper authority; he has documents (in a pouch at his side) that establish the chain of ownership.

**Two approaches:**
- **Medicine DC 12:** Treat the fever — water, rest, the correct herbs from the roadside kit. If successful, Tesshu becomes coherent enough to be grateful and explains the box's history: his master received it as a gift from the Japanese court, carried it west as a teaching object, not intending to leave it in Nishapur. He does not want it destroyed. He wants it to reach the right hands. He offers the carrier the authentication phrase for Anastasios — the phrase Ahmad gave (if Act 1 succeeded) is confirmed; if Act 1 failed, this is the first time the carrier hears it. He asks to accompany the carrier into Tabriz at a slower pace.
- **Insight DC 12:** The carrier reads Tesshu's intentions: he is not trying to steal the box; he is trying to be present at the delivery because he wants to see it received correctly. He will not fight. He will not call for officials. His illness is real. If the carrier can reach Tabriz two hours before Tesshu recovers enough to walk, there is no competing claim in time.

**Failure (no roll or failed both):** Tesshu arrives at Anastasios's gate one hour after the carrier. He makes his claim. The deacon holds both parties outside while he sends a runner to find Anastasios in Maragha. The Act 5 difficulty increases significantly.

**Flavor:** The village has a sheep pen and a bread oven and nothing else. Tesshu is propped against the wall with his traveling satchel under his head. He does not speak when the carrier approaches — he watches. When he finally speaks, it is in very careful Persian, slowly: "I know which box you are carrying. I am not here to take it. I am here because my teacher is dead and I want to see it arrive." He looks at the carrier for a long moment. "You do not have to trust me. But the phrase written on the inner lid of the box — do you know what it says?"

### Act 5 — Tabriz, Scholar Quarter (Anastasios's House)
**DC 12 | Persuasion or Deception**

Anastasios is not at home. He left three days ago for Maragha — the post-station there is holding a manuscript shipment he was waiting on. His gate-keeper, a Syrian deacon named Demetrios, is polite, unhurried, and operating under Anastasios's standing instructions: accept no manuscripts on the scholar's behalf without either the scholar's written authorization or the specific phrase from the intake protocol.

Outside the gate: a Venetian factor named Domenico, who has been waiting two hours with an offer letter to purchase Eastern manuscripts for his employer. He is watching the carrier carefully.

**Conditions:**
- If Act 1 succeeded (carrier has the authentication phrase): Persuasion DC 12. The carrier presents the phrase ("She had no name he could repeat, and he never learned it until she was gone"). The deacon confirms it matches the intake protocol. He accepts the box, issues an interim receipt, and logs it under Anastasios's standing classification: "Anonymous Address." The Venetian factor watches, folds his offer letter, walks east.
- If Act 1 failed but Act 4 succeeded (Tesshu gave the phrase, carrier has it): Same as above — Persuasion DC 12.
- If neither Act 1 nor Act 4 succeeded (carrier does not have the phrase): Deception DC 12. The carrier must fabricate a reason the deacon should accept the box — an official errand, a madrasa authority claim, an invented emergency. Success: deacon accepts under provisional intake ("held pending scholar's return, authentication outstanding"). Fail: the box waits outside. The Venetian factor makes his offer again.
- If Act 3 failed (customs note arrives): Add +2 to any DC at this act — the deacon has heard from the customs office and is nervous about the box's provenance.
- If Act 4 failed and Tesshu arrived first: The deacon is holding two parties at the gate. The carrier must outargue Tesshu's competing claim (Persuasion DC 14) or both parties wait for Anastasios.

**Flavor:** Demetrios is small, precise, and has been doing this job for eleven years. He has a ledger he carries under his left arm. He knows what he is allowed to do and what he is not. The Venetian factor has good shoes and a pleasant face and does not stop looking at the box. When the carrier presents the authentication phrase, the deacon writes it down, checks it against a card in his ledger, and says: "Yes. That is the one." He takes the box without ceremony and closes the gate.

### onPass

Anastasios's receipt arrives three days later at Ahmad's madrasa: *"Chrysanthemum seal received. Intake logged under Anonymous Address, per standing protocol. The silk scroll has been examined. The brushwork verse is a commentary on the practice of sending poems without signature — the argument being that the sender's absence is the condition that makes the poem honest. The Persian translation in the second hand is correct but stops before the argument concludes. I will finish it. Tell your carrier: the fan inside the box has no name on it. This is the correct condition."*

The Venetian factor sent an offer to Anastasios directly; the letter was declined without reply.

---

### onFail

The box reaches Anastasios via the Maragha post, one week late. The deacon logs it under "Correspondence: delayed." The silk scroll is intact. The fan inside has been refolded — Demetrios's hands, then Anastasios's — neither man knew what he was handling. Neither damaged it. Anastasios writes to Ahmad: *"Received. Late. The brushwork verse is exactly what I hoped for. There is a fan inside the box — no name on it. Who sent this originally? I would like to write to them."* Ahmad writes back: *"I do not know her name. Neither did the man who sent it."*

## Seeds AMS-02 through AMS-06

### AMS-02 — "The Cicada's Husk"
**Source hook:** Utsusemi flees Genji's second visit, leaving behind a thin silk scarf on the sleeping mat. He ends up with her companion instead. He wears the scarf under his own robe for months. The cicada sheds its husk; the husk is what remains.

**1367 commission:** A Tabriz silk merchant received a consignment of Eastern fabric wrapped around a document he cannot read. The document — tucked inside a folded silk scarf, evidently used as packing material by someone who knew what they were doing — is a scholarly note in Japanese that his Greek-speaking contact Anastasios (Tabriz) says he cannot deal with; it should go to a Byzantine manuscript collector in Trebizond who handles Eastern personal correspondence. The scarf itself is also to be delivered — it was clearly used deliberately, not accidentally; it is part of whatever message is being sent.

**Route:** TBZ → TRB (Tabriz → Trebizond)
**TOKEN:** A folded silk scarf, very thin, wrapped around a sealed note. The scarf is old and faintly perfumed; the note inside is sealed with red wax, no mark.
**Theme:** The thing left behind when someone flees is not left accidentally; the object that remains when the person goes is the truest part of the farewell — more honest than anything the person could have said

### AMS-03 — "The Rainy Night Register"
**Source hook:** The rainy night discussion: Uma no Kami, Tô no Chûjô, and Genji pass a sleepless night classifying women by their correspondence. The argument is that handwriting reveals character; that the middle class of women — educated enough to write, free enough to be themselves — produces the most interesting letters and the most unexpected lives. A classified bundle of letters, sorted by quality of hand, with scholarly commentary, is itself a kind of legal document.

**1367 commission:** A Byzantine calligrapher in Trebizond (Nikephoros, attached to the imperial court in residence) has prepared an analysis of handwriting samples — drawn from letters in three languages — demonstrating how character can be read from the quality of the script. He has been commissioned to send this analysis to a scholar-jurist in Constantinople who is working on the question of whether correspondence can serve as character testimony in civil courts. The analysis is to be delivered in a sealed register, not opened in transit.

**Route:** TRB → CON (Trebizond → Constantinople)
**TOKEN:** A sealed leather register containing a handwriting analysis with folded sample letters mounted inside. The seal is the Trebizond imperial court stamp.
**Theme:** Handwriting is testimony; the expert who classifies character from letter-formation reads a court document that no official inquiry can replicate — what a hand makes under no observation is the most truthful record a person produces

---

### AMS-04 — "The Closed File"
**Source hook:** Fujitsubo's letters to Genji are so charged that receiving them makes him tremble. When she is named Empress, the correspondence becomes impossible — the letters already received are a closed file, evidence of something that cannot be acknowledged. But they cannot be destroyed either. They are the impossible obligation preserved.

**1367 commission:** A Persian court lady in Tabriz — a widow of a Jalayirid official — has dictated a series of sealed letters over the past year to a man she can no longer address directly (he has been elevated to a position where her correspondence would be politically dangerous to him). She has decided not to deliver them to him but to deliver them to the Weimar archive, sealed, permanently unopened, with instructions that they are to be filed under the recipient's name as "correspondence received but not transmitted." The act of filing is the only acknowledgment possible.

**Route:** TBZ → WM (Tabriz → Weimar archive)
**TOKEN:** A cedar tube, sealed at both ends with black wax, containing seven folded letters tied with a green cord. No recipient name on the outside — only a note: "Filed as received. Not for opening."
**Theme:** The document that creates an obligation both parties know cannot be met must still be delivered somewhere; the archive that receives the impossible demand is the only form of acknowledgment both parties can afford

### AMS-05 — "The Teaching Fan"
**Source hook:** Genji finds young Murasaki (a child who resembles Fujitsubo) and takes her as a ward. He teaches her calligraphy, music, seasonal aesthetics, and taste. The education is also a design: he is making her into his ideal. The annotated music scores, the corrected calligraphy examples — all carry the asymmetry of the teacher who can see the gap between what he teaches and what he means, and knows the student cannot yet see it.

**1367 commission:** A Japanese-language scholar in Samarkand — a rare figure, a Zen monk's student who stayed in the city after his master died — has compiled a set of annotated music tablatures: Japanese koto notation adapted for the Persian dutar, with commentary on the pedagogical differences between teaching a tradition you learned by inheritance and teaching a tradition you learned by translation. ## §AMS-02 — Vignette Cycle 2: "The Spirit's Account"

### Cycle vs. Cycle — What This Angle Reveals

Cycle 1 ("The Scarlet Fence Poem") took the angle of the anonymous gift: the sender's absence is the condition that makes the poem honest — the fan over the hedge with no name removes the sender's obligation and makes the receiver's act entirely their own. Honesty through absence.

Cycle 2 takes the opposite: honesty through involuntary presence. Rokujô could not speak her claim in life without violating every social constraint the Heian court imposed. Her spirit spoke it exactly, through a dying woman, without those constraints — saying what she had waited years to say. The physician who wrote down what the spirit said has the most honest record of that love in existence, because the speaker had no ability to choose what to say. Cycle 1 was the chosen absence that creates freedom; cycle 2 is the unchosen presence that creates testimony.

### Act III — Maragha (MRG): Finding the Woman

*The Moon-on-Water Fan · in the woman's possession · the Fighter arriving at the Saffron House inn*

The Saffron House inn in Maragha, near the Maragha observatory district: a clean caravanserai with a courtyard, the smell of saffron and charcoal smoke. The woman — Dilnoza, a young Uzbek scholar's assistant, traveling with her brother and two colleagues toward Isfahan — is in the courtyard with the fan open in her hand, using it to shade her eyes from the late afternoon sun. She is looking at it with interest — she noticed the cipher mark on the inner rib and has been wondering what it means.

The fighter explains. The situation is straightforward. Dilnoza is intelligent and not possessive about an object she received by mistake.

### Act IV — Road Back to Tabriz (Road): The Fan's Problem

*The Moon-on-Water Fan · recovered, in the Fighter's hands · road back to Tabriz*

On the road back toward Tabriz with the fan, the fighter meets the court official's personal messenger — riding hard south toward Maragha — who carries an update: the embassy reception has been moved up by a day. There are now fewer than two days. And separately: the official, upon reflection, has decided he does not want the specific fan returned to the set. The fan has, in the forty-eight hours of its absence, acquired the quality of having been in unknown hands. He has had a copy made for the set by a local craftsman (the cipher is slightly different — two crescents, not quite interlocking). He wants the original fan sent to Weimar rather than returned.

The messenger explains this change of plan carefully and gives the fighter a letter for Sweelinck.

"The fan that was exchanged in darkness and recovered in daylight," the messenger says, reading from the letter, "has a different nature now than before the exchange. The official does not know with whom the fan spent three days. He prefers not to know. He would rather the archive know."

*What the Fighter sees:* This is the moment Dilnoza's question becomes relevant. The fan was given away; now the official refuses to have it back. The exchange that happened in daylight — Rustem giving it as a courtesy, Dilnoza receiving it with curiosity, the fighter recovering it with an explanation — has made the fan something the official cannot use for an official purpose.

### Act V — Weimar (WM): The Archive

*The Moon-on-Water Fan · returned · Archivus Sweelinck's intake desk · Weimar*

Archivus Sweelinck opens the fan. He looks at the silverleaf ground, the painted moon, the inner rib with the two interlocking crescents. He reads the official's letter. He reads Dilnoza's message to Rustem if the fighter has it.

"The fan was exchanged in darkness," he says. "It was given by mistake to a woman who looked at it by daylight and noticed what it was. It was recovered. Now the official cannot use it. Because the exchange already happened." He sets the fan on the intake desk. "The identifying mark on the inner rib proves it belongs to the official. The fact that it has been in unknown hands means it cannot serve that function anymore. The proof and the disqualification are the same thing." He is quiet for a moment. "The same fan. The same cipher. Different history."

He opens the classification ledger. "Diplomatic Documents — Objects That Prove Identity and Simultaneously Negate It." He writes for a long time. "First filing: the Moon-on-Water Fan, via Maragha, with the official's acknowledgment that return was declined."
