## Four Sentences

Written in Georgia around 1225 AD, *The Knight in the Panther's Skin* (*Vepkhistqaosani*) is a chivalric verse romance composed at the court of Queen Tamar, set across a mythologized Arabia, India, and the fictional kingdom of Cathay — a world of palaces, wilderness, and sea-cave prisons where knighthood is tested to its uttermost limit. It belongs to the tradition of the great medieval epics, occupying a place alongside Dante and the Persian masters — a courtly poem in which war and weeping are equally honoured expressions of the heroic soul. Shota Rustaveli is Georgia's national poet in the fullest sense: *The Knight in the Panther's Skin* is Georgia's Shakespeare, its Homer, the text inscribed on wedding chests and read aloud at feasts for eight centuries, memorized in its entirety by educated Georgians the way English schoolchildren once memorized Milton. What makes it immortal is its insistence that love is not weakness but the highest spiritual discipline — that grief, when endured with loyalty and courage, is itself a form of heroism — and that friendship between men, solemnly sworn, is as sacred and binding as any oath to God.

## Long Summary

### The World and Its Heroes

The poem opens at the court of Rostevan, the great king of Arabia, whose only heir is his daughter Tinatin — a woman of such beauty, wisdom, and moral authority that the poem never lets the reader doubt her fitness to rule. Rostevan voluntarily abdicates in her favour, an act that sets the poem's central ethical argument in motion: real kingship recognises excellence wherever it lives, including in women. Tinatin is not a passive beauty awaiting rescue. She is a sovereign, a thinker, and the architect of the quest that drives the entire poem. Her court champion is Avtandil, a young knight of unsurpassed martial skill, who loves Tinatin with total devotion — and the devotion is returned, though court protocol makes its open expression impossible. Theirs is a love that exists in the space between the said and the unsaid, conducted through glances, through the sending of champions on impossible errands, through poems left on the pillow and messages carried by trusted women. Tinatin loves Avtandil enough to send him away into years of danger because she trusts him to return, and Avtandil loves Tinatin enough to go.

The inciting event is strange and dreamlike. While riding near the shore, King Rostevan and Avtandil glimpse a knight weeping alone by a stream — a man of extraordinary beauty and martial bearing, clothed in a panther's skin, his cheeks streaked with tears that fall like pearls. When they approach, the knight vanishes. Rostevan sends Avtandil to find this mysterious weeping knight and learn his name and story. This is Tinatin's first act of queenship: when Avtandil hesitates — because to seek the stranger means leaving Tinatin for an indefinite time — it is she who charges him. "Go," she says, in effect. "This is what I ask. Prove your love by leaving me." The logic is characteristically Rustavelian: love is proved not by proximity but by sacrifice.

### Tariel — The Grief-Bound Hero

After three years of searching, Avtandil finds the knight of the panther's skin. His name is Tariel, and his story — told to Avtandil in a wilderness cave, with both men weeping openly at the sorrow of it — is the emotional core of the poem. Tariel was the supreme champion of the king of India, a figure of mythic martial power. He was also the adopted son of the Indian court, raised alongside the king's daughter, Nestan-Darejan. He and Nestan-Darejan fell in love: a love that the poem presents as absolute, metaphysical, and beyond all earthly judgment. Nestan-Darejan is depicted with language usually reserved for divine figures — she is the sun, the rose of the world, the very reason beauty exists.

The tragedy begins when the king of India, unaware of Tariel and Nestan-Darejan's love, arranges her marriage to the prince of Khvarazmia. Nestan-Darejan secretly sends Tariel a letter commanding him to fight the wedding — to kill the Khvarazmian prince rather than allow the marriage. Tariel obeys, slaying the prince. This act, done in love and at his beloved's direct command, becomes the wound around which the rest of the poem bleeds. The king of India is furious. The Khvarazmians demand war. But most devastating of all: Nestan-Darejan, the woman who sent the letter, disappears. The king's chamberlain, Usen, and his wife Davar — Nestan-Darejan's foster-mother — have hidden her away, then sold her into captivity to cover their own treachery. Tariel tears the chamberlain to pieces with his bare hands when he learns this. Then, with no thread to follow, no kingdom willing to help him search, Tariel abandons the court, abandons his military rank, and retreats into the wilderness to grieve.

This is where we find him at the story's beginning — not mad, but destroyed by grief into something that transcends ordinary sorrow. He has become grief itself, clothed in the skin of the animal he hunts alone, weeping at streams, living in a cave with only the memory of Nestan-Darejan to sustain him. Rustaveli does something radical here: he does not treat Tariel's sustained public weeping as weakness. The poem presents his grief as the truest measure of the depth of his love. A man who has lost the most beautiful and beloved woman in the world, and who continues to exist in that loss without betraying her or forgetting her, is not pitiable. He is magnificent. The panther's skin is not a symbol of savagery but of Tariel's determination to remain outside the human world — to refuse to participate in courts and politics and arranged marriages until Nestan-Darejan is found or he is dead.

Tariel's toughest moral decision comes when Avtandil finally finds him and offers friendship and help. Tariel must choose whether to accept help — whether to allow another person into the sanctum of his grief — and whether to admit that he cannot rescue Nestan-Darejan alone. For a warrior of Tariel's pride, this is no small thing. He chooses openness, and the poem treats this as its own kind of heroism: the willingness to be seen in one's devastation, to trust another man with the full weight of one's love story.

### Avtandil — The Hero of Divided Loyalty

Avtandil is perhaps the more complex hero because he is defined by a structural conflict that the poem never resolves cheaply. After hearing Tariel's story, Avtandil swears brotherhood with him and commits to helping find Nestan-Darejan. He returns to Tinatin's court and reports — but then must ask permission to leave again, this time potentially for years, on a quest in service of a friend rather than his sovereign queen. Tinatin grants permission. Avtandil departs.

The second departure is harder than the first. During his second journey, Avtandil discovers through the merchant Usen (a different character, or perhaps the same name used with slight variation across translations) and through the slave-woman Asmat — who has been living alone at the edge of the wilderness caring for Tariel from a distance — crucial intelligence: Nestan-Darejan has been sold to the king of the sea-fortress Kajeti, a demon-lord whose island stronghold is virtually impregnable. No single knight can storm it. The quest requires an army.

Here is Avtandil's second great moral crisis. His king in Arabia, the old Rostevan, sends word demanding that Avtandil return to court. It is a direct royal command from the sovereign he serves. Avtandil disobeys. He chooses Tariel's cause — and by extension, the rescue of Nestan-Darejan — over his feudal obligation. He writes a letter explaining himself, but the explanation cannot undo the fact of disobedience. Rustaveli does not soften this. Avtandil breaks his feudal oath, and the poem treats this as a tragedy even as it endorses it: loyalty to sworn friendship is presented as equal to or greater than loyalty to a throne.

### Pridon — The Third Brother

The three heroes are united when Avtandil recruits Pridon, the king of Mulghazanzer, a warrior whose ships and army will be needed for the assault on Kajeti. Pridon's involvement is cemented by the law of knightly brotherhood that the poem establishes: once a warrior of honour is told the full truth of a just cause, he is bound to assist. Pridon's integration into the fellowship is swift but genuine, and the three men — Tariel, Avtandil, Pridon — represent the poem's ideal: that the greatest good any warrior can do is to give himself entirely, in sworn brotherhood, to the service of love and justice.

### The Rescue and Its Cost

The assault on Kajeti is one of the poem's great action sequences. The three heroes, with Pridon's forces, storm the demon king's fortress in a night raid of staggering violence — Tariel in particular fighting with an abandon that clearly draws on suicidal courage, because a man who has nothing left to lose except his beloved is the most dangerous thing in any poem. The king of Kajeti is slain. Nestan-Darejan is found in her prison, her beauty undimmed — Rustaveli insists on this — but her spirit scarred by captivity and the knowledge that her letter to Tariel caused a war and cost her years. The reunion between Tariel and Nestan-Darejan is written with extraordinary restraint for a poem so given to extravagant feeling. They weep. They cannot speak. The poem allows silence to be the deepest language.

After the rescue, the poem moves to joyful resolutions: Tariel and Nestan-Darejan marry. Avtandil returns to Arabia and marries Tinatin. The doubled wedding is the poem's triumphant closing image — love that persisted across years of separation, war, wilderness exile, and prison finally fulfilled. But the poem does not end in pure celebration. Rostevan has aged. Tariel and Nestan-Darejan take the throne of India. There is a valediction quality to the ending, as though Rustaveli knows that the world the poem describes — of absolute loyalty, of love as a spiritual vocation, of friendship that overrides thrones — is a world that has to be actively maintained against entropy and time.

### The Human Condition Themes

The poem's deepest argument is about what love is. It is not decoration or reward. It is the engine of the heroic soul. Without Nestan-Darejan, Tariel cannot exist. Without Tinatin, Avtandil has no direction. But crucially, the women are not passive in this relationship: Tinatin gives Avtandil his quest, and Nestan-Darejan gave Tariel his wound. Both women exercise agency. Both women suffer. The poem's insistence on the spiritual equality of the sexes — expressed through Rostevan's voluntary abdication and the way Tinatin's orders are obeyed as absolute commands — is eight centuries ahead of many of the other texts in this library. Loyalty is the poem's blood. Every major choice the heroes make is a loyalty test. Grief, in this world, is not pathology — it is proof of love's reality. A man who does not grieve for what he has lost never truly loved it. And friendship, the sworn brotherhood of Tariel, Avtandil, and Pridon, is what makes the impossible rescue possible: not any single warrior's strength, but three warriors wholly committed to one another's cause.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Weeping Knight of the Mountain Road** — A lone warrior in black armour has been seen weeping at a crossroads for three days, turning away all who approach. The king's court needs the road cleared; no one knows if the knight is dangerous, grieving, or cursed, and the Fighter is sent to learn the truth without drawing steel first.

2. **The Letter That Started a War** — A princess, imprisoned in her own tower by a political marriage she did not consent to, passes a sealed letter to a smuggler asking an unknown champion to stop the wedding by any means necessary. The Fighter intercepts the letter and must decide: deliver it to its intended recipient and ignite a war, or suppress it and condemn the princess to a loveless captivity.

3. **Asmat's Vigil** — A lone woman has lived for two years at the edge of a haunted forest, bringing food and word of the outside world to a grief-mad warrior within. She asks the Fighter to help her carry a message deep into the forest — but the warrior who receives it may not be safe to approach.

4. **The Brotherhood Oath** — Three knights must swear sworn brotherhood before a rescue attempt on an island fortress controlled by a demon lord. The ritual requires each man to name the thing he would sacrifice for the others. The Fighter witnesses the oath — and must decide whether to add their own name to the covenant.

5. **Storm the Sea-Fort** — The demon king of Kajeti has taken a hostage whose identity can restore a shattered kingdom. The heroes need a ship captain, a night with no moon, and a Fighter who can hold a narrow gate while three warriors storm a tower. The plan has no exit strategy for anyone on the gate.

6. **The Panther's Skin** — A captured warrior refuses to name himself, refuses to eat, refuses to speak to anyone except to recite verses about a woman no one at court has ever heard of. The court physician says he will die within the month if no one can reach him. The Fighter is locked in a cell with him and told: make him want to live.

7. **Tinatin's Commission** — A queen sends her champion away on a quest that will test him to destruction — not because she does not love him, but because she does. The Fighter is assigned to escort the champion to the border. The queen's farewell is conducted in public with perfect composure. Only the Fighter sees, at the last moment, that her hands are shaking.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: Tinatin's Commission

*Drawn from Storyline 7: the moment Avtandil is sent away by the woman who loves him, and the Fighter who escorts him to the threshold of the unknown.*

---

### Act I — The Queen's Gift

*The Thing: a silk cord, deep red, wound around a carved bone cylinder no larger than a thumb — a message capsule, sealed with black wax pressed with the queen's signet. It is whole, sealed, warm from being held. Tinatin holds it. The throne room, late afternoon, the court assembled.*

Tinatin stands at the dais. The court has been told that the champion Avtandil will depart before dawn to seek a mysterious weeping knight reported three days' ride east. Her voice, when she speaks, is level as a ruled line.

"We charge our champion with this errand. He will find the knight. He will learn his name and his grief. He will return with what he learns."

She descends the three steps from her throne — a thing queens do not do, a thing that silences the room — and places the message capsule in Avtandil's palm. Her fingers do not linger. Her face does not change.

"If you are lost," she says, "the capsule will tell whoever finds you who sent you and why."

Avtandil closes his fist around it and bows.

*The Fighter, standing at the door-post with the escort detail, watches the queen walk back to her throne and thinks: she has just handed him her heart inside a case of bone, and smiled while doing it.*

---

### Act II — The Road's First Night

*The Thing: the message capsule, sealed and whole, on a cord around Avtandil's neck, resting against his breastbone beneath his mail. The road east, a stand of pines, firelight.*

Three days out. The escort has thinned to the Fighter and two outriders. Avtandil has barely spoken since departure — not from coldness, but from the particular silence of a man carrying something too large to set down. At the fire he opens his mail coat and takes out the capsule to look at it. He doesn't open it. He turns it in his fingers, tracing the sealed wax.

"You know what she said, before the formal speech?" he asks the fire, not any person in particular.

The Fighter says nothing. The outriders sleep.

"She said: come back. Not as a command. The way you say something when you know you cannot make it a command."

He tucks the capsule back inside his mail. He does not speak again.

*The Fighter listens to the pines and thinks: this is what it means to be sent away by someone who cannot stop you — they put themselves in your pocket and hope the weight of it turns you around.*

---

### Act III — The Ambush on the Shale Road

*The Thing: the message capsule, now hanging loose outside Avtandil's mail — he took it out to show proof of royal errand to a suspicious border warden, and has not put it back. The shale road east of the Alazani ford, midday, sudden armed men from both treelines.*

Eight men. Brigands or border soldiers — impossible to tell at distance, and the question is academic. Avtandil draws and the Fighter moves left to take the wider arc.

The fighting is brief and ugly. Avtandil takes a cut across the forearm — not deep, but bleeding freely. In the scramble, the capsule's cord snaps. It falls in the shale. The Fighter sees it fall, kills the last brigand, and retrieves it from the stones while Avtandil is binding his arm.

It is scratched now. The wax seal has a crack running through it, but holds. The Fighter hands it back without comment.

Avtandil stares at it for a long moment. "I thought it was gone." His voice is strange.

"It held," the Fighter says.

*The Fighter holds that cracked seal in memory and thinks: the seal breaking would not have changed her orders or his oath — but both of them looked at that crack the way you look at an omen.*

---

### Act IV — The Parting at the Border Stone

*The Thing: the capsule, cracked seal, back around Avtandil's neck on a new cord — the Fighter's own boot-lace, cut and re-threaded. The border marker, a standing stone carved with the kingdom's device, beyond which the escort's commission ends.*

The two outriders have turned back. It is just the Fighter and Avtandil at the stone. Beyond it, the road narrows into unknown terrain where a weeping stranger was last seen. The escort's commission is to the border. No farther.

Avtandil dismounts and looks east for a long time. Then he looks back west, in the direction of the court, which cannot be seen.

"Three years," he says. "If I am not back in three years, tell her—" He stops. He shakes his head. "No. Tell her nothing. She will know."

He takes the capsule from his neck and holds it out to the Fighter. "Take this back to her. I don't need it found on a dead man."

The Fighter does not reach for it.

"She gave it to you," the Fighter says. "Not to me."

Long silence. Avtandil puts the capsule back inside his mail. He mounts. He does not look back again.

*The Fighter watches him go and thinks: he turned down the one thing that could have brought him home early — and that is exactly what she knew he would do.*

---

### Act V — The Return and the Burning

*The Thing: the message capsule, cracked seal, worn and darkened by three years against a warrior's skin — held by Tinatin, who has just received it from Avtandil's hand. The throne room, morning. Avtandil has returned, alive, with his sworn brother Tariel and the woman Tariel spent three years dying for. The capsule is whole but changed: it smells of road and old grief and distance.*

Tinatin breaks the wax seal — the cracked one, the one the shale road split — and opens it herself. Inside is a scrap of silk on which, years ago, she wrote two words in ink that has not faded.

*Come back.*

She looks at Avtandil. He is alive. He has done what she asked — found the weeping knight, learned his name, and in the years between, helped restore him to his beloved. He has come back.

She folds the silk carefully. She holds it to the candle on her arm-rest and lets it burn — not in destruction but in completion, the way a vow is released once it is fulfilled. The small flame takes the silk in three seconds. She drops the ash and holds the empty bone cylinder in her palm.

"It worked," she says. Not to the court. To him.

Avtandil crosses the room without protocol and takes her hand — in front of everyone, in front of old Rostevan, in front of the court that has waited three years for this — and the bone cylinder falls from her open palm and rolls across the stone floor and is heard by everyone in the room and no one picks it up.

*The Fighter, standing at the door-post where they stood three years ago, looks at the empty capsule on the floor and thinks: she put herself in his pocket, and the weight of it brought him all the way home.*

## Cycle 2 — Angle: *The Panther's Skin — The Question That Makes a Man Choose to Stay*

**What this angle reveals that cycle 1 missed**: Cycle 1 showed love expressed through absence — the queen's composed sacrifice, the champion's willingness to go into the unknown carrying her heart in a bone case. Cycle 2 shows love expressed through total refusal — the warrior who has made his grief into an identity; who will not eat, will not name himself, will not speak except in verses about a woman no one else has heard of; for whom living has become the same as abandonment. The question the Fighter must find is not "do you want to live" — that question has been asked and answered with silence. The question is: *who will write her name when you are gone?*

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Panther's Skin*

#### The Tablet

*The Name-Tablet — a small cedar writing tablet, blank, physician's mark scratched in the corner — given to the Fighter before entering the cell; the physician's instruction: "Write down anything he says about her. If we have her name, we can search." The cedar is smooth and pale. It has never been written on.*

---

**ACT I**

*The Name-Tablet — blank — in the physician's study, the cell corridor beyond the door, morning.*

The physician's name is Maro. She is forty-five, has been court physician for twenty years, and has never lost a patient to silence before. She has tried food, water, medicines, three clergymen, two musicians, and the court jester. The warrior lies on the cell floor, clothed in a panther's skin that may have once been a distinction and is now simply what he wears. He has spoken since his capture only in verse — four lines repeated, then others; always the same woman; always the same cadences; always in a language the court does not fully understand.

"He will die within the month," Maro says. "Not from starvation, precisely. From refusing to be in the world." She hands the Fighter the cedar tablet. "Write down anything he says about her. If we have her name, we can search." She pauses. "That is not, however, why I am asking you to go in. I am asking you to go in because I have run out of things to try."

*— She is not asking for a method. She is asking for someone who will not leave until the right question is found.*

> **Dialogue DC 12.** Tell Maro what the difference is between making someone want to live and finding the single remaining obligation that makes staying possible — that the question is not "do you want to live" but "who will do the one thing you have not yet done." She needs to know the Fighter understands this before sending them in.

**Fail:** Maro studies the Fighter for a long moment. "You're going to tell him to think of people who love him." She is not unkind. "Come back at the afternoon bell with a better answer. Think about it."

**Pass:** Maro hands over the tablet without another word. She adds one thing: "His verse changes at some point. There is a line about a woman who cannot be found. That is the line to follow." She opens the cell door.

**TOKEN GRANTED: The Name-Tablet** — blank cedar tablet, physician's mark, intended for the one remaining act of naming.

---

**ACT II**

*The Name-Tablet — blank — in the cell, the warrior sitting against the far wall, the panther's skin around his shoulders.*

The warrior is tall, clearly powerful even in his state, and he is reciting. Not loudly. He is reciting the way a man prays when he has stopped expecting an answer: from habit, from necessity, from the feeling that stopping would be the end of the last thread. He does not look at the Fighter when the door opens. He has been not-looking at people for weeks.

The Fighter sits down opposite him. Does not speak first. Listens.

The verse is about a woman who is the sun. Specifically: a woman whose face, when she turns it away, makes the world go dark in the middle of the day. He says it three times. Then he says a different verse: about a woman who gave him a letter that he killed a man over. He says this one only once.

*— He has stopped. He is still looking at the wall. He spoke the letter verse only once. That means it costs him more.*

> **Dialogue DC 12.** Ask about the letter — specifically, whether the letter still exists, and whether anyone who loved her would want to know what it said. Not: "tell me about her." Not: "she would want you to live." Ask about the letter. Ask what happens to the letter's words when he is gone.

**Fail:** He turns his face to the wall. The question was too direct. Try again — different approach; the Fighter must find the question he will answer before the approach is lost.

**Pass:** He does not answer immediately. He turns his head slightly — not toward the Fighter, but away from the wall. After a long silence: "The letter exists." He says nothing more. But he has stopped reciting.

**TOKEN STATE 2**: The Fighter writes "the letter exists" on the cedar tablet. The blank is no longer blank. A single fact.

---

**ACT III**

*The Name-Tablet — one line written — in the cell, the warrior looking at the Fighter for the first time.*

The warrior is looking at the tablet.

He did not expect the Fighter to write down what he said. He has been speaking to people for weeks and no one wrote anything down. He looks at the three words and something changes in his face — not softening, exactly. More like: recognition that the Fighter is doing something different from what everyone else has done.

"You wrote it down."

"Yes."

"Why?"

*— He wants to know if the Fighter is writing down evidence, or writing down witness. These are different things.*

> **Dialogue DC 13.** Tell him why — not: "so we can search for her." Tell him the real reason: because a man about to die has one remaining act available that no one can take from him, which is to put her name in a record that will exist after he is gone; and that record begins with what the Fighter writes, but it is completed by what he writes. He is the only one who knows her name. If he dies without writing it, the name dies with him.

**Fail:** He goes still. The answer sounded like leverage. He is not wrong — it is leverage. Try again with fewer words. The argument is true; the delivery was wrong.

**Pass:** He is quiet for a long time. Then he extends his hand and takes the tablet. He reads the three words the Fighter wrote. He turns the tablet over to the blank side and holds the stylus for a long time without moving.

He writes a name.

He hands the tablet back.

**TOKEN STATE 3**: Her name is written on the tablet in the warrior's own hand. The tablet now carries both the Fighter's witness and the warrior's own act. It is no longer the physician's document. It is shared.

---

**ACT IV**

*The Name-Tablet — two inscriptions — in the cell, voices in the corridor.*

Two guards are outside the cell door. The court captain's voice is audible through the planks: "...physician's assessment is terminal; no point delaying; take him to the lower level before the morning count." They have decided he is going to die anyway and they want the cell back.

*— If they move him now, the last conversation is over.*

> **Combat: 2 guards — AC 12, HP 20 each.** They open the door ready to use force. They will stop if reduced to half health or given a reason to wait; they are not committed to cruelty, only to efficiency.

**Fail:** The guards take the warrior. He goes without resisting. The Fighter follows to the lower level and must continue there; the conversation can still be completed, but the context is harsher.

**Pass:** Both guards down or persuaded to wait. The door holds. The cell is quiet again.

The warrior has watched the fight without moving. When it is over, he says: "You could have let them take me."

"Yes."

A long pause. He picks up the tablet again. He writes something underneath her name. A place. A direction. Something specific.

**TOKEN STATE 4**: A place-name written on the tablet beneath her name — a direction, a last-known location. The tablet now holds: one fact (the letter exists), her name, a direction. It is a document.

---

**ACT V**

*The Name-Tablet — three inscriptions — in the cell, early afternoon.*

The warrior has been looking at the tablet for a long time. He is not reciting anymore.

"If I write my name," he says at last, "it means I am agreeing to be found. To be known. To be someone she can ask for when she is free." He looks up at the Fighter. "It means I believe she will be free."

*— He is asking the Fighter to confirm that this belief is not absurd. He does not need hope. He needs the Fighter to have looked at the situation and found the belief reasonable.*

> **Dialogue DC 11.** Not: "she will be found." Not: "have faith." Tell him what the Fighter actually believes: that the woman who sent a letter commanding a war to be stopped rather than endure a marriage she did not choose is not the kind of woman who stays captive if there is any way to refuse; that the act of writing his name is not hope, it is the single remaining obligation — to be findable when she is ready to find him.

**Fail:** He sets the tablet down without writing. The argument needs one more thing. Try: name the one thing the Fighter has seen in the past hours that suggests she would act. He has been telling the Fighter about her. The Fighter should know.

**Pass:** He takes the tablet. He writes his name.

He hands it to the Fighter.

"Take it to the physician. Tell her I will eat tomorrow."

**TOKEN TAKEN: The Name-Tablet**

The cedar tablet leaves the cell in the Fighter's hands. On it: one fact in the Fighter's hand; her name in the warrior's hand; a place-name in the warrior's hand; his own name in the warrior's hand. The physician receives it in the corridor. She reads it without speaking. She goes to order food.
