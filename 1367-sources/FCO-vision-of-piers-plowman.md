## Cycle 1 Header

**Angle:** The Pardon That Costs Nothing

Langland wrote this poem while the Black Death was still fresh in England's memory, the church was visibly corrupt, the labor laws were brutal, and every institution of mercy had a price. He gave the world a plowman — not a king, not a saint, not a scholar — as the figure of Truth. That plowman's pardon, when finally opened, contains two lines: do well and have well. Every bishop's bull, every friar's indulgence, every papal certificate is worth less. The poem's energy is fury at what mercy has become — and wonder that the real thing is still available, free, to anyone who will do the work.

## Pass 1 — Full Telling

*Finished the poem last night and cannot shake it. Here is the whole thing, spoilers and all.*

William Langland wrote Piers Ploughman three times. We have the B-text here, the expanded version, composed around 1377 — though the poem's internal references point to events as early as 1362 (the great windstorm) and as late as the Peasants' Revolt of 1381. He wrote it near Malvern Hills, in Worcestershire, probably as a minor cleric — educated enough for Latin, poor enough to know hunger. He had witnessed two waves of the Black Death. He had watched the church collect indulgences for sins while plague-priests abandoned their parishes. He had seen the Statute of Laborers try to hold wages down while half the peasantry was dead. He was, in short, furious on behalf of everyone who was not powerful.

The poem opens in May. The narrator — Will — falls asleep on Malvern Hills beside a brook and dreams. He sees a vast field of folk between two landmarks: a tower on a hill (Truth) and a dungeon in a dale (Care, dwelling of Wrong). The field is England: plowmen working, merchants chaffering, pilgrims lying, hermits wandering, friars preaching for profit, pardoners selling fake indulgences, lawyers silent until shown money. The whole social world in two hundred lines, satirized with the precision of someone who has lived inside it.

Lady Holy Church descends from the tower and explains the vision to the dreamer. The tower is Truth; the dungeon is Wrong; and the one thing that matters, above all treasure, is Truth: *Whan alle tresors arn tried, Truthe is the beste.* She leaves when the dreamer asks how to know the false.

He looks left and sees Lady Mede — gorgeous, jeweled in rubies and sapphires, dressed in scarlet, desired by everyone. She is not simply bribery. She is the corrupt version of reward: not what you earn but what you can buy. She is being married to Falsehood, with Simony and Civil Law as notaries. Theology objects and sends the case to London. The king takes Mede into custody. He proposes to marry her to Conscience. Conscience refuses: she is "tikel of hire tail, tale-wis of hire tonge, / As commune as a cartwey to ech a knave." The king summons Reason. Reason arrives and preaches hard: no mercy for Wrong until every layer of society reforms — the lords, the clergy, the merchants, the prelates. The king resolves to be guided by Reason and Conscience alone.

Then the great confession sequence, Passus V. Reason preaches to the Field of Folk. Repentance takes over. Each of the Seven Deadly Sins steps forward:

**Pride** (Pernele Proud-herte) falls flat on the earth and promises to wear a hair shirt. Easy enough to confess when someone else makes you ashamed.

**Lechery** promises to drink only with a duck for seven years — fasting for lust, which says something about how poorly he understands either.

**Envy** is the most frightening: pale, blistered, body swollen with spite. He cannot eat for envy of his neighbors. He greets the man he hates most in the street with great courtesy because the man is stronger than he is. In church he prays that his enemies get the plague. He lives "lik a luther dogge," his gall poisoning everything. He says he is sorry, but what he means is he cannot get revenge.

**Wrath** was once a cook in a nunnery — he made soups of scandal, and the nuns came to blows. Now he walks with the friars and the possessioner-priests, whispering his books of grievances into their sermons. He is happiest when both sides hate each other equally.

**Covetousness** is the masterpiece of the sequence. Sire Hervy, brow beetled, lips blobbed, cheeks like a leather purse, louse walking on his threadbare tabard. He learned to cheat at cloth — stretching the selvedge, packing pressed cloths, getting thirteen yards out of ten. His wife Rose the Regrater brewed pint-ale and pudding-ale together and sold it to laborers at a groat a gallon. He lent money to Lombards, clipped coins, laundered gold through Rome, speculated in credit — and considers this a normal life. He tells Repentance that the only time he made restitution was when he rifled the saddle-bags of sleeping merchants. Repentance tells him flatly: that was robbery, not restitution; you'd have been better hanged. He breaks down and nearly falls into despair until Repentance pulls him back with the reminder that God's mercy is greater than all wickedness as a coal-spark in the sea.

**Gluttony** is the comic pinnacle of the poem. He sets out for church to confess — genuinely, or so he says — but Beton the brewer intercepts him: "I have good ale, Gloton, woltow assaye?" He goes in. The tavern is Langland's entire England in miniature: Clement the cobbler, Hikke the hackney-man, Tymme the tinker, Clarice of Cocklane, Dawe the diker, Griffyn the Welshman, Rose the dishwasher, Godfrey of Garlickhithe. They trade Clement's cloak for Hikke's hood, Robyn the roper arbitrating. They drink from morning until evening-song. Gluttony drinks a gallon and a gill. His guts rumble like two greedy sows. He pisses a pint during a paternoster. He blows his wind at the bottom of his spine — and all the company holds their noses. He falls at the threshold; Clement catches him mid-vomit and receives the full reward in his lap. His wife and his wench drag him home to bed. He sleeps Saturday and Sunday until sunset. His first words on waking: "Where is the bowl?" His wife shames him. Repentance rebukes. His confession is magnificent in its specificity: swearing God's soul nine hundred times; eating before fasting days were done; sitting so long in the tavern he ate and slept at once.

**Sloth** nearly falls asleep during his own confession. He is a priest of thirty years who cannot sing or read saints' lives but knows all the Robin Hood ballads by heart. He made forty vows and forgot them by morning. He has lain abed in Lent with his mistress until matins were past, then gone to the friars instead. When Vigilate throws water in his face to wake him, he vows to attend mass every day for seven years — if sickness prevents him, that is another matter.

Then Hope and Repentance send the whole multitude on a pilgrimage to find Truth. A palmer is consulted — he has been everywhere, to every shrine — but he has never heard of Truth. Piers the Plowman steps forward. He knows the way. He has plowed for Truth for forty years. Truth is in the heart; the way there goes through the Ten Commandments; but first, he must finish plowing his half-acre.

Passus VI: the half-acre. Knights, wastrels, ladies embroidering, beggars, the whole spectrum of medieval labor. The knight tries to control Waster by appeal to chivalric courtesy; Waster refuses. Piers calls Hunger, who beats Waster senseless and sets the entire able-bodied community to work. But when Hunger withdraws, the wastrels return — they will not be disciplined by anything but necessity.

Passus VII: Truth sends Piers a pardon that covers all who help honestly in the work of the world. Kings, knights, bishops (if they do their duty), merchants (with conditions), lawyers (very few), laborers (yes, if they work truly), beggars only if genuinely unable. A priest reads over Piers's shoulder and says: this is no pardon at all — it is two lines: *Et qui bona egerunt, ibunt in vitam eternam. Qui vero mala, in ignem eternum.* Do good, go to heaven; do evil, go to hell. There is no papal formula, no indulgence, no certificate. Piers tears the pardon in rage — not at Truth, but at the priest's smug dismissal — and vows to leave plowing for prayer. The dreamer wakes on Malvern Hills, meditating on what good works actually are and whether any pardon from the pope can match them.

The second half of the poem becomes the search for Do-Well, Do-Better, Do-Best — three virtues that keep receding. Thought explains them as stages of ethical life; Wit as the castle where the soul is guarded; Study as the repository of knowledge too often wasted on the unworthy; Clergy as institutional learning. In a long vision, Nature shows the dreamer all creation — every creature following Reason except Man. The dreamer challenges Reason; Reason rebukes him sharply: who are you to correct me? Imaginative appears and teaches him humility.

In Passus XIII: Conscience invites the dreamer to dine with Clergy. A fat Doctor of Divinity eats all the rich food while preaching penury — Patience and the dreamer are seated at a side table with sour bread and water. When the doctor is finished eating, Conscience asks him what Do-Well is. He answers glibly, reaches for the wine cup. The dreamer is furious. Conscience and Patience leave the feast together on pilgrimage, which is the poem's moral pivot: institutional learning is not enough; you must walk out into the world with patience as your only provision.

They meet Haukyn the Active Man — a wafer-seller, a minstrel, a man who feeds laborers and beggars but whose coat of Christianity is stained with every sin visible. Not great sins: small ones. Pride in appearance. Envy of neighbors. Backbiting and gossip. Sloth in prayer. Covetousness creeping through every transaction. Patience and Conscience, together, try to teach Haukyn to clean his coat — through poverty, patience, and honest labor. The volume ends with Haukyn's coat still spotted and his repentance still incomplete.

Volume 2 (not read in full but known from Wright's summary): Soul explains clergy corruption. Piers appears again, tending the Tree of Charity, which is propped up by the three persons of the Trinity. The fruit falls — and from it, the Incarnation begins. The dreamer watches the Nativity, the Passion, the Harrowing of Hell — where Christ in the armor of Piers the Plowman smashes the gates of Hell and frees the patriarchs. The Church is founded. Grace distributes gifts. Pride and Antichrist attack the Castle of Unity. Conscience calls for aid. The friars infiltrate in the guise of a physician. Unity falls. Conscience sets out again to find Piers the Plowman — and the poem ends without resolution, mid-search.

**What it argues:** Langland believes mercy and salvation are real, and free, and available — but everything between the individual soul and that mercy has been corrupted, bought, sold, or fallen asleep in the tavern. The poem's rage is inseparable from its hope. The one figure who cannot be bought — Piers — is a plowman. Not a pope. Not a friar. Not a king. A plowman who knows the road because he has walked it forty years.

### Chapter Notes

**Prologue:** The field full of folk. Cat and mice fable (the parliament of rats who will bell no cat). Every social order satirized in a hundred lines. England as a dirty dream.

**Passus I:** Holy Church descends and teaches: Truth is the best treasure; love is the remedy; the tower is where you must aim, the dungeon is where Wrong lives. She leaves before the dreamer can ask how to navigate between them.

**Passus II:** Lady Mede betrothed to Falsehood. The marriage charter grants them all the earldoms of Envy, Wrath, Covetousness, Lechery, Gluttony, Sloth. Theology objects; the case goes to London.

**Passus III:** Mede at Westminster, bribing everyone. The friar absolves her of fifty years of adultery for a stained glass window. Conscience refuses to marry her and enumerates her crimes: she corrupts judges, lets prisoners free for gold and hangs the innocent, is pryvee with the pope. The king orders them to be reconciled; Conscience refuses without Reason.

**Passus IV:** Reason arrives. Peace complains against Wrong (rape, theft, battery). Mede and Wisdom try to bribe Wrong's way out. The king refuses: no mercy without Reason. Reason and Conscience will govern the realm.

**Passus V:** Seven Deadly Sins confess. The tavern sequence (Gluttony). Hope sends the crowd on pilgrimage; a palmer who has been everywhere cannot find Truth.

**Passus VI:** Piers the Plowman's half-acre. The social contract in miniature: plowman plows, knight protects, lady sews, beggars beg, wastrels waste. Hunger called in as the only effective enforcement.

**Passus VII:** Truth's pardon: two lines. Priest challenges it. Piers tears it. Dreamer wakes pondering what do-well actually is.

**Passus VIII:** The friars' confusing answer about Do-Well. Thought appears and explains Do-Well/Do-Better/Do-Best. Three days' walking in dialogue.

**Passus IX:** Wit's Castle of Kind: the human body/soul, guarded by Inwit and his five sons (the senses). Cain's line and the corruption of wedlock.

**Passus X:** Study complains that wisdom is cast before swine. Clergy's long lecture on corruption and a prophecy of monastic dissolution. Scripture rebukes the dreamer for presumption.

**Passus XI:** Fortune's seductions. Nature shows the dreamer all creation from a mountain. The dreamer challenges Reason; Reason rebukes. Imaginative appears to correct.

**Passus XII:** Imaginative's exhortation: clergie vs. kynde wit; the adulteress saved by Christ's writing in the dust; the swimmer and the non-swimmer analogy.

**Passus XIII:** The feast at Clergy's house. The fat Doctor who eats well and preaches penury. Conscience and Patience leave together; they meet Haukyn the wafer-seller.

**Passus XIV:** Haukyn's coat of Christianity, spotted with every small sin. Patience and Conscience teach patient poverty. The volume ends here, Haukyn still unclean.

## Pass 2 — Slower

### Will the Dreamer
Will is the most self-aware narrator in Middle English literature. He knows he is writing a poem and therefore not living it. He is educated enough to argue with Scripture, humble enough to be ashamed of himself for doing so. He is lazy (his own confession), curious past the point of wisdom, and driven by something he cannot name. Every authoritative figure he meets tells him to stop asking questions and do the work — and he keeps asking. He is us: the person who understands more than they live by, and is ashamed of the gap.

### Lady Mede
Mede is not simply corruption. She is the beautiful, available, plausible version of everything good — reward, gratitude, generosity — turned to the service of power. She argues, not without logic, that all commerce involves mede: the laborer takes his hire, the priest his mass-penny, the king his tribute. Conscience's counterargument — that there are two kinds of mede, the earned and the corrupt — is theologically correct but politically useless. The brilliant thing Langland does: Mede is neither villain nor fool. She is simply what the world runs on when Truth is too expensive.

### Conscience
Conscience is the poem's most active moral figure — he is in every major dispute, riding, arguing, refusing. He refuses to marry Mede ("Crist it me forbede!"). He refuses to kiss her. He leaves the comfortable feast to walk with Patience through the hostile world. At the poem's end, he leaves the fallen Unity to search again for Piers. His most important quality is that he moves: he cannot be bought, but he can be outmaneuvered, and when the castle falls he does not despair — he walks out. He is loyalty to an idea expressed as perpetual motion.

### Piers the Plowman
Piers appears rarely but each appearance changes everything. He is not allegorical in the usual way — he is too specific, too earthy, too angry. He tears the pardon in a genuine fit of rage at the priest's smugness. He has worked for Truth forty years and knows the road through the body rather than the intellect. By the poem's later passus, he becomes identified with Christ himself — Christ puts on Piers's coat to enter Jerusalem, so that the devil won't recognize God coming. Langland's extraordinary move: the type of Christ is not a king or a saint but an English plowman with mud on his boots.

### The Seven Sins
As characters, the sins are the most alive things in the poem. Envy (pale, swollen with bile, greets his enemies with courtesy because they are stronger) is a type found on every merchant street in any century. Covetousness (Rose the Regrater, the Lombard credit lines, the confession that rippling becomes theft) is a portrait of the medieval small businessman of extraordinary accuracy. Gluttony is the poem's great comic set piece — not evil exactly, just utterly unable to govern himself, and endearing for it. Their confessions are not redemptive. Pride will be proud tomorrow. Sloth will sleep through matins again. The poem knows this.

### Haukyn (Activa-vita)
The wafer-seller is the poem's ordinary man: not great enough to sin grandly, not innocent enough to be guiltless. His stained coat is every Christian's actual state — not damned, not saved, just spotted. Conscience and Patience try to teach him that patient poverty is the only real cleaning. Haukyn's final lament is the most moving moment in the volume: a man who has worked all his life, fed laborers and beggars, never been given a fair reward by pope or lord, and yet whose soul is marked by ten thousand small betrayals of the truth he knows.

### Patience
Patience is the counter-figure to every institutional solution: not Clergy, not Reason, not Law, but the figure who carries in his "poke" only Sobriety, Simple Speech, and True Faith — and who says these are sufficient for any journey, any danger, any enemy. The doctor at the feast dismisses him as a fool. He is not a fool.

## Pass 3 — The Scene: Gluttony at the Tavern (Passus V)

Gluttony has made his decision. He is going to mass, and then he is going to confession. It is morning; the street is cold; he is already rehearsing his contrition. He is a large man and he does not walk so much as fill the street.

Beton the brewster stands in her doorway. She knows him. "I have good ale, Gloton. Woltow assaye?" She produces the bait: pepper and peonies, a pound of garlic, fennel seed for fasting days.

He goes in.

The room is already full. Clement the cobbler has thrown his cloak on the table and is trying to trade it for Hikke the hackney-man's hood. Tymme the tinker is there with two of his apprentices. Dawe the diker. Clarice of Cocklane — a woman of the street — and the clerk of the church beside her. Godfrey of Garlickhithe, who smells like his trade. Griffyn the Welshman. Rose the dishwasher. A ribibe-player, a rat-catcher, a street-sweeper. It is London before nine in the morning.

They settle Clement's cloak. Hikke gets the hood; Clement gets the cup; Robyn the roper arbitrates because the deal is not quite honest but close enough. The cup goes around. Then goes around again. They play games. They make bets. They sing. They argue. They drink from when Beton opened her door until the evening bell rang for vespers.

Gluttony drinks a gallon and a gill.

He is enormous at the best of times. Drunk, he is geological. His guts begin to make sounds like two greedy sows settling in mud. Then the catastrophe of the paternoster — he pisses a pint in the time it takes to say one Our Father. He turns and offers his rear to the room as an instrument of wind. All who heard that horn wished it had been stoppered with furze.

He moves toward the door. His legs have been replaced with something unreliable. He staggers left, right, like a man laying snare-lines in a field. At the threshold he stumbles. The threshold wins. He goes down.

Clement catches him mid-fall but Clement is a cobbler, not a wrestling man, and Gluttony is enormous, and the physics of what happens next are regrettable for everyone in range. Clement receives the full account of Gluttony's day across his lap. There is no dog in Hertfordshire hungry enough to lap what Clement is wearing now.

Gluttony's wife and his servant girl drag him home by his arms and get him to bed. He sleeps through all of Saturday. He sleeps through Sunday until sunset. He is snoring when the church bells ring for vespers. He is snoring when they ring for compline.

When he wakes, his first thought, his first word, his first conscious act — is to ask where the bowl is.

His wife does not answer that question. She has other things to say. Repentance is there too, and says them louder. Gluttony's confession pours out then: he has sworn God's soul nine hundred times (nine hundred is not a round number; this is a specific count); he has eaten past the hour on fasting days; he has sat so long at the ale that he fell asleep eating; he has drunk for love of stories and company and drunk more when men fed him tales.

"This shewing shrift," Repentance tells him, "shall be merit to thee."

And Gluttony cries — genuinely, for a moment — for the wasted life. He vows: no fish on Friday until Abstinence gives him leave. Even from his aunt he has hidden all his life.

*This is the Fighter's vantage point: a corner table, a cup of ale, untouched. They watch the whole arc: the morning decision, the first cup, the long afternoon's slide, the threshold fall, the snoring, the waking question. They do not intervene. Their job is to remember — because what they carry tonight was entrusted to them by someone who believes there is a mercy that is not for sale, and the world they move through is composed of men in exactly Gluttony's condition: full of good intentions and better ale.*

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Pardon of Piers** — A humble clerk hands the Fighter a sealed pardon from Truth's own agent — plain parchment, wax seal, no gold. Every corrupt gatekeeper along the route to the prisoner it is meant for tries to confiscate, replace, or invalidate it. The document cannot be bought because it was given free; every substitution is recognizable because the real thing is plain.

2. **The Half-Acre Guard** — Piers is plowing while a hundred pilgrims wait. The Fighter is hired to stand between Waster's gang and the grain. Waster won't be stopped by appeal to duty; only Hunger works. The skill check is knowing when to call Hunger in and when to send him away before he kills the workers Piers needs.

3. **Mede's Charter** — The Fighter is unknowingly hired to carry a false marriage contract from Falsehood's notaries to Westminster — a document that would bind justice to corruption for a generation. Conscience intercepts them at the city gate and the Fighter must decide whose side they are actually on.

4. **Conscience's Walk** — After leaving the feast, Conscience and Patience need an armed escort through a city where Mede's agents control every checkpoint. The Fighter must get them from the archive district to the open road without being stopped, bribed, or arrested on a warrant that Simony manufactured that afternoon.

5. **The Fragments of the Torn Pardon** — Piers tore his pardon in rage and the pieces blew across the field. A scholar sent to collect them for study hires the Fighter. Each fragment is held by a different person who claims it proves their own righteousness. The Fighter must carry all the pieces to the scholar before Covetousness buys the last one.

6. **The Cat's Bell** — The Parliament of Rats agreed unanimously to hang a bell around the cat's neck; unanimously, no rat will carry it. The Fighter is not a rat. They are hired to do what no one else will. The question is not whether they can reach the cat — it is what the cat will do when a human, not a rat, appears.

7. **Haukyn's Night** — Haukyn's spotted coat has become visible to every official in the city at once — a miracle of shame. He has one night to reach Patience before the commissioners of conscience find him and strip it. The Fighter runs escort through a city that now reads his sins on the outside of his clothes.

**New theme chosen:** *Corruption wears the face of mercy; the genuine article has no price.*

No existing quest involves the Fighter navigating institutional corruption — courts, archives, licenses, indulgences — where every official checkpoint is owned by the enemy, and the token they carry is precisely what the system cannot buy because it was given free. The Pardon of Piers inverts SEN (carrier becomes hunted) and MAN (healing through hostile law): here, the token is not dangerous because of what it conceals but because of what it lacks — it has no gold, no seal of power, no credential that the corrupt world recognizes. Its authority is invisible to Mede. That invisibility is the quest's engine.

## 5-Act Vignette Play — "The Pardon of Piers"

*French noir register. The Fighter witnesses. The token is the subject.*

**Location:** Weimar (WM) — the archive-haunted city, where Archivus Sweelinck controls the registration of all documents. The Great Hall of Licenses, the Register of Indulgences, the Lady's Court of Exemptions, the city gaol. A city in which every document of mercy has a price and every price is recorded.

**Token:** The Pardon of Piers — a rectangle of plain parchment, rolled and sealed with grey wax and a thumbprint. No episcopal flourish. No gilded ribbon. Inside: two lines of Latin. The sealer's name is not on it. The recipient's name is written in a plowman's hand, barely legible. Every corrupt gatekeeper will claim it is a forgery, because it looks like one.

---

### Act 1 — The Scholars' Ward, Before Dawn

The room is cold. Perkin sits at a table with a single candle — an old man, a minor clerk, ink-stained, shaking slightly. He has been waiting since midnight. He spent three days walking to Weimar from a farm.

He explains without theatre: there is a man named Perkyn in the city gaol, condemned to hang in two days for a debt he did not incur. The pardon is real — it is from Truth's own agent, carried by a chain of honest men. It is all that can save Perkyn. Perkin cannot deliver it himself; he has no entry to the archive district without a license he cannot afford.

He hands the Fighter the parchment. It is lighter than it should be. The wax is plain grey. There is no mark of authority a gatekeeper would recognize.

"The gates will offer to inspect it," Perkin says. "Let no one break the seal."

**TOKEN ACTION:** ACCEPT

**Mission bit:** onPass → grant `pardon_of_piers`
*The Fighter takes the pardon. It weighs nothing. That will be its first problem.*

### Act 2 — The Gate of Licenses (WM/CI checkpoint)

Dawn. A gate in the archive district wall. The Gate of Licenses requires that all documents passing into the registration zone be logged, stamped, and verified. The commissary on duty is a man named Favel — smooth voice, heavy rings, eyes that assess everything for its weight in coin.

He examines the pardon without touching it. He says the seal is irregular. He says the format is non-standard. He produces, from a drawer, a certified pardon-form — gilded edge, four episcopal stamps, a ribbon of white and gold — and explains that this is the correct format for a document of mercy in Weimar, and that for a modest verification fee, the Fighter's document can be transferred into the proper form.

The grey seal will have to be broken. The two lines will be transcribed — officially. Favel will do the transcribing himself.

"No," the Fighter says. Or does not say — but conveys.

The Gate of Licenses has a secondary mechanism: the guardroom door. If the Fighter cannot pass through official channels, they must find the unofficial one.

**TOKEN ACTION:** CARRY — pardon intact

**Skill check:** INT or CHA to navigate past Favel without the seal broken. Failure: delayed one hour, watched, exit only through the alley route (which costs health and reveals the Fighter's destination to Favel's informant).

**Mission bit:** check `pardon_of_piers` — if missing, quest has already failed and this scene does not occur.

---

### Act 3 — The Register Hall (WM Archive)

The archive district. The Register Hall is where all documents in Weimar are authenticated. Archivus Sweelinck's clerks process two hundred documents a day. One of them — a Brother Cyvyle, who manages the Indulgence Registry — intercepts the Fighter in the corridor.

Cyvyle is not hostile. He is smooth, scholarly, genuinely helpful in tone. He has heard that someone is carrying an unregistered document through the quarter. He offers to register it — which would give it official standing, a wax sigil of the archive, recognition by every gatekeeper in the city.

The registration fee is standard. The process takes one hour. The document will be authenticated.

What he does not say, but the Fighter can determine with a moment's attention: authentication in Weimar requires the document's contents to be disclosed, copied into the register, and evaluated for theological conformity. A pardon that says only "do well and have well" — that contains no pope's name, no bishop's seal, no fee schedule — will be ruled invalid by the registry's standards. It will be confiscated as a counterfeit.

Cyvyle's offer is genuine. His offer is also a trap that he does not know is a trap.

**TOKEN ACTION:** CARRY — pardon intact

**Skill check:** WIS to recognize the danger before agreeing. Failure: the Fighter agrees, loses one hour, and must escape the archive before the registration process extracts the document — a DEX check in the corridor, guards alerted.

**Mission bit:** check `pardon_of_piers`

---

### Act 4 — The Lady's Court of Exemptions (WM/SQ — Scholars' Quarter)

Midmorning. The Lady's Court is a receiving room in the Scholars' Quarter sub-district — officially for exemptions from standard registration requirements. In practice, it is Lady Mede's regional office. The agent here is called Dame Giftes; she is not the Lady herself but she has her ring.

Dame Giftes knows about the pardon. She has been informed. She produces a competing document: a beautifully executed pardon for one Perkyn — the correct name, the correct crime, the correct gaol — bearing the stamps of three bishops and the seal of the regional ecclesiastical court. She says the Fighter's document is a forgery; this is the real pardon. She is, she explains, trying to help.

She will trade. She takes the grey-sealed parchment; the Fighter takes the gilded pardon; Perkyn goes free.

The gilded pardon costs nothing to the Fighter. It costs everything to whoever obtained it. Perkyn will be released — but released into debt, under obligation to Dame Giftes's network for the rest of his life. The mercy has a price, and the price is a leash.

Combat or skill: Dame Giftes has hired muscle in the anteroom. The Fighter can fight their way out or negotiate — but they cannot simply walk away; she will send word to the gaol that the plain document is a forgery, and Perkyn will be hanged on the basis of that word.

**TOKEN ACTION:** CARRY — pardon intact (or combat to keep it)

**Skill check:** STR (combat) or CHA (expose the leash, shame Dame Giftes's clerk into letting them pass). Failure: the pardon is held; Fighter must retrieve it from the anteroom by force before Dame Giftes seals the gaol order.

### Act 5 — The Gaol Gate, High Noon

Two days before Perkyn's hanging. The gaol gate is simple: one guard, one lock, one prisoner. The corruption has been upstream; here there is just the bureaucratic fact of a condemned man and an open window in the delivery schedule.

The Fighter hands the pardon through the bars.

Perkyn is a small man, middle-aged, debt-lined. He holds the grey parchment for a long time. His hands shake when he breaks the seal.

He reads the two lines. He reads them again.

*Et qui bona egerunt, ibunt in vitam eternam.*  
*Qui vero mala, in ignem eternum.*

He laughs. Not bitterly — genuinely. "It says to do well," he tells the Fighter. "I did not know it would be this short."

The warden comes. The pardon is presented. It has no episcopal stamp. It has no registry seal. The warden looks at it for a long time. He knows, as Perkyn knows, that the document carries a certain authority that is not written in the wax — the authority of having been brought here by someone who fought through every gate that Mede owns to deliver it.

He unlocks the cell.

## Cycle 2: The Fragments of the Torn Pardon

### Pass 1 — Plot Précis (The Fragments of the Torn Pardon)

Piers tore his pardon in rage when the priest mocked it — "Do well, and have well, and God shall have thy soul; Do evil, and have evil, and expect none other" — and the pieces scattered across the field. Each piece blew to a different person in the allegorical city, and each person has decided their fragment vindicates their interpretation of righteousness. A scholar has been commissioned to reassemble the pardon before Covetousness can buy the last piece and ensure no complete version can exist. He cannot collect it himself — every fragment-holder will debate a clerk but not resist one. He needs a carrier who will take what is given without argument, and argue when argument is unavoidable, and carry the first piece as a reference standard to authenticate the rest.

### Pass 2 — Fighter's Role (The Fragments of the Torn Pardon)

The scholar gives the Fighter the first fragment — recovered from a widow at the field's edge who held it reverently because she recognized the seal. The Fighter carries it as the reference piece and retrieves the other three fragments from a Friar (who claims his piece excuses friars from penance), a Pardoner (who claims his piece validates his own indulgences), and a merchant's steward (who claims his piece covers his master's usury through charitable donation). The final fragment has been purchased by Covetousness's agent and must be taken by force before it reaches a buyer who will seal it away permanently. All pieces go to the scholar before the day ends.

### Pass 3 — What This Angle Reveals That Cycle 1 Missed

Cycle 1 was the genuine pardon moving through corrupt gates — the plain document resisting purchase because it was given free and costs nothing and says only "do well." *The Fragments of the Torn Pardon* shows what happens when the genuine document is scattered: each fragment is claimed by someone who reads their own righteousness into the piece they're holding, and they are not entirely wrong — each piece contains something true, and each holder emphasizes the part that flatters them. What this angle reveals: the pardon's power requires wholeness. A fragment of "do well, and have well" only tells half the structure; a fragment of "do evil, and have evil" becomes an instrument of despair without the promise attached. Piers tore the pardon in fury at the priest who could not read what mattered. The fragments scattered into exactly the hands that would mis-read them. The Fighter's job is not to correct the misreaders — it is to complete the assembly so the pardon can say the whole thing.

*Storyline 5 — Retrieval and Assembly. Piers's torn pardon has scattered to four holders in the allegorical city. TOKEN: the First Fragment — the portion reading "Do well, and have well, and God shall have thy soul" in full — received from a widow in Act I, carried as the reference standard through Acts II–IV, assembled with the other pieces and delivered to the scholar in Act V.*

#### ACT I — *The Field's Edge*
*The first fragment: in the widow's keeping, not yet the Fighter's. Location: MGF — Malvern field edge, dawn.*

The field where Piers plowed is empty now. The pilgrims have gone. The scholar stands at the field's edge with the expression of a man who has been arguing with his own conscience about whether this errand is scholarship or salvage, and has decided it is both.

He explains: the pardon was genuine; Piers tore it in anger; the pieces scattered to the city; each person who found one claims it proves what they already believed. A widow who lives at the field's edge found the first piece and has kept it under her hearthstone. She will give it to someone who asks correctly — not a priest, not a scholar, not a lord. Someone who asks correctly.

**Scholar:** "Carry the first piece through the city to collect the others. Hold it up when someone claims their fragment means something the text doesn't support. The words are simple. The words are the authority. Bring me all four pieces before the day ends — Covetousness has sent a buyer for the last piece."

*Insight check DC 12: understand what "asking correctly" means — not with authority, not with argument; with the same simplicity the pardon itself has; the widow held it correctly and will give it to someone who holds it the same way.*

**Failure:** You go to the widow with the bearing of someone on official business. She looks at you carefully and does not hand the fragment over immediately. She asks what you will do with it. When you explain about the scholar, she says: "He cannot read what this says." She gives it to you anyway but the first exchange has cost something.

**Success:** You ask to see what she found. She takes it from under the hearthstone. You hold it in both hands and read it aloud quietly: "Do well, and have well, and God shall have thy soul." She nods once and lets you take it. "Bring it back to the place it belongs," she says. You receive the First Fragment of Piers's Pardon.

#### ACT II — *The Friary*
*The first fragment: in the Fighter's hand. Location: FRY — city friary district.*

The Friar has his fragment framed. He is very proud of it. It reads, in his fragment: "...pardoned perpetually with Paul's testament..." — a phrase that, read out of context, sounds very much like a perpetual exemption for mendicant orders from temporal accountability. The Friar has been showing it to anyone who will listen.

**Friar:** "You see? The plain text. Paul's testament. Which means the friars are covered, don't you think? Under apostolic authority? The fragment says so quite clearly."

He is not lying about what the fragment says. He is lying about what it means by not saying what the rest of the pardon says.

*Deception check DC 12: navigate his argument without entering the debate on his terms — find the angle that removes the fragment from the frame without calling him a liar, which would let him make it a question of your authority rather than the text's.*

**Failure:** You argue. He argues back. The friary is good at this — they have been arguing in exactly this register for a generation. You spend an hour and leave with the fragment only because you invoke the scholar's commission, which he does not legally have to honor.

**Success:** You hold up the First Fragment beside his. "Read them together," you say. He does. The full text is different from his fragment alone and he knows it and cannot say it isn't. He takes the frame off the wall and hands over the fragment with the expression of a man making a contribution to scholarship. You add the Friar's Fragment to your satchel.

#### ACT III — *The Market*
*The first fragment + friar's fragment: in the satchel. Location: MKT — city market district.*

The Pardoner has set up a stall in the market and is selling his own indulgences. In a glass case beside the indulgences he has displayed Piers's fragment — his piece reads "...remit of sins here set forth..." — which he is using to authenticate his own products. "Certified by the original!" His customers are paying well.

He will not give the fragment up while it is generating revenue. But he cannot refuse a direct comparison in public.

*Persuasion check DC 13: hold up the First Fragment in the market, publicly, and read it alongside his fragment — making the complete phrase legible to the crowd standing around his stall, so that the gap between the fragment in his case and the genuine article beside it is visible to the people who just paid him.*

**Failure:** He is faster than you. Before you finish reading, he closes the glass case and calls for his assistant. The assistant is large. You get the fragment but you get it by the fragment's market value — he names a price, you pay, the crowd disperses.

**Success:** The crowd hears the full phrase and looks at his case and at his indulgences with the expression of people doing arithmetic. The Pardoner takes his fragment out of the case himself, before the arithmetic finishes, and hands it over with the professional manner of someone pivoting. "An honest comparison. I have always admired honest comparison." You add the Pardoner's Fragment to your satchel.

---

#### ACT IV — *The Counting House*
*The first fragment + two other fragments: in the satchel. Location: CTH — counting house district.*

The merchant's steward has the third fragment, which his master purchased at auction last month. The fragment reads "...good works done in the name of..." and the merchant has had a certificate attached to it by a cooperative clerk asserting that his charitable donations constitute exactly the good works described. The certificate and the fragment are now filed together as evidence of the merchant's spiritual standing — useful in negotiations with the Church about his lending practices.

The steward will not surrender a document that has been incorporated into a legal file. But the Fighter has the other three pieces now, and can demonstrate that the merged filing certificate changes the meaning of the fragment by removing the clause that immediately follows it.

*Insight check DC 13: find the specific adjacent phrase on one of the other fragments that, when placed beside the merchant's fragment, changes its meaning in a way the steward cannot argue with — not because it's damaging to the merchant's case, but because it makes the certificate's claim technically unsupportable.*

**Failure:** The steward calls the cooperative clerk. The cooperative clerk has a legal argument. The legal argument takes until evening. You get the fragment but you are now late — Covetousness's agent has had extra time to complete his purchase.

**Success:** You find the phrase — "...good works done in the name of Truth, not in the name of the doer..." — and lay the two pieces side by side on the steward's table. The certificate's wording requires the clause about the doer's name, which the adjacent fragment refutes. The steward reads it three times. Then he pulls the fragment from the file with the expression of a man removing something that was never going to survive scrutiny. You add the Merchant's Fragment to your satchel.

---

#### ACT V — *The Gate*
*Three fragments assembled, the last piece contested. Location: WM — Weimar archive district gate.*

Covetousness's agent is at the gate. He has the last fragment. He has a buyer. The buyer is a pardoner-guild representative who will seal the fragment in a case and file it as a contested document — which will prevent the scholar from assembling the full pardon for as long as the challenge stands, which will be longer than the scholar has.

The agent is not here for debate.

*Combat: Covetousness's Agent, AC 14 HP 26. He has the last fragment in his coat pocket. Retrieving it is the objective.*

**Failure:** The agent escapes with the fragment. The buyer takes possession and files the challenge. The scholar receives three of the four pieces and can read most of the pardon but not all of it — the last line, the one about doing evil, is missing. He assembles what he has. The pardon says only the promise, not the condition. It is still useful. It is not complete.

**Success:** The agent does not escape. The last fragment goes into the satchel. All four pieces are in the Fighter's hand as the scholar opens his study door.

The scholar assembles the four pieces on his desk. They fit. The full text reads across all four: "Do well, and have well, and God shall have thy soul; Do evil, and have evil, and expect none other."

He reads it twice. Then he looks up.

**Scholar:** "It says exactly what it always said. It says nothing that hasn't been available to anyone who knew how to ask."

He gives the Fighter a scholar's coin — the formal acknowledgment that a commission from the school has been completed. You receive the Scholar's Coin. The First Fragment is in the scholar's hands with the other three.

*The Fighter thinks: the Friar's piece said friars are exempt. The Pardoner's piece certified his indulgences. The merchant's piece covered his usury. Every reading was wrong in exactly the same way — each person read their own righteousness into the fragment and left out the line that qualifies the promise. I did not correct any of them. I only completed the assembly. The pardon says the same thing it always said. It always did.*
