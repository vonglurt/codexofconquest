## Four Sentences

Lady Gregory's *Cuchulain of Muirthemne* (1902) is the definitive English retelling of Ireland's oldest hero cycle — the Ulster Cycle — narrated in the Kiltartan dialect she shaped from the speech of the Connacht peasants around her estate, a voice W.B. Yeats called "the most beautiful prose style" he had ever encountered, and which gave the Irish Literary Revival its vocabulary. The book arranges the great stories of Cuchulain (born Setanta), champion of the Red Branch warriors of Ulster, from his miraculous birth and boy-deeds through the wooing of Emer, the great cattle raid, and finally his death — tied to a standing stone, too wounded to stand, killing one last enemy from a sitting position as ravens settled on his shoulder. Its emotional heart is the tragedy of Deirdre and the sons of Usnach — the most beautiful love story in Irish literature, where a druid's prophecy of destruction cannot be outrun and a jealous king's false promise costs Ulster its three finest men, its greatest champion's oath-keeper, and the laughter of every hall in Ireland. Lady Gregory is one of the great literary figures of her era not because she wrote these stories but because she heard them still living in the mouths of Irish speakers and understood, before anyone else, that a civilization was in the act of dying and its art needed to be carried somewhere it would last.

## Long Summary — Heroes, Decisions, Romance, Human Condition

### The World

The Ireland of Cuchulain of Muirthemne is Iron Age Ulster — Emain Macha is its capital, a hilltop fortress where Conchubar mac Nessa rules the Red Branch warriors. This is a world of feasts, cattle, champion's portions, and sacred prohibitions (the *geis* — a taboo which, if broken, costs a man his luck and finally his life). The warriors fight at river fords in single combat, sleep in round houses on the hills, and move through a landscape alive with divine presences: gods who father sons on mortal women, druids who pronounce doom at births, supernatural ravens who land on the dying. Lady Gregory renders all of it in prose that sounds both ancient and neighborly, so that heroes speak like people you know and their grief arrives without warning.

### Cuchulain

Born Setanta to the divine father Lugh of the Long Hand through his mortal mother Dechtire, Cuchulain is destined from birth to be Ireland's greatest warrior and to die young. His defining feature is the *riastrad* — the battle-fury — which warps his body beyond recognition: one eye sucked back into his skull, the other protruding, his joints rotating inside his skin, a plume of black blood boiling from his crown. Outside the fury he is gentle, eloquent, and bound by a code of honor so rigid it functions as a second set of geasa. He takes the name Cuchulain — Hound of Culain — as a boy when he kills a smith's famous watchdog in self-defense and offers himself as its replacement until a new hound is trained.

His training under the warrior-woman Scathach in Scotland gives him the Gáe Bulg — a barbed spear thrown with the foot that enters a body as one point and opens into thirty barbs inside the wound. This weapon defines him. He will use it only once on someone he loves, and that moment undoes him more than any enemy ever could.

### Emer

Cuchulain woos Emer — the wisest and most accomplished woman in Ireland — through a courtship conducted entirely in riddle-speech, each of them coding their conversation so that neither's words can be used against them by onlookers. She marries him knowing he will love other women, knowing he will die young, knowing his name will outlast everyone who ever loved him. Her Only Jealousy — when the water goddess Fand attempts to take Cuchulain into the otherworld — shows her bargaining the one thing she has left: she offers to give him up, if Fand will go back. Fand goes. Cuchulain is given a drink of forgetfulness by the druids so that he will not grieve. Emer receives no such drink. Lady Gregory does not mention this, which is its own comment.

### Deirdre and the Sons of Usnach

The great counterpoint tragedy. At her birth, the druid Cathbad pronounces that Deirdre will be the most beautiful woman in Ireland and will bring great sorrow upon Ulster. Conchubar decides to marry her himself and keeps her in seclusion, away from young men, until she is old enough. But she sees Naoise one morning and she chooses him. She shames him into taking her — by touching his cheek and calling him a warrior who plays it safe. He can refuse nothing after that.

Naoise and his brothers Ardan and Ainnle flee with Deirdre to Scotland, where they live in exile and in love for years. When Conchubar sends Fergus mac Róigh — Ulster's greatest champion, former king who gave up his throne rather than break his word — with a guaranteed safe-conduct to return, Deirdre refuses. She has dreamed of ravens with bloody mouths. She sings her lament for Scotland — for the glen of Loch Etive, for the wood of Cuan — and Naoise says: a man cannot live in exile forever. They go.

Conchubar's guarantee is false. Eógan mac Durthacht kills Naoise. The brothers fall. Deirdre is brought to Conchubar's hall and lives there a year without smiling — "like a ewe that has lost her lamb." Then she dashes her head against a stone rather than be passed between Conchubar and Eógan like a possession. Fergus, whose word was used and broken, burns Emain Macha to the ground and goes into permanent exile in Connacht with three thousand warriors.

### Ferdiad and the Price of the Cattle Raid

During the great War for the Bull of Cuailgne, while Ulster's warriors lie helpless under the pangs of Macha (a curse that strikes Ulster's men with the weakness of a woman in labor in Ulster's hour of need), Cuchulain holds the ford against Connacht's entire army alone — single combat, one champion per day, all winter. Maeve eventually sends Ferdiad — his closest friend from their years training with Scathach, the only warrior alive whose skill matches his.

They fight for three days. Each evening they share food across the ford and tend each other's wounds. On the third day, Cuchulain uses the Gáe Bulg. Ferdiad falls. Cuchulain's lament over the body is the most devastating passage in the book: "I loved the noble ways of thee, I loved thine upstanding body. There was a time I would have thought it as good as the whole province of Ulster against thee, and thy falling by me." He is not the same afterward.

### Connla — The Only Son of Aoife

Before leaving Scathach's training camp, Cuchulain loved Aoife, Scathach's rival. She bore his child. He left the boy a gold thumb-ring and three instructions: give his name to no man, yield to no man, refuse no man combat. Years later, a boy appears at the ford of Ulster — wearing Cuchulain's ring, refusing his name, yielding to nothing. Cuchulain fights him. He uses the Gáe Bulg. The boy falls. As he dies, Cuchulain sees the ring and understands. He carries his son's body to the Red Branch hall and lays it before his king. The warriors sit without speaking. There is nothing to say to a man who has done what the code required and lost everything by it.

### The Death of Cuchulain

Cuchulain's enemies maneuver him into breaking his geasa one by one, each violation costing him strength. He is struck by spears cast by the sons of men he killed, spears made by druids specifically to kill him. Mortally wounded, he asks to be allowed to go to the lough and drink. He returns, walks into the water and washes himself, and when his legs fail, he lashes himself to a standing stone so that he will die on his feet facing his enemies. He kills one final man while tied to the stone. A raven lands on his shoulder. Only then does Lugaid, watching from a distance, approach to take the head.

### The Human Condition

Every gift in this book is also the curse. Deirdre's beauty is the prophecy of destruction. Cuchulain's strength means he must fight alone while his king sleeps. Fergus's honor — the absolute inviolability of his given word — is the mechanism by which Conchubar murders the three finest men in Ulster. The *geis* is not arbitrary taboo; it is character made mandatory. Cuchulain cannot yield at a ford because yielding would make him someone who yields at fords. He cannot survive as that person. So he fights to the stone, and lashes himself to it, and dies standing.

Lady Gregory's Kiltartan prose makes all of it sound like what happened to a neighbor. Which is the genius of it. The grief arrives in ordinary sentences.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **The Hound's Night** — Young Setanta has killed Culain's watchdog and must serve as the smith's hound himself until a replacement is trained. Guard Culain's forge through one night while Setanta keeps watch on the road — raiders know the famous dog is gone. A courage-and-combat vigil quest.

2. **Fergus's Word** — Fergus mac Róigh has given his personal guarantee of safe-conduct to escort Deirdre and the sons of Usnach from Scotland back to Ulster. The Fighter carries Fergus's cloak — the physical mark of his protection — from the Scottish dockside to Emain Macha. The cloak arrives. The word is broken anyway. The quest ends at Borrach's fire.

3. **The Ford at Dawn** — Cuchulain holds the Ulster ford alone against Connacht's army while Ulster sleeps. The player fights one champion at the ford each day as proxy escort — buying time, sustaining the line, getting wounded and returning each morning.

4. **Three Nights at the Ford** — During the three-day duel with Ferdiad, escort a healer's supply cart to Cuchulain's camp each evening. Witness the two men sharing food and medicine across the ford. The player cannot intervene; they can only make sure the supplies arrive, and witness what it costs two friends to kill each other correctly.

5. **The Ring at the Ford** — A boy wearing Cuchulain's thumb-ring appears at the ford and refuses to give his name. Carry a message to Cuchulain before the combat begins — a Perception check to read the boy's face in time — knowing that knowing may be worse than the alternative.

6. **The Breaking of the Geasa** — Cuchulain's enemies are sending false messengers to maneuver him into violating his sacred prohibitions one by one. Intercept the messengers before they reach him. Each successful interception buys one more day. Failure is cumulative.

7. **The Standing Stone** — Cuchulain has lashed himself to a standing stone to die on his feet. Guard the stone against his enemies until the raven lands. Then retrieve his sword before it can be taken as a trophy. A vigil-and-retrieval quest where the TOKEN is the sword the dying man can no longer hold.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: *Fergus's Word*

*Storyline 2 — Escort Quest. Fergus mac Róigh has pledged his personal word to bring Deirdre and the sons of Usnach safely from Scotland to Emain Macha. The Fighter is assigned to the escort column. The TOKEN is Fergus's battle-cloak — a physical mark of his protection, given to Naoise at the Scottish dockside.*

**THE TOKEN: FERGUS'S CLOAK** — a heavy war-cloak of red wool, the unmistakable mark of Fergus mac Róigh. He removes it from his own shoulders at the harbor and drapes it over Naoise: "While this rests on you, my word rests on you. No man in Ulster will lift a hand against what is under Fergus mac Róigh's cloak." The Fighter's job is to guard the column and ensure the cloak reaches Emain Macha. It will reach Emain Macha. What happens there is not the Fighter's fault. The cloak is burned in Act V.

---

### ACT I — *The Dockside at Scotland*
*The cloak: on Naoise's shoulders, dry and heavy, smelling of its owner. Location: a Scottish harbor, dawn.*

The sea is silver and cold. Naoise and his brothers Ardan and Ainnle stand at the dock with their gear. Their men move around them, loading the boat. Deirdre stands apart from all of them, her back to the water, looking at the hills of Scotland she is leaving.

Fergus speaks to her in the voice he uses for difficult things — gentle, careful.

**Fergus:** "You have my word, Deirdre. Conchubar has sworn peace. We will bring you home."

**Deirdre,** not turning: "I dreamed of ravens, Fergus. Three of them, with their mouths red."

**Fergus:** "Dreams are not true things."

She boards without answering. Fergus removes his cloak and lays it over Naoise's shoulders himself. He looks at the Fighter.

**Fergus:** "Guard the cloak as you would guard him. If it is taken from him by any hand — any hand — ride back to me at once."

*The Fighter thinks: she knows. She has known longer than any of us, and she is boarding anyway because he asked her to.*

---

### ACT II — *The Crossing*
*The cloak: around Naoise's shoulders, heavy with sea-spray. Location: the channel between Scotland and Ireland, midday.*

The boat pitches in the grey channel. The sons of Usnach sit together at the bow, low-voiced, their heads close. Deirdre stands at the stern looking back at Scotland until it is no longer visible.

When Scotland disappears into cloud, she begins to sing — a lament in the old tongue, for the glen of Loch Etive, for the wood of Cuan, for the sloe-bush, the blackbird, the water. It is not a sad singing. It is worse than sad. It is the singing of a person who has already accepted the accounting.

The oarsmen stop rowing. Naoise puts his arm around her and she stops.

**Naoise:** "We go home."

**Deirdre:** "I had no home but the one behind us."

He holds her close. Over her head his eyes find the Fighter's — they say: *I know. Do not say it. There is no path back that is not worse than forward.*

The cloak is soaked with spray. The Fighter reaches over and straightens it on Naoise's shoulders. He nods once.

*The Fighter thinks: he is bringing her to her death and he knows it and cannot do otherwise. A man who turns back in exile is no man. That is the trap.*

---

### ACT III — *The Feast of Borrach*
*The cloak: on Naoise's shoulders, drying now, inland. Location: a small hall on the Irish coast, one day's ride from Emain Macha.*

Borrach, a lord of the coast, stands in the road with a feast prepared — for Fergus specifically. He insists. It is Fergus's geis: he cannot refuse a feast when offered one. Every person in Ulster has known this for thirty years.

The Fighter watches Fergus's face as he understands. It is not surprise. It is the expression of a man counting the steps of a trap he walked into too far back to retreat from.

**Fergus,** quietly, to his son Illan: "Take my place. They have my word. Guard the cloak."

He turns to the Fighter last.

**Fergus:** "If anything seems wrong when you arrive — if anything at all is wrong — ride back to me. Do not wait. Ride immediately."

He goes into Borrach's hall. The door closes. The sound of the feast begins immediately, as if it had been running already.

**Naoise,** watching the door: "And so Conchubar has his way."

**Deirdre:** "We should turn back."

**Naoise:** "We cannot turn back. We came under a king's guarantee. To flee now is to say the sons of Usnach are afraid."

She looks at Illan — Fergus's young son, brave but not Fergus. She looks at the cloak on Naoise's shoulders. She looks at the Fighter once, and then she looks at the road ahead, and she walks.

*The Fighter thinks: Fergus knew this feast was waiting. I think he knew before we sailed. I think Conchubar counted on it.*

---

### ACT IV — *Emain Macha*
*The cloak: seized from Naoise's shoulders and thrown down. Location: the hall of Emain Macha, evening.*

The hall is wrong the moment you enter. Armed men where feast-guests should stand. Conchubar sitting with Eógan mac Durthacht at his right hand — Eógan, who killed Naoise's father. No welcome-cup. The torches too bright, showing too much.

Eógan steps forward. He looks not at Naoise but at Conchubar, and waits for the nod.

The nod comes.

Eógan takes Fergus's cloak from Naoise's shoulders with one hand. Naoise stands uncovered in the king's hall.

The hall erupts. The sons of Usnach fight — three men and extraordinary — but they are three. Illan falls covering Ardan. Ardan and Ainnle fall covering Naoise. Naoise falls last, in the center of the floor.

Deirdre does not scream. She goes to her knees beside him and covers his face with her hands. She looks up at the Fighter — the cloak is on the stones, in blood — and speaks with the precision of someone who prepared these words long ago.

**Deirdre:** "Take it to Fergus. Let him see what his word was worth in Conchubar's hall."

The Fighter takes the cloak from the floor.

*The Fighter thinks: Fergus did not betray them. Fergus was betrayed. It is not the same thing. It will feel the same to him.*

---

### ACT V — *Borrach's Fire*
*The cloak: burned. Location: Borrach's feast-hall, same night.*

Fergus is still at the feast when the Fighter arrives. He is sitting alone with an untouched cup of wine, watching the door with the face of a man who already knows. He sees the Fighter. He sees the cloak. He does not ask.

He stands slowly and takes the cloak from the Fighter's hands. He holds it — still damp, beginning to stiffen. The feast continues around him, no one understanding yet.

Then he carries the cloak to Borrach's fire and puts it in.

He watches it burn until it is ash. Then he turns to his men, who have gathered near the door, reading his face.

**Fergus:** "We leave Ulster tonight. We go to Connacht. We go to Maeve, who will receive us. And everything we have given Ulster — our loyalty, our oaths, our years — we will take it back, with interest, until there is nothing left of Emain Macha but the memory of what it owed us."

He looks at the Fighter one last time.

**Fergus:** "You carried the cloak. You carried it faithfully. That is all that was yours to do."

He pulls his war-ring from his finger and holds it out. Then he walks out into the dark. Three thousand men follow.

The quest is finished. The cloak is ash. The word could not be kept by the man who made it and could not be broken by the man who carried it.

*The Fighter thinks: I carried his word from Scotland to this fire and I carried it exactly. There is no comfort in that. There is only the fact of it, which will have to be enough.*

## Cycle 2: The Standing Stone

### Pass 1 — Plot Précis (The Standing Stone)

Cuchulain's enemies, working through Calatín's daughters, have broken his geasa one by one through false messengers and compelled violations. He is struck by three druid-made spears on the open plain. Mortally wounded, he asks leave to go to the lough and drink and wash himself. When his legs fail on the return, he lashes himself to the standing stone with his own belt — he will not die fallen. He kills one final enemy who approaches too early. A grey raven lands on his shoulder. Only then does Lugaid, watching from the eastern treeline since dawn, cross the plain to take the head. The sword, loosened from Cuchulain's failing grip, rests against the stone's base. Lugaid takes the head. What he does not reach in time is carried north to Emain Macha.

### Pass 2 — Fighter's Role (The Standing Stone)

The Fighter arrives at the stone while Cuchulain is still conscious. He does not need rescue. He needs a witness who understands the commission exactly: hold the stone's perimeter so no one crosses the field while he still breathes — not by fighting, because his enemies are still afraid of him; by presence — and when the raven lands and Lugaid comes, take the sword from the ground before it becomes a trophy and carry it north to the Red Branch.

### Pass 3 — What This Angle Reveals That Cycle 1 Missed

Cycle 1 was about the faithfully-kept word arriving at its broken destination — the tragedy of faithfulness in a faithless context. *The Standing Stone* reveals something stranger: the man who decides the form of his own death. Cuchulain is not dying in defeat. He is dying with intention. He lashes himself upright. He kills from the stone. He waits for the raven with the calm of someone whose accounting is complete. What the Fighter witnesses is not grief but mastery over the shape of ending — and what this requires from a witness is not help, not intervention, but precision. Cycle 1 had the Fighter carrying someone else's word. Cycle 2 has the Fighter holding the space of a decision already made, and then carrying the one thing that can still be carried somewhere it will be kept.

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Standing Stone*

*Storyline 7 — Vigil and Retrieval. Cuchulain is lashed to the standing stone on the Ulster plain, dying. The Fighter holds the vigil. TOKEN: Cuchulain's Sword — established at his belt Act I, received by Fighter Act III when grip fails, contested Act IV, delivered Act V.*

**THE TOKEN: CUCHULAIN'S SWORD** — a long iron blade in a battered scabbard. In Act I it is belted at his hip, still his. In Act III his grip fails and the scabbard slides down the stone. In Act IV the Fighter holds it against two men who want it as a trophy. In Act V it is delivered to Emain Macha — the one piece of him that reaches the hall he spent his life defending.

---

#### ACT I — *The Stone, Midday*
*The sword: belted at his side, still his. Location: PSU — pillar stone, Ulster plain.*

The plain is open in every direction. The standing stone is grey and old, taller than a man, with a faint stain at its base that is not from weather. Cuchulain is lashed to it with his own belt — standing, wounded past the kind of help you carry in a satchel, his color the color of the stone behind him. His eyes are fixed on the eastern treeline where Lugaid has been waiting since before dawn.

He hears you approach. He does not look away from the field.

**Cuchulain:** "I know what you do. You carry things. Stay."

He is quiet for a moment.

**Cuchulain:** "The women came. Leam brought food and I ate because she asked. Niamh wept and I would not leave while she wept. I sent them away when the weeping was done. I need someone who will not weep."

The treeline at the field's east edge is absolutely still.

**Cuchulain:** "When the raven lands, Lugaid comes. He wants this sword." He does not touch the scabbard. "It is not his. It belongs to Emain Macha. When he comes, take it before he does. Carry it north."

He looks at you for the first time — the measuring look, the full attention, the last of his strength spent on something he has decided matters.

**Cuchulain:** "Hold the stone. Do not fight. They will not cross while I still breathe — they are afraid of me yet. Be here. That is the whole commission."

*The Fighter thinks: he is giving me the only commission he has left. The stone is his. The sword will be the Red Branch's. I am the distance between what happens here and what Emain Macha will remember of it.*

---

#### ACT II — *The Long Afternoon*
*The sword: still at his hip, still his. Location: PSU — same stone, afternoon.*

The sun moves. At the field's edge, figures appear and withdraw — Lugaid's men testing the boundary, seeing whether he is dead yet, unable to cross while his eyes still track them. Cuchulain does not speak again. He is past speech. But his eyes still move and the enemies at the treeline still will not cross.

You have been at the stone's base for three hours. The wind from the north is cold. One of Lugaid's men walks half the distance across the field, stops, looks at Cuchulain's face, and walks back.

*The Fighter thinks: they are still afraid of him. Even now.*

The light begins to change. The waiting has its own weight. To hold still when every instinct says move requires something closer to endurance than to courage.

---

#### ACT III — *The Raven*
*The sword: loosening as the grip fails. Location: PSU.*

Late afternoon. His hand, which has been closed around the pommel since before you arrived, loosens. He cannot prevent it. The scabbard begins to tilt against the stone. He watches it happen with a kind of absolute quiet, as if watching something that belongs to someone else at a great distance.

The grey raven comes in from the north. It circles once. It lands on his shoulder.

At the treeline, Lugaid steps out.

You have the time it takes a man to cross fifty yards of flat grass.

*Perception check DC 13: read the exact moment the raven's shadow falls on the stone — before Lugaid takes his first step, not after.*

**Failure:** You see the raven land. You look at Lugaid. You look at the sword. The sequence costs three seconds. Lugaid is ten feet away when your hand and his reach the scabbard at the same moment. You do not lose it, but the retrieval is a contest rather than a custody, and Cuchulain's commission was for a custody.

**Success:** You see the shadow before the bird. You have the scabbard in your grip the moment Lugaid steps from the treeline. He crosses the field and finds the sword already held. He takes the head without speaking and turns back. You receive Cuchulain's Sword.

---

#### ACT IV — *The Trophy Men*
*The sword: in the Fighter's hand, contested. Location: PSU.*

Lugaid is already walking back to the treeline with the head. But two of his men — not Lugaid, men who came for something to bring home — have decided the sword is as good as a head and approach from two sides with the manner of men who expect possession to transfer on the basis of numbers.

They do not ask. They do not negotiate. They approach.

**Failure:** You are driven from the stone. Lugaid calls his men off before they do worse — he is not without honor — and you leave the field alive, but the sword goes with them. Emain Macha will hear of this death from someone else's account.

**Success:** They learn what the sword's previous owner could have told them: the object is not the difficult part. You hold the stone until they withdraw. The field empties. The plain is quiet. The raven is still on his shoulder and the sword is in your hand and you are the only living person left on the field.

---

#### ACT V — *Emain Macha's Gate*
*The sword: carried north. Location: ULC — Ulster plain road north, Emain Macha gate.*

The road north is three hours. No one follows. At Emain Macha's inner gate, Conall Cernach stands with the face of a man who has been counting hours. He was supposed to be with Cuchulain today. He was not there. He knows what your arrival means before you reach him.

He looks at the sword. He does not take it yet.

**Conall:** "How did it end?"

This is not a question about facts. He knows the facts — Lugaid, the spears, the stone. He is asking about the form. He is asking what kind of death it was.

*Persuasion check DC 12: give a true account to a man who loved the dead — not an elegy, not a comfort, but the exact thing you witnessed, in the order it happened, without amendment.*

**Failure:** You tell it in the register of grief. Your voice apologizes for the fact of it. You soften one thing. You leave out the moment the raven landed because you do not want to say what it meant. Conall takes the sword. He looks at it a long time. "You were there," he says, and nothing else. The commission is complete but the account is not what Cuchulain sent you to carry.

**Success:** You say it exactly: the stone, the belt, the waiting, the raven landing before Lugaid moved, the sword already in your hands when he arrived. He chose the form. He chose the stone. He was present for what happened to him. Conall is quiet a long time. Then he takes the sword from your hands with both of his, the way you receive something that will live in a case with a name on it. "He would have chosen you for this," he says. You receive Conall's Coin — the Red Branch's acknowledgment. The sword is in Emain Macha's keeping.

*The Fighter thinks: I carried his commission from the stone to this gate and I carried it exactly. There is no comfort in that. There is only the fact of it, which will have to be enough. It is.*
