## Cycle 1 — Angle: *The Founding Loss — What the city is built on and who pays for it*

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling

Virgil wrote the *Aeneid* between roughly 29 and 19 BC, at the request — or at least the encouragement — of Augustus Caesar, who wanted a national epic comparable to Homer. He never finished it. He died at Brindisi in 19 BC while still revising, leaving instructions to burn the manuscript. His literary executors defied him. What survives has unfinished half-lines and transitional inconsistencies — beautiful accidents preserved against the author's own wishes.

The poem opens mid-storm: *"Arma virumque cano"* — "Arms and the man I sing." The man is Aeneas, Trojan prince, son of Anchises and Venus, carrying his elderly father on his back out of burning Troy. The storm is Juno's malice. The arms are inherited — carried from Troy across seven years of Mediterranean wandering to the coast of Italy, where Aeneas will fight a brutal war to found what will become Rome.

**Book 1:** Juno hates the Trojans. She persuades Aeolus to unleash storms on Aeneas's fleet. Neptune calms the sea. Aeneas makes landfall near Carthage, where Dido is founding a new city. Venus (his mother, in disguise) tells him Dido's history: her husband Sychaeus was murdered by her brother Pygmalion; she fled Tyre with the treasury and founded Carthage in North Africa. *"Dux femina facti"* — a woman led this enterprise. Aeneas, rendered invisible by Venus, enters Carthage and sees — in the temple of Juno — murals depicting the Trojan War. His enemy memorialized his people's suffering in stone. He weeps: *"Sunt lacrimae rerum et mentem mortalia tangunt"* — there are tears for things, and mortal matters touch the mind. He makes himself visible, meets Dido, feasts in her hall. Cupid, disguised as Ascanius, inflames Dido with love. She asks Aeneas to tell his story.

**Book 2:** Aeneas narrates the fall of Troy. Sinon's false tale about the Wooden Horse. Laocoon's warning — *"timeo Danaos et dona ferentis"* — I fear the Greeks even bearing gifts — goes unheeded; sea-serpents kill him and his sons. The horse enters Troy. That night the Greeks pour out. Priam's son Polites is killed before his father; Pyrrhus then drags Priam himself to the altar and kills him there. *"Haec finis Priami fatorum"* — this was the end of Priam's story. Aeneas finds Hector's ghost who tells him to flee with the Penates. Aeneas nearly kills Helen; Venus stops him, shows him the gods literally pulling Troy's walls down. He returns to his father: Anchises refuses to leave, calls himself too old. A flame appears on Ascanius's head, unburning, and a shooting star streaks toward Italy — divine signs. Anchises consents. Aeneas carries Anchises on his shoulders, holding the Penates; Ascanius holds his hand; Creusa walks behind. At the meeting point, Creusa is gone. Aeneas goes back into the burning city three times searching. He finds only her ghost, who speaks: Italy awaits you, a royal bride awaits you; do not grieve. Three times he tries to embrace her — three times she slips through his arms like wind, like sleep. *"Par levibus ventis volucrique simillima somno."* The founding loss. Every grief that follows lives inside this one.

**Book 3:** Seven years of wandering. Thrace first: Aeneas tries to break branches from a mound for altar-greens and blood drips. A voice: "I am Polydorus — Priam sent me here with gold; the Thracian king killed me when Troy fell. *Auri sacra fames*" — accursed hunger for gold. A proper burial, and they leave. Delos: the oracle says seek "the ancient mother." Anchises misremembers — he thinks Crete. They settle in Crete; plague strikes. The Penates appear in Aeneas's dream: not Crete — Italy. They sail again. Strophades: the Harpies. Buthrotum: Helenus (Trojan prophet) and Andromache (Hector's widow) have built a miniature Troy in Epirus. Andromache is still performing funeral rites at an empty grave-mound for Hector — she has a tomb but no body. She asks immediately about Ascanius: *"Ecquid in antiquam virtutem animosque virilis / et pater Aeneas et avunculus excitat Hector?"* — does his father Aeneas, does his uncle Hector (now a ghost), rouse the boy to the old courage? Helenus prophesies: seek the white sow with thirty piglets; avoid the eastern Italian coast (full of Greek settlers); consult the Sibyl at Cumae. Aeneas sees Italy for the first time — the shout: *"Italiam!"* A brief landing, more sailing, the Cyclops coast where they rescue Achaemenides (a crewman of Odysseus left behind after the blinding of Polyphemus). Drepanum, Sicily: Anchises dies. The end of Aeneas's narration.

**Book 4:** The most beautiful and most devastating book. Dido can't sleep. She confesses to her sister Anna: *"Agnosco veteris vestigia flammae"* — I recognize the traces of the old flame. Anna encourages her. Sacrifices, omens, the hunt. Juno and Venus conspire: a storm, a cave — Dido and Aeneas take shelter together. *"Ille dies primus leti primusque malorum causa fuit"* — that day was the first of her death and the first of her sorrows. Dido calls it marriage. Virgil calls it cover for the crime. Fama — Rumor — spreads through Africa. Iarbas, a Libyan king who wanted Dido, prays to Jupiter in rage. Jupiter sends Mercury to rebuke Aeneas. Mercury finds him dressed in Tyrian purple, building Carthage — playing house. The rebuke: *"heu regni rerumque oblite tuarum?"* — have you forgotten your kingdom and your obligations? Aeneas is struck silent. He orders the fleet prepared secretly. Dido finds out. The great confrontation: her passionate denunciation (*"nec tibi diva parens... sed duris genuit te cautibus horrens Caucasus, Hyrcanaeque admorunt ubera tigres"* — your mother wasn't a goddess; the Caucasus granite made you, Hyrcanian tigers nursed you). His cold answer: *"Italiam non sponte sequor"* — I do not follow Italy of my own will. It is duty, not choice. He does not love her less; he leaves anyway. She asks for delay only, through Anna; Mercury comes again at night with a harder warning. Aeneas flees at dawn. Dido from the citadel watches the sails fill. Her curse — she calls for an avenger from her bones: *"exoriare aliquis nostris ex ossibus ultor"* — let some avenger arise. She means Hannibal. She means the Punic Wars. She mounts the pyre she has built in the inner court (pretending it's a magic ritual to Anna), falls on Aeneas's sword. Her last words: *"Vixi, et quem dederat cursum fortuna, peregi"* — I have lived, and the course fortune gave me I have completed. She dies not quite complete — Iris descends from heaven to cut her golden hair and release the soul properly. The scene closes. Aeneas, out at sea, sees smoke. He doesn't know what it means.

**Book 5:** The funeral games for Anchises's anniversary in Sicily. A boat race, a foot race (Nisus falls in blood and deliberately trips his rival to help his beloved Euryalus win — the first hint of the Nisus-Euryalus friendship that will cost both their lives in Book 9), boxing (the ancient champion Entellus knocks out the young Dares), archery (Acestes shoots a flaming arrow that becomes an omen). The boys' cavalry display — the *Ludus Troiae* — Ascanius leading. Then Juno sends Iris to incite the Trojan women, worn out by years at sea, to burn the ships. They do. Jupiter's rain saves all but four. Aeneas despairs. The elder Nautes advises leaving the weary women and old men with Acestes in Sicily — found a city there (Acesta). Anchises's ghost appears: go to the Sibyl, descend to the Underworld, see me. Aeneas leaves a colony in Sicily and sails. At night the god Sleep (disguised as a sailor) convinces Palinurus, the helmsman, to sleep for just a moment — and the god throws him overboard. *"Nudus in ignota, Palinure, iacebis harena"* — naked, Palinurus, you will lie on an unknown shore. Aeneas takes the helm himself.

**Book 6:** The center and pivot. Cumae. The great cave of the Sibyl, one hundred mouths. Aeneas asks her to speak the prophecy aloud rather than writing it on leaves — leaves scatter. She speaks: war, war, war in Italy — a new Achilles, a new Hector, a new cause in a foreign woman. *"Bella, horrida bella / et Thybrim multo spumantem sanguine cerno"* — wars, terrible wars, and the Tiber foaming with much blood. Aeneas asks to descend to the Underworld to see Anchises. The Sibyl's famous answer: *"facilis descensus Averno; / ... sed revocare gradum superasque evadere ad auras, / hoc opus, hic labor est"* — easy is the descent to Avernus; but to recall the step and emerge to the upper air — this is the task, this is the work. First requirements: find the Golden Bough (Persephone's tribute), and bury the unburied dead among the fleet. Venus's doves lead him to the Bough. The unburied dead turns out to be Misenus the trumpeter, drowned by Triton for the vanity of challenging the gods to a music contest. They build his pyre and bury him.

The descent: the geography of the Underworld, its vestibule filled with personified abstractions — Grief, Care, Disease, Age, Fear, Hunger, Poverty, Death, Labor, Sleep, Evil Joy, War, the Furies. Charon's river. The unburied crowd the near shore, waiting a hundred years before they can cross. Palinurus is there — he reached land but was killed by locals. He begs for burial; the Sibyl promises his headland will bear his name forever. Charon challenges them; the Sibyl shows the Golden Bough and Charon relents. Cerberus drugged with a honeycake. The sections of the dead: infants; those wrongly condemned; suicides; the Fields of Mourning.

In the Fields of Mourning is Dido. Aeneas sees her through shadow, the way you see the new moon through cloud. He says: *"Funeris heu tibi causa fui?"* — was I the cause of your death? He swears he left against his will. She turns away, returns to Sychaeus (her first husband) — *"coniunx ubi pristinus illi / respondet curis aequatque Sychaeus amorem"* — where her first husband responds to her grief and matches her love. Aeneas weeps and follows her with his eyes as she goes.

Further: the warriors. Mutilated Deiphobus (Helen betrayed him the night the Horse was opened — she removed his weapons while he slept and called the Greeks). The fork: left to Tartarus, right to Elysium. The Sibyl describes Tartarus's horrors: *"Discite iustitiam moniti, et non temnere divos"* — Phlegyas's eternal warning: learn justice, and do not despise the gods. Elysium: blessed meadows, Orpheus playing, the Trojan heroes, the great souls. They find Anchises reviewing the souls waiting for reincarnation, counting his future descendants.

The doctrine of transmigration: souls purified over a thousand years drink Lethe and forget, then return to new bodies. Anchises leads Aeneas to a high place and shows him the Parade of Roman Heroes — the souls waiting to be born. Romulus. Numa. The kings. Caesar. And then: *"Hic vir, hic est, tibi quem promitti saepius audis, / Augustus Caesar"* — here he is, the man you have often heard promised to you, Augustus Caesar — who will restore the Golden Age. And the political speech of the epic: *"Tu regere imperio populos, Romane, memento; / hae tibi erunt artes; pacisque imponere morem, / parcere subiectis, et debellare superbos"* — Roman, remember to rule the nations with your power; these will be your arts: to impose the custom of peace, to spare the subject, to defeat the proud. And then the young Marcellus — Augustus's nephew, died at 23, his heir and the great hope — walking with his head down under a dark shadow. Anchises weeps: *"Manibus date lilia plenis"* — give lilies with full hands. Scatter flowers. He is what might have been.

Aeneas is sent back through the Ivory Gate — the gate of false dreams. (*"Sunt geminae Somni portae... altera candenti perfecta nitens elephanto, / sed falsa ad caelum mittunt insomnia Manes"*) — There are two gates of Sleep; one is horn, through which true shades pass easily; the other is gleaming ivory, through which the dead send false dreams upward. Scholars have argued for two thousand years: why the Ivory Gate? Servius said it marks the unreality of what was shown. Others say: because Aeneas is still alive and only the dead properly exit the gate of horn. Or: everything shown was the future, and the future is still, strictly speaking, false — it hasn't happened yet.

Book 7's opening: arrival at the Tiber's mouth. Ascanius eating the flat bread-discs used as plates, says *"heus, etiam mensas consumimus"* — we're eating the tables too! The Harpies' curse fulfilled. Aeneas knows he has arrived.

#### Chapter Notes

- **Book 1 — Storm and Shore**: Juno's hatred drives the action; the murals in Juno's temple drive the emotional register. The structural irony: Aeneas weeps seeing himself depicted in the enemy's temple; the pain and the preservation are the same act.
- **Book 2 — The Fall of Troy**: Aeneas as witness and narrator. The chain: Laocoon warns → ignored → serpents → horse inside → city dies. Creusa's absence at the end is the founding wound.
- **Book 3 — The Wanderings**: Polydorus as the first lesson (gold destroys hospitality); Andromache as the lesson of false homecoming (a miniature Troy is not Troy); Helenus as the lesson of prophecy by limitation (the Sibyl knows more than I can tell you).
- **Book 4 — Dido**: Virgil's great political tragedy. The poem's most read book. The love is real; the duty is also real; both are true simultaneously, and one of them kills her.
- **Book 5 — Funeral Games**: Relief, ceremony, community, and then Juno's final strike at the ships. The colony left in Sicily is the first piece of what Rome will be — people who couldn't make it to the end.
- **Book 6 — The Katabasis**: The structural and emotional climax. The Underworld as a map of Roman values — who is punished, who is rewarded, who is anticipated. The Ivory Gate sends Aeneas back carrying a vision the poem marks as conditional.
- **Book 7 (opening) — Arrival**: The prophecy fulfilled in bread. The Tiber. Latinus and Lavinia. The oracle of Faunus. Turnus as suitor. The war is beginning.

### Pass 2 — Characters

**Aeneas** — *Pius Aeneas*. The qualifier is doing everything. *Pius* means dutiful, pious, loyal — not warm. He carries his father out of Troy on his shoulders. He leaves Dido. He weeps at the Underworld's gate for all the dead he knows. He is not cold; he is a man who chooses duty each time despite feeling otherwise, and the strain of this shows in the places where he almost doesn't. His greatness is also his limitation: he is history's instrument, not history's subject.

**Dido** — The most fully human person in the poem. Three phases: the founder (she built a city from nothing with refugees and gold she saved from her own murderous brother), the lover (who returns to feeling after years of refusing), the woman betrayed (whose rage and grief are the most justified emotions in the poem). Her curse is a prophecy. She knows Aeneas doesn't intend to destroy her; he just will anyway. *"Italiam non sponte sequor"* is almost worse than if he'd chosen to leave — he's leaving because he has no choice, which means there's nothing she can say.

**Anchises** — The father in three registers: (1) the man who must be physically carried, the weight of the past Aeneas cannot put down; (2) the man who dies in Sicily, whose absence shapes all of Book 5; (3) the prophet in Elysium, the father transfigured into vision, who shows his son what all the suffering was for. His grief for Marcellus — *"tu Marcellus eris"* — is the most human moment in Elysium: a man in the land of the dead weeping for someone not yet born who will die too young.

**The Sibyl** — A woman ridden by a god. Her speech shifts between her own voice and Apollo's compulsion. Her practical wisdom is the poem's best advice: don't let the oracles blow away on leaves; find the Bough first; bury the dead before you ask to pass. Her line about the descent/return is the most cited in the poem and means everything about how Virgil understood difficulty: the hard thing isn't getting down, it's getting back.

**Creusa** — The founding absence. She is the first thing Aeneas loses and the loss that makes all the other losses structurally bearable, because she names herself as chosen — the Mother of the Gods keeps her here. She's not taken; she's held. This is a very deliberate design choice: the founding loss has a guardian.

**Juno** — The divine antagonist whose hatred is permanent but not incomprehensible. She hates Trojans for reasons that predate Aeneas (the Judgment of Paris, Ganymede, the prophecy about Carthage). Her hatred drives the plot. Her eventual reconciliation (in the books not read here) is required for the poem's resolution.

### Pass 3 — The Pivotal Scene: Dido's Death (Book 4, lines 642–705)

The mural brief:

*At trepida, et coeptis immanibus effera Dido, / sanguineam volvens aciem, maculisque trementis / interfusa genas, et pallida morte futura, / interiora domus inrumpit limina, et altos / conscendit furibunda rogos, ensemque recludit / Dardanium, non hos quaesitum munus in usus.*

Trembling, wild with her great undertaking, Dido — her eyes rolling bloodshot, her trembling cheeks flushed with blotches, pale with imminent death — bursts through the inner threshold of her house and climbs in fury to the high pyre, and unsheathed the Trojan sword, not gotten for this purpose.

She climbs. The sword is Aeneas's — she already knew she'd use it. The physical detail: *sanguineam volvens aciem* — her bloodshot eyes rolling. This is not beauty. This is a person at the exact edge.

*Hic, postquam Iliacas vestes notumque cubile / conspexit, paulum lacrimis et mente morata, / incubuitque toro, dixitque novissima verba:*

When she saw the Trojan clothes and the familiar bed, she paused a moment in weeping and in thought, lay down on the bed, and spoke her last words:

*"Dulces exuviae, dum fata deusque sinebant, / accipite hanc animam, meque his exsolvite curis."*

Sweet relics, while fate and god allowed, receive this soul, and release me from these cares.

She is lying on the bed she shared with Aeneas. The *exuviae* — spoils, relics, remains — are his clothes, his things, what he left behind. She calls them sweet. She is thanking them. The tenderness is almost unbearable.

*"Vixi, et quem dederat cursum fortuna, peregi, / et nunc magna mei sub terras ibit imago."*

I have lived, and the course that fortune gave me I have completed; and now my great shade will go beneath the earth.

The last line she is proud of: she founded a city, she avenged Sychaeus, she received great guests, she loved. She dies with an account closed.

*Dixit, et, os impressa toro, "Moriemur inultae, / sed moriamur" ait.*

She said it, and pressed her face into the bed: "We will die unavenged — but let us die."

The shift to first person plural (*moriamur*) in the midst of dying alone. She reaches for company at the end and finds only herself, plural.

She falls on the sword. The sword does not kill her cleanly — it was not a wound suited to dying. Anna finds her: *"Hoc illud, germana, fuit? Me fraude petebas?"* — was this the plan, sister? You deceived me? She holds her dying sister, wipes the blood. Dido tries three times to raise herself. Three times. (Creusa also: three times Aeneas tries to embrace her. Three times.) Juno sends Iris: the rainbow descends, cuts the golden hair, and the soul is freed. The final image is spectral meteorology — *"mille trahens varios adverso sole colores"* — drawing a thousand colors against the sun.

### Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **"Sunt Lacrimae Rerum"** — Aeneas sees the Trojan War memorialized in murals on the walls of Juno's temple at Carthage — painted by enemies who kept his people's grief in stone. The commission document for those murals survives, sealed in a cedar box, in a monastery near old Cumae. Fighter must deliver it to the Weimar archive.

2. **The Ivory Gate Fragment** — A wax tablet carried from the Underworld through the gate of false dreams, bearing the first twenty names from Anchises's Parade of Heroes — people not yet born when it was inscribed. The archivist who receives it must decide: file under Prophecy, or under False Records. Fighter carries it through three institutional gatekeepers who all have conflicting theories.

3. **Creusa's Third Attempt** — Archaeological workers near the ruins of Troy have found a single woman's sandal in the rubble of a sanctuary, with a handprint burned into the clay beside it. A scholar who has identified it as the spot where Creusa vanished is being silenced by a Greek nationalist academic faction. Fighter escorts the attestation.

4. **Palinurus's Shore** — Palinurus (the helmsman thrown overboard by the Sleep god) was promised by the Sibyl that his headland would bear his name forever — *nomen Palinuri habebit*. The name is being erased from a coastal chart by a duke who wants his own name on the cape. Fighter carries the Sibyl's original prophetic text to the maritime registry.

5. **The Widow's Calculation** — Andromache's empty tomb for Hector — *viridique quem caespite inanem sacraverat* — a mound of green turf with no body. She performed the rites anyway. A theologian in 1367 is arguing this constitutes heresy (rites for someone still alive, in theory — Hector's soul might have been reincarnated by Virgil's logic). Fighter carries the attestation of orthodox grief.

6. **The Harpies' Clause** — The Harpy Celaeno prophesied at the Strophades that the Trojans would eat their tables before they could build walls in Italy. When Ascanius fulfills this prophecy (eating the flat-bread platters), it triggers a ritual obligation. Someone in 1367 has found a Strophades document claiming those islands still collect the theological debt. Fighter.

7. **Dido's Sealed Accusation** — Before her death, Dido dictated one final document to her secretary: a formal complaint to the gods, naming Aeneas by lineage and describing the circumstances with the precision of a legal deposition. The secretary hid it in the archive; it survived Carthage's fall (146 BC) and 1,500 years of transmission. Fighter must deliver it to the one archive that will receive both sides of an old claim.

## Theme Check

**Existing themes to avoid**: loyalty under long watch; grief in an object; honor vs. shame; love and fate; posthumous truth; healing through hostile law; the hunted carrier; ancient witnesses; corruption/mercy; fate through broken things; heroism without shelter; invitation as weapon; the ransoming poem; the one who won't weep; love refusing loss; the martyr's death-command; the prophecy that changes nothing; the original fire; anonymous reparation; the compelled traitor; the silenced victim; the cracked relic; the enemy's timing strategy; the condemned's right to be heard; the impossible witness; the champion's erased near-disaster; mercy-before-the-seal; the son's flight; authorship as claim; the wound as witness; divine vs. human record; the debt to the dead; the last just act; the bound man's incomplete transmission.

**FCO theme chosen**: *The enemy made your monument. The record of grief is preserved by those who caused it. Without them, you would not be in the archive.* (Source: the Carthaginian murals in Juno's temple, memorializing Trojan suffering in the house of their divine enemy — Aeneas sees himself depicted by the people who helped destroy him, and is comforted by this.)

This is distinct from all above themes. The nearest neighbor is LGW (posthumous truth) and FLR (divine vs. human records) but neither covers: the victim's record kept by the perpetrator; the inadvertent archival act of the enemy; the grief preserved in hostile custody and recognized as *yours* nonetheless.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: *Sunt Lacrimae Rerum*

### The Commission

*The Mural Brief — dry, folded papyrus in oilskin wrapping — written by the hand of the chief priest of Juno's temple at Carthage, 814 BC — two columns: Punic on the left, Latin translation on the right — specifying each scene of Trojan suffering to be painted in the great hall — last line of commission: "so that all who enter may know that the world contains tears for things." Held now by an old Franciscan monk in a ruined scriptorium near Cumae.*

---

**ACT I**

*The Mural Brief — dry and salt-smelling in its oilcloth — in the monk's hands — on a stone table in the scriptorium ruins near Cumae, morning.*

The monk's name is Brother Eudo. He is sixty-three, was assigned to inventory the library before dissolution, and found the document three months ago in a cedar box under the floor. He has not left since. He sits at the stone table in the open ruin — the roof fell in the winter of 1358 — reading a phrase he cannot translate. He does not know Virgil. He knows only that three words in the Latin column say *lacrimae rerum* and that someone long ago thought that was important enough to commission in stone.

The Fighter arrives in the morning, referred by a pilgrim on the road.

Eudo does not speak at first. He slides the oilcloth-wrapped papyrus across the table with both hands, palms flat. He has been deciding to trust the wrong person for three months. The Fighter must read what trust looks like when it has waited too long.

*— A man who has been carrying something by himself long enough begins to hold it differently. The hands tell you when he's ready to let go.*

> **Skill Check: Insight DC 12.** Read that Eudo isn't handing you a document — he's handing you the person he's been since he found it. The words that open him are not reassuring ones; they are precise ones. Name what you see.

**Fail:** Eudo pulls the oilcloth back. "I am sorry. I am not ready." The Fighter waits in the ruined courtyard until evening. Try again; he comes out at vespers.

**Pass:** Eudo sets his palms flat. "There is a place in Weimar that keeps things. I have heard of it. I cannot make this journey — my feet are wrong for it. But you have the kind of face that has carried things before." He unwraps the oilcloth and lets you look. The papyrus crackles. The Punic column is dense, geometrical. The Latin column is clearer: each scene specified with a painter's precision — *Priamus rex ante aras occisus* — king Priam slain before the altars; *Achilles corpus Hectoris sub muris trahens* — Achilles dragging Hector's body beneath the walls. The last line: *ut qui ingrediuntur sciant esse lacrimae rerum* — so that all who enter may know the world contains tears for things.

**TOKEN GRANTED: The Mural Brief** — folded papyrus in oilskin, Punic and Latin columns, smelling of cedar and old salt, the last line in the priest's careful hand: *esse lacrimae rerum.*

---

**ACT II**

*The Mural Brief — in the Fighter's satchel — on the coastal road north of Cumae, late afternoon.*

A Genoese merchant named Bartolomeo has been following Brother Eudo for six weeks. He does not want the document for its content; he wants it for what it will fetch. His family held the Genoese trading post at Tunis until 1356; he believes anything excavated near Carthage belongs to his family's archive by right of prior commercial claim. He is not violent. He is patient and well-dressed and uses legal language with the ease of a man who has won arguments this way before.

He intercepts the Fighter on the road at the lip of a creek crossing.

"That document was found in proximity to Carthaginian commercial territory. I am prepared to pay fairly for its return to proper custody." He opens a wallet. The coins are genuine. He is offering a category, not a theft.

*— He believes what he's saying. That's the problem. Believing the wrong category is worse than lying.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 13.** Convince Bartolomeo that historical commission documents exist in a category that commercial prior-claim cannot touch — they belong to the persons depicted, not to the persons who trade nearest the site.

**Fail:** Bartolomeo files an injunctive claim on the road. He is bluffing, but the bluff costs a day's delay. The Fighter camps at the creek. Try again at dawn; Bartolomeo is less certain in the morning light.

**Pass:** Bartolomeo closes his wallet. A long silence. Then: "I don't suppose it would make the papers show 'Genoese collection' in the provenance." "No." Another silence. He steps aside. "Tell the archivist I had prior claim and declined it. That's worth something." He gives you a notarized letter of his disclaimer to present at intake. 

**ITEM GRANTED: Bartolomeo's Disclaimer** — notarized document, signed and sealed, stating that Genoese commercial claim is formally declined in favor of archive delivery. Provenance paperwork. Weight: almost nothing.

---

**ACT III**

*The Mural Brief — still in satchel — at the mountain waystation monastery of San Agostino, Alpine approach, three days north.*

A bishop's agent named Padre Orsini is waiting in the refectory. He traveled twelve days from his bishop's palace in Mantua to intercept this document on the road. The bishop has a theological argument in preparation — that even the enemies of Rome recognized the sanctity of grief as divinely ordered — and he wants the Mural Brief as evidence. He is correct that the document supports this argument. He is also correct that the bishop's cause is just. The problem is that no single institution should hold the argument and its evidence simultaneously.

Orsini is accompanied by two house guards. He is prepared to be courteous about this.

*— He knows he's right. He just doesn't know what "right" means in this case.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 14.** Persuade Orsini that the brief must reach a neutral archive precisely because the bishop's argument is important — housed in an episcopal library, the evidence becomes partisan; housed in Weimar, it remains available to all sides including the bishop's theologians.

**Fail:** Orsini produces a writ from his bishop authorizing requisition of relevant archival materials. The guards step forward. The Fighter must fight through them.

**Pass (Persuasion):** Orsini pauses. His theological training kicks in: he recognizes the argument. "The bishop will not be pleased." The guards step back. "Tell the Archivus the Bishop of Mantua extends his greetings and his regret."

*If the Fighter fails Persuasion:*
> **Combat: 2 guards — AC 12, HP 15 each.** They are professionals, not enthusiasts. They will yield at half health if offered a graceful exit.

**ITEM GRANTED: San Agostino Receipt** — the monastery guestmaster's entry log, showing the date and the nature of the document passing through — chain of custody, dated, witnessed.

**ACT IV**

*The Mural Brief — in satchel — at the Rhine river crossing at Säckingen, late March, spring flood.*

The barge is the only crossing for three miles in either direction. The bargeman charges triple in flood season. He will not wait. The ferry is a flat wooden platform guided by pole and rope, and the spring current is running fast and high. The oilskin around the papyrus is good, but the barge is low and when the current catches the rope in mid-crossing, the satchel goes over the railing.

The Fighter reaches in without thinking.

*— Cold water. The Mural Brief is not water. Pull it up.*

> **Skill Check: Athletics DC 13.** Keep the oilskin-wrapped papyrus out of the water during the barge crossing while the current tries to tear you sideways. One hand on the railing, one in the river.

**Fail:** The satchel soaks through. The oilskin holds but a corner of the papyrus is wet and blooms. The cedar-oil smell intensifies. The document survives but the final Latin line blurs slightly. Something is lost — Eudo's worst fear. The Fighter wrings out their sleeve, crosses, presses on. The archive will need to restore from context.

**Pass:** The satchel comes up dripping but the oilskin sealed. The bargeman poles them across the last thirty meters in silence. The far bank is mud and alder. The Brief is intact.

**ITEM GRANTED: The Rhine Sand** — a handful of Rhine gravel and sand pressed into a cloth from the bank — a specific physical timestamp of the crossing, sealed in the handkerchief-cloth. Not useful. Evidence of where it has been.

**ACT V**

*The Mural Brief — in satchel, slightly river-damp but sealed — at the Weimar archive intake, the desk of Archivus Sweelinck, late afternoon.*

The Archivus looks at the oilcloth. He opens it with the flat of a bone knife and reads both columns without speaking. He turns to the last line twice. He is working something out.

"I understand what this is. I do not understand what category it belongs in." He sets his pen down. "A Carthaginian priest commissioned a painting of Trojan suffering in the temple of Juno — the Trojans' divine enemy. The purpose stated is preservation of grief for the world's understanding. But this was also Juno's temple. The priest was not a friend to Troy." He looks up. "Why would an enemy do this?"

*— He doesn't need proof. He needs a category. The answer isn't a fact. It's a filing decision.*

> **Skill Check: Insight DC 12.** Read that Archivus Sweelinck is not asking a historical question. He is asking an archival one: under what heading does unwilling testimony live? The category is not "Greek Records" or "Carthaginian Records" — it is something else.

**Fail:** The Archivus files it provisionally under "Foreign Religious Commissions" and the document enters a holding queue. The Fighter waits in the anteroom while Sweelinck consults senior colleagues. Try again when he returns — he has been thinking.

**Pass:** The Fighter names it: Acts of Unwilling Witness. The records made by those who had reason not to make them. Sweelinck looks at you for a long moment. Then he writes a new subcategory header at the bottom of an existing folio and copies the title in his careful index hand. He files the document. He closes the box. He says, very quietly:

"Without them, you wouldn't be in the record."

**TOKEN TAKEN: The Mural Brief**

The document passes into the Weimar archive under a new subcategory. The oilskin wrapping is stored separately. The cedar smell persists in the intake room for several days.

## Cycle 2 — Angle: *Palinurus's Shore — The Naming That Was Sworn Before the Death*

**What this angle reveals that cycle 1 missed**: Cycle 1 showed the enemy preserving the record of their victims' suffering — grief held in hostile custody, the act of unwilling witness. Cycle 2 shows *local* administrative authority attempting to erase what the divine explicitly promised: the helmsman who died guaranteeing the fleet's safe passage received one inheritance — the Sibyl's prophecy that his headland would carry his name forever (*nomenque a te capiunt loca*). That promise has no deed, no administrator, no living enforcer. A duke in 1367 who wants his own name on the cape is counting on that silence. The document that fights erasure is older than the dispute itself: the Sibyl named the place before anyone had authority over it to rename it.

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *Palinurus's Shore*

#### The Tablet

*The Sibyl's Shore-Prophecy — wax tablet, pressed in the Sibyl's own stylus-hand, sealed in an iron case with a black wax plug — the text reads: nudus in ignota, Palinure, iacebis harena; nomenque a te capiunt loca. Naked, Palinurus, you will lie on a foreign shore; and the place will take your name. Kept for 1,387 years in the Sibyl's vault archive at Cumae, in a niche behind the oracle seat, by six successive keepers none of whom threw it away.*

---

**ACT I**

*The Sibyl's Shore-Prophecy — sealed in its iron case — on the stone shelf behind the oracle seat, Sibyl's vault archive at Cumae, morning.*

The keeper's name is Carmela. She is seventy-one, the sixth keeper in a line that has never been formally appointed — only inherited. The archive is a deep room cut into the tufa rock behind the original oracle grotto. The niche where the tablet rests is cool and dark. The black wax seal on the iron case is original. She has never opened it.

She knows what is happening at Cape Palinurus. A pilgrim told her three weeks ago: the duke of the southern province has ordered new coastal charts. The cape is now called *Promontorio del Duca*. The pilots who use the old name are being fined. She has been deciding whether to send the tablet north for nine days.

*— She is not asking whether you can carry it. She is asking whether you know what it is.*

> **Dialogue DC 12.** Tell Carmela something true about what it means to die guaranteeing a passage and have only a name as your inheritance. She does not need comfort. She needs to hear that the Fighter understands the category.

**Fail:** Carmela sets her hands flat on the case. "Not yet." She is not angry. She will be here at vespers. Try then.

**Pass:** She lifts the iron case with both hands and sets it on the stone ledge between you. She does not open it. She adds a small slip of vellum to the exterior — her conveyance note, folded under the wax plug: *carrier authorized, Carmela di Cumae, keeper, Sibyl's Archive.* "The naming was performed before anyone was available to sign. Tell the registry master that." She steps back.

**TOKEN GRANTED: Sibyl's Shore-Prophecy** — wax tablet in iron case, black wax seal, Carmela's conveyance slip folded under the exterior wax plug; text: *nudus in ignota, Palinure, iacebis harena; nomenque a te capiunt loca.*

---

**ACT II**

*The Sibyl's Shore-Prophecy — in the Fighter's satchel — at the coast road toll point, southern Italy, afternoon.*

The duke's tax officer is stationed here. His name is Filiberto. He has a list of proscribed items: anything related to the cape name dispute, anything referencing "Palinurus" or "the old helmsman's headland." He is not malicious. He is doing his job.

He sees the iron case in the satchel. He reads Carmela's conveyance note. He does not know the Sibyl's vault archive exists. He knows the seal is not commercial.

*— He is looking for a category. Give him one he cannot file as contraband.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 12.** Argue that a prophetic-religious archive document predating any current administrative authority by thirteen centuries falls outside commercial inspection jurisdiction — the duke's writ covers cartographic materials, not ancient oracle records.

**Fail:** Filiberto stamps the case *"held for review"* and sets it on his desk. The Fighter waits while he sends for guidance. The guidance takes a day. The category question works on him in the night; he releases it with an uneasy notation.

**Pass:** Filiberto reads the conveyance note twice, looks at the black wax seal, looks at the Fighter. He produces a transit slip and stamps it: *reviewed, not impounded — category: religious-archive document, pre-administrative, Sibyl's Archive, Cumae.* He attaches the slip to the exterior of the iron case. The road south is open.

**TOKEN STATE 2**: Iron case now bears Filiberto's official transit stamp on an attached slip — *reviewed, not impounded* — establishing that the document passed administrative scrutiny and was not subject to the duke's cartographic proscription.

---

**ACT III**

*The Sibyl's Shore-Prophecy — iron case with transit stamp — Cape Palinurus harbor town, evening.*

The harbor is a small port, thirty fishing boats, one tavern. The new ducal charts are already posted at the harbor master's office: *Promontorio del Duca.* The harbor pilots are meeting in the tavern. Three have been fined this week for using the old name. Their guild log uses "Capo Palinuro" going back eighty years.

The guild master is Piero. He is fifty, square-handed, has spent forty years reading this coast. He knows every current and rock around the headland. He does not know who Palinurus was.

The Fighter tells him.

*— He listens. He knows about dying to guarantee a passage. He has known pilots who did not come back.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 13.** Convince Piero to co-sign a geographic continuity attestation confirming that "Capo Palinuro" has been the continuous local name in pilots' usage, and that his guild's seal on the Sibyl's iron case constitutes a second authority for the naming. He will not sign for an argument. He will sign for the truth.

**Fail:** Piero shakes his head. "I don't sign documents for strangers." He is not hostile. He is tired. Come back in the morning after the early fishing run; he always has.

**Pass:** Piero uncaps his guild seal and presses it into the black wax of the iron case's plug alongside Carmela's slot. "We have always called it Capo Palinuro. Write that down somewhere." The seal impression sits in the wax beside the archive keeper's notation.

**TOKEN STATE 3**: Guild seal pressed into the exterior wax alongside Carmela's keeper slot — the tablet is now a dual-authority document: Sibyl's Archive and Harbor Pilots' Guild of Cape Palinurus both attest the naming.

---

**ACT IV**

*The Sibyl's Shore-Prophecy — iron case with transit stamp and dual seal — coast road north toward Naples, night.*

Two men step out of the trees a mile north of the harbor town. They are not the duke's official agents — they are the kind of men the duke uses when he does not want official records of the transaction. They want the case. They know what it contains. Someone in the harbor watched the guild seal being pressed.

*— They have orders to confiscate, not to kill. That is a limited comfort.*

> **Combat: 2 opponents — AC 13, HP 22 each.** They fight to disarm and take the satchel. They will break off if reduced below half health.

**Fail:** They get the satchel. The Fighter pursues. The road south back to the harbor is an hour; Piero will know who those men work for and where they shelter.

**Pass:** The satchel stays. Both men down or broken. The night road ahead is empty.

**TOKEN STATE 4**: Carried intact through ambush. The dual seal is undamaged.

---

**ACT V**

*The Sibyl's Shore-Prophecy — iron case, transit stamp, dual seal — Naples Maritime Registry, the desk of Ser Matteo di Napoli, morning.*

Ser Matteo receives everything with patient skepticism. He reads Carmela's conveyance note. He looks at the transit stamp. He looks at the guild seal. He opens the iron case and reads the wax tablet — slowly, his lips moving on the Latin.

He sets the tablet down.

"The Sibyl of Cumae said the place would take his name." He looks up. "That is not a deed. That is a prophecy." A long pause. "The duke's renaming order is administrative. This is prophetic-geographic record." He picks up his pen, sets it down. "These are not the same jurisdiction."

*— He is not wrong. He needs to know which one is older.*

> **Skill Check: History DC 11.** Establish that the Sibyl's naming act predates any administrative authority over the cape by thirteen centuries — making it the founding geographic record; the duke's order is a revision, not an original naming, and revisions must show the prior name as superseded rather than erased.

**Fail:** Ser Matteo files it provisionally under "Disputed Geographic Names — Pending Administrative Resolution." He consults the registry's legal counsel. Counsel agrees with the Fighter's argument. The tablet comes back to the priority queue.

**Pass:** Ser Matteo writes a new entry header at the bottom of the geographic records folio: *Divine Naming Category — prophetic geographic record, pre-administrative; naming performed before administrative authority over the named location existed.* He copies "Capo Palinuro" as the established prior name. He writes the duke's renaming as "revision, subject to prior naming precedent." He files the iron case.

He says, quietly:

"The duke can call it what he likes. The registry will show what it was called first — and by what authority."

**TOKEN TAKEN: Sibyl's Shore-Prophecy**

The iron case enters the Naples Maritime Registry under *Divine Naming Category.* Carmela's conveyance note, Filiberto's transit stamp, and Piero's guild seal are each indexed separately as chain-of-custody evidence. The cape keeps its name in the record.

### Latin and the Romance Languages — Orientation

Latin is the mother of five major modern languages: Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, French, and Romanian. When Virgil wrote *arma virumque cano* in 19 BC, he was writing in the root system that would eventually produce all of them. **Cognates** are words that share the same ancestor — Latin *caelum* (sky / heaven) becomes Italian *cielo*, Spanish *cielo*, French *ciel*, Portuguese *céu*. Latin *flamma* (flame) becomes French *flamme*, Italian *fiamma*, Spanish *llama*. Latin *mors / mortis* (death) becomes French *mort*, Spanish *muerte*, Portuguese *morte*, Italian *morte*. Latin *lacrima* (tear, as in a tear from an eye) becomes Italian *lacrima*, Romanian *lacrimă*, Spanish *lágrima*, Portuguese *lágrima* — the word barely changed in two thousand years.

When you read Virgil's Latin with a Romance language background, you are reading your own language's great-grandmother. The main structural difference is **inflection**: Latin encodes grammatical function in word-endings (*casus* — "cases") rather than in word-order. So *puer amat puellam* (the boy loves the girl) and *puellam amat puer* mean the same thing — *puer* (nominative: subject) and *puellam* (accusative: object) signal roles regardless of position. This is why Virgil can arrange words for **rhythm and emotional emphasis** in ways that English translation almost always loses. The metrical form is **dactylic hexameter** — long/short/short, long/short/short — a wave pattern that sounds like breath and water. Reading it aloud, even roughly, restores a dimension that no English version carries.

The Aeneid's Latin is highly compressed. One Latin line often requires three English lines to unpack. This is not a failing of translation — it is a property of the language. The density is the meaning.

### Grammar Lesson: *Sunt Lacrimae Rerum*
#### Book 1, line 462 — the most cited line in the Aeneid

> ***Sunt lacrimae rerum et mentem mortalia tangunt***

**Word by word:**

| Latin word | Grammatical form | English |
|-----------|-----------------|---------|
| *Sunt* | 3rd person plural present of *esse* (to be) | "there are" / "they exist" |
| *lacrimae* | nominative plural of *lacrima* (f., 1st declension) | "tears" — the subject |
| *rerum* | genitive plural of *res* (f., 5th declension) | "of things" — possessive/partitive |
| *et* | coordinating conjunction | "and" |
| *mentem* | accusative singular of *mens* (f., 3rd declension) | "the mind" — object of *tangunt* |
| *mortalia* | nominative/accusative plural of *mortalis* (adj.) | "mortal things" — subject of *tangunt* |
| *tangunt* | 3rd person plural present of *tangere* (to touch) | "touch / affect" |

**The two clauses:**

*Sunt lacrimae rerum* — "There are tears of/for things." This is not saying that someone is crying. It says the world structurally *has* tears in it — they are a property of things-as-they-are. The genitive *rerum* is famously ambiguous: it can mean "tears FOR things" (objective genitive — we weep for what we lose), "tears OF things" (partitive — things contain grief inside them), or "tears BELONGING TO things" (possessive — grief is the world's own). Virgil certainly intended all three meanings to vibrate simultaneously.

*Et mentem mortalia tangunt* — "And mortal things touch the mind." The fact that things die, that they are mortal, reaches the thinking self (*mens*) and *touches* it — *tangere* is physical, direct, immediate. Not "affect the understanding" but "lay hands on the mind."

**The cognate chain:** *lacrimae* → Italian *lacrima*, Spanish/Portuguese *lágrima*, Romanian *lacrimă* (the word has barely changed). *Tangunt* → *tangere* → English *tangible*, *tangent*, French *tangent*. *Rerum* → *res* → French *rien* (nothing: "not a thing"), English *real* (from Late Latin *realis*), Italian *roba* (stuff, things).

**How to say it, metrically:** SUNT / la-cri-MAE / RE-rum / ET / MEN-tem / mor-TAL-i-a / TAN-gunt. The pattern is dactylic hexameter: six feet, each foot a long syllable followed by two shorts (or two longs). The line has a slight pause — a *caesura* — after *rerum*, splitting it into two thoughts that the *et* then joins. Say it slowly. The line has the sound of a wave completing.

**Why this is the quest's title:** Aeneas is looking at paintings of his own people's suffering, in the temple of the goddess who helped destroy them, built by the people who were also enemies. He is comforted. The line is his explanation to himself of why this is possible: the world *has* grief as a structural feature; to see that grief remembered anywhere is to be held by the world's own property, not by a friend's mercy.

### Book 1 — *Juno's Wrath, Carthage, and the Murals*

**Historical moment:** Book 1 was written during the founding years of the Augustan regime (29–19 BC). Augustus had just ended a century of civil war and was carefully constructing the mythology of Roman destiny. Virgil's task — chosen or assigned — was to write a poem that traced Roman origins back to Troy, through Aeneas, into a genealogy that included Julius Caesar and Augustus himself. The poem is political in its DNA, but the politics produce genuine art because Virgil was not a propagandist — he was a man trying to hold the suffering and the glory of history simultaneously in one poem.

**Key phrases of Book 1:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Arma virumque cano* | Arms and the man I sing | Five words that split the poem's whole concern: *arma* (public, military, Rome) and *virum* (private, human, the cost) — the poet signs his name in the gap between them |
| *Musa, mihi causas memora* | Muse, recall to me the causes | Virgil asks the Muse to explain *why* — the poem cares about causation, not just events |
| *Tantaene animis caelestibus irae?* | Can such great anger live in heavenly minds? | The poem's first moral question; divine wrath held up for examination |
| *Dux femina facti* | A woman was leader of this enterprise | Dido's founding of Carthage; precise and respectful; not ironic in the original |
| *Sunt lacrimae rerum* | There are tears for things | The structural proposition; the most important line in the poem |
| *O socii — neque enim ignari sumus ante malorum* | O companions — for we are not ignorant of suffering before this | Aeneas rallying his men; *ignari* is the key: they are not innocents encountering grief, they are trained by it |
| *Forsan et haec olim meminisse iuvabit* | Perhaps someday it will help to remember even these things | Spoken as encouragement; believed less than it is spoken |

**Grammar note — *cano*:** The verb *canere* (to sing) is the first-person singular present: *cano* — "I sing." This is notable: Homer begins with an imperative (*"Sing, goddess"*) — the poet calls on an external voice. Virgil begins with himself. *Cano* is the author claiming the poem in the first word. The voice that follows is personal and will weep, hesitate, and grieve in ways Homer's narrator never does.

**Pivotal emotional beat:** Aeneas standing invisible inside Juno's temple, looking at the murals of his own people's destruction. The grammar of the moment is significant: *"se intexere"* — he wraps himself in the murals, in the cloud of his own invisibility. He is simultaneously present and erased. He weeps at seeing Priam pictured, Achilles, the Trojan women. The tears are not only for grief — they are for the recognition that the world kept the record without him.

**Cognate note for the murals passage:** *"videt Iliacas ex ordine pugnas / bellaque iam fama totum vulgata per orbem."* — He sees the battles of Troy set in order, and the wars now made common knowledge throughout the world. *Fama* — rumor, fame, report — enters here for the first time. Latin *fama* → Italian *fama*, French *fame* (archaic), English *fame*. Dido has heard the wars. The world has heard. Aeneas discovers he is already a story.

**Quest seed — Book 1:** ***"The Painter's Afterword"*** — When the murals were completed, the master painter who made them wrote a brief postscript to the commission, explaining what he had learned about Trojan grief from the commission's research (letters from Troy survivors, accounts from traders). This postscript was filed separately from the Mural Brief. It is found in a different cedar box, fifty miles from the first, in the hands of a Neapolitan book-dealer who doesn't read Latin. It describes a scene not in the contract: Andromache performing funeral rites at Hector's empty tomb — a thing the painter added without authorization because his source described it and he could not leave it out. *(Flag: outstanding quest seed — plan.md)*

### Book 2 — *The Fall of Troy, Told at Dido's Table*

**Historical moment:** Book 2 is formally a set-piece: Aeneas narrating the fall of Troy to Dido and her court. Virgil has Aeneas tell his own story in first person — an unusual structural choice that makes the fall of Troy felt, not witnessed from outside. The political stakes: Augustus wanted Rome to remember it was built on catastrophe, not on triumph. The founding myth requires a founding loss.

**Key phrases:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Conticuere omnes intentique ora tenebant* | They all fell silent and held their faces intent | The first line of Book 2; the court listening; the silence before grief speaks |
| *Timeo Danaos et dona ferentis* | I fear the Greeks even bearing gifts | Laocoon's warning; the most famous proverb from the Aeneid; carries the meaning "beware what looks like generosity" |
| *Crimine ab uno disce omnes* | From one crime learn them all | Aeneas summarizing the Greeks; a generalization from Sinon's lie to the entire nation |
| *Horresco referens* | I shudder telling it | Aeneas interrupts his own narration — the poet acknowledging that memory has a body |
| *Haec finis Priami fatorum* | This was the end of Priam's fates | Pyrrhus kills Priam at the altar; three words close a king's life and a civilization; *fatorum* is plural — not one fate but the whole accumulated weight of them |
| *Quid non mortalia pectora cogis, / auri sacra fames?* | To what do you not drive mortal hearts, / accursed hunger for gold? | Polydorus's death; three-word sentence of moral condemnation still in use two thousand years later |
| *Par levibus ventis volucrique simillima somno* | Like light winds and most like flying sleep | Creusa's ghost slipping through Aeneas's hands; the most beautiful description of loss being ungraspable |

**Grammar note — *horresco referens*:** A present participle (*referens* — "telling," "relating") combined with a present-tense verb (*horresco* — "I shudder"). The combination means: *right now, in the act of telling you this, I am shuddering.* The narration is not past-tense report; it is present-tense re-experience. Aeneas doesn't just remember the fall of Troy; he re-enters it every time he speaks it. The participial construction is common in Latin and carries enormous flexibility — English usually must expand it: "I shudder as I tell this."

**Pivotal emotional beat:** The loss of Creusa. Not the sack of Troy — the sack is public, historical, inevitable. The loss of Creusa is personal and accidental. She was behind him. He didn't check until too late. He goes back three times into the burning city — *ter frustra comprensa manus effugit imago* — three times the seized image escapes his hands. The ghost tells him not to grieve, that greater things await him. He tries to receive this as comfort. It is not fully received. The founding of Rome begins with a man failing to hold his wife in a burning city, and she has to release him because he won't let go.

**Cognates to note:** *Imago* (image, ghost, appearance) → Italian *immagine*, French *image*, Spanish *imagen*, English *image*. *Ignis* (fire) → Spanish *fuego*, Italian *fuoco*, French *feu*, Portuguese *fogo* (Latin *ignis* is less directly preserved but cognate through root). *Fatum* (fate) → Italian *fato*, Spanish *hado/fado*, Portuguese *fado* (the famous Portuguese word for fate/sorrow comes directly from this root — and the musical form *fado* takes its name from it).

**Quest seed — Book 2:** ***"Creusa's Third Temple"*** — Creusa's ghost says the Mother of Gods (Cybele) has chosen to keep her. Cybele's temple in Phrygia kept a register of those "held" by the goddess — people who vanished in divine custody and whose names were added to a stone tablet. A medieval monastery built over the temple site in Asia Minor holds a fragment of this tablet with Creusa's name on it (in the form *Kreusa*). The fragment is being disputed between two Greek Orthodox bishops who each claim it proves a different theological point about holy disappearance. A third party — a Franciscan pilgrim — found it in the rubble and just wants it somewhere safe. *(Flag: outstanding quest seed — plan.md)*

### Book 3 — *The Wanderings: Wrong Turns and Miniature Troys*

**Historical moment:** Book 3 is the least-read book of the Aeneid — often called episodic and slow. But it is doing something subtle: showing the Trojans making mistake after mistake (Thrace, Crete) precisely because they don't yet know where they're going. The oracle keeps saying "seek the ancient mother" and they keep misunderstanding. This is not incompetence; it's the condition of all founding — you don't know the destination until you're nearly there.

**Key phrases:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Postquam res Asiae Priamique evertere gentem / immeritam visum Superis* | After it seemed good to the gods above to overturn the affairs of Asia and the undeserving race of Priam | The opening of Book 3; *immeritam* — "undeserving" — is Virgil inserting moral judgment into the invocation; Troy didn't deserve what happened to it |
| *Auri sacra fames* | The accursed hunger for gold | Polydorus again; this phrase is so compressed it functions as a standalone sentence, a moral proverb, a verdict |
| *Vivite felices, quibus est fortuna peracta / iam sua* | Live happy, those of you for whom fortune is already completed | Aeneas's farewell to Helenus and Andromache at Buthrotum; the most precise definition of envy-that-isn't-envy in the poem — he recognizes they have *finished* whereas he has not |
| *Italiam primus conclamat Achates* | Achates first shouts "Italy!" | The first sight of Italy; *primus* — it matters who saw it first; it matters that it was Achates, the faithful companion; the relief is given a proper name and a first voice |
| *Monstrum horrendum, informe, ingens, cui lumen ademptum* | A horrible monster, shapeless, vast, whose light has been taken away | The blinded Polyphemus; this is one of Virgil's most technically brilliant lines — four adjectives across the line like a growing dread, and the fourth is not about size or shape but about the missing light; the horror is what is absent |

**Grammar note — *immeritam*:** An accusative singular feminine adjective, modifying *gentem* (race, people). Its prefix *im-* is the Latin negative (like *in-* before consonants, *im-* before m/p/b). *Merere* = to deserve, to earn. *Immerita* = the undeserving. The word is politically charged: Virgil is saying that Troy's fall was not justice. The Trojans did nothing to deserve it. This sits in tension with the rest of the poem, which shows the gods had reasons — but those reasons are never quite sufficient to the magnitude of the punishment.

**Pivotal emotional beat:** Aeneas's encounter with Andromache at Buthrotum. She has built a miniature Troy in Epirus. She is performing funeral rites at an empty green mound — a cenotaph for Hector, who has no grave here. *Viridi quem caespite inanem sacraverat aras* — a mound of green turf, empty, with sacred altars. She is performing full funeral rites for an absent body because it is the only thing she can do. When she sees Aeneas she thinks for a moment he might be a ghost. She is living inside a performance of a place that no longer exists. *Effigiem Xanthi Troiamque videtis / quam vestrae fecere manus* — you see an image of the Xanthus and a Troy that your hands have made. Aeneas says this gently, but it is also a kind of diagnosis: a copy is not a home. Andromache will never leave Buthrotum. She has chosen her grief as her geography.

**Cognates to note:** *Caespite* (from *caespes*, turf, sod) → no direct modern Romance cognate but the root is in English *cespit* (cesspit, from turf-covering of pits). *Imago* (the empty mound as an *image* of Hector) recurs here: *inanem* — empty, hollow. Latin *inanis* → Italian *vano*, Spanish *vano*, French *vain*, English *vain* (through Old French). The cognate chain: emptiness → futility → vanity.

**Quest seed — Book 3:** ***"The Buthrotum Register"*** — Andromache's empty cenotaph for Hector is a ritual site. Over the centuries, other displaced peoples performed similar rites there — empty tombs for those killed far from home with no recoverable body. A Byzantine-era priest began keeping a register of these "empty dedications" at Buthrotum (the modern Albanian town Butrint). The register was written in Greek and eventually came into the possession of a Venetian merchant-family. By 1367 it is in a private library in Venice, and the Greek Orthodox church wants it repatriated to the new Greek patriarchy in Constantinople. Fighter escorts the register. *(Flag: outstanding quest seed — plan.md)*

### Book 4 — *Dido: The Love That Cannot Be Kept and the Grief That Cannot Be Released*

**Historical moment:** Book 4 is where the poem's political project creates its most uncomfortable tension. Augustus needed Carthage to be the enemy — the Punic Wars are the defining trauma of Roman history, and Carthage was the existential threat. But Virgil wrote Dido so fully, so justly, so beautifully that she became the character everyone remembered. The political reading (Dido represents Cleopatra, oriental passion distracting the Roman from his duty) was noted in antiquity and is probably partly intended. But the literary reality overwhelms it: Dido is right. Her arguments are correct. Her grief is proportionate. She is destroyed by a process she cannot resist and a man who does not choose to destroy her.

**Key phrases:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *At regina gravi iamdudum saucia cura / volnus alit venis* | But the queen, long wounded by heavy care, nourishes the wound in her veins | The opening of Book 4; *saucia* (wounded), *volnus alit* (nourishes the wound) — love as a wound that feeds itself; not a healing but a feeding |
| *Agnosco veteris vestigia flammae* | I recognize the traces of the old flame | Dido admitting to Anna that she is falling in love; *vestigia* — traces, footprints; the old flame (Sychaeus) has left a shape in her that Aeneas fits; devastating in its precision |
| *Heu vatum ignarae mentes!* | Alas, the ignorant minds of prophets! | Dido performing sacrifices for omens while already burning; the augurs cannot see what is in front of them; religious ceremony and personal disaster occupying the same moment |
| *Uritur infelix Dido* | Unhappy Dido burns | The short central diagnosis; *uritur* is passive — she is being burned, not burning herself; she is not the agent of her own destruction |
| *Ille dies primus leti primusque malorum / causa fuit* | That day was the first of her death and the first cause of her sorrows | The cave scene; fate stated in retrospect; *leti* (of her death) — Virgil announces the ending while narrating the middle |
| *Improbe Amor, quid non mortalia pectora cogis!* | Shameless Love, to what do you not drive mortal hearts! | The narrator's interruption; Love personified and accused directly; the poem's voice breaks through |
| *Nec tibi diva parens... sed duris genuit te cautibus horrens / Caucasus, Hyrcanaeque admorunt ubera tigres* | Your mother was no goddess... the Caucasus made you from hard cliffs, Hyrcanian tigers offered you their breasts | Dido's denunciation of Aeneas; the rhetorical climax of her grief; she is saying: you are what cold things made, you have animal milk in you |
| *Italiam non sponte sequor* | I follow Italy not of my own will | Aeneas's answer; the coldest line of the poem; not "I must leave" but "I am not even choosing this" — which erases him as a moral actor and leaves Dido with no one to blame |
| *Exoriare aliquis nostris ex ossibus ultor* | Let some avenger arise from my bones | The curse; Hannibal; the Punic Wars announced in a dying woman's grief |
| *Vixi, et quem dederat cursum fortuna, peregi* | I have lived, and the course fortune gave me I have completed | Dido's last claim before falling on the sword; a closed account; she is not diminished at the end |
| *Sic, sic iuvat ire sub umbras* | Thus, thus it is sweet to go under the shades | The last words before the sword; the repetition — *sic, sic* — is the most devastating moment in the book; she drives the blade twice and the line says it twice |

**Grammar note — *agnosco veteris vestigia flammae*:** This line is the emotional center of Book 4. *Agnosco* = I recognize (again — *ag-* + *nosco*, to know). This is not new love; it is recognition of a shape she already knew. *Vestigia* = traces, footprints, marks left behind — normally used for animal tracks, footprints in snow, the physical evidence of where something has been. *Veteris* — genitive singular of *vetus* (old, established, longstanding) — "of the old." *Flammae* — genitive singular of *flamma* (flame, fire, love). The whole phrase: "the footprints of the old fire." Love leaves footprints in the soul. New love is recognizable because it fits the existing imprints. This is grief as template — and the tenderness and danger of the line is that the template was made by someone who died, and anything that fits it will re-open the grief that made it.

**Cognates in this book:** *Flamma* → Italian *fiamma*, French *flamme*, Spanish *llama* (as in Dalai Lama, also the animal — different etymology — but the fire-word is *llama* in old Spanish). *Amor* → Italian/Spanish/Portuguese *amor*, French *amour*, English *amorous*. *Veni, vidi, vici* isn't in the Aeneid but the structure of *vixi... peregi* — I lived, I completed — is the same syntax of terminal accounting.

**Pivotal emotional beat — the repeated *sic*:** Dido on the pyre, having made her final speech, presses her face into the bed (*os impressa toro*) and says: *"Moriemur inultae, sed moriamur."* "We will die unavenged — but let us die." The plural *moriamur* is startling: she is dying alone on a pyre and she says "us." She reaches for company at the moment of dying and finds only herself, compounded. Then she falls on the sword. The word *sic, sic* — "thus, thus" — as she does it. Modern scholars debate whether this means she drives it twice, or speaks the word with two breaths, or simply that the gesture requires repetition because the first attempt doesn't complete what she intends. The ambiguity is structural. Dido is never fully finished in this poem.

**Quest seed — Book 4:** ***"The Annotated Account"*** — Dido's secretary Barce (mentioned briefly — Aeneas's nurse, but Virgil says Dido summoned *Barcen nutricem* to send Anna away before the suicide — suggesting a servant who survived) was present in the palace during the final days. She wrote a first-person account of events, including a transcription of Dido's last spoken words to her sister Anna. This account was hidden in the walls of the palace during the sack of 146 BC. Modern excavation at Carthage has found it, sealed in a lead tube. A Genoese antiquarian is trying to use it as evidence that Aeneas was legally a husband under Carthaginian customary law — he wants to sue the city of Rome (symbolically) and is actually using it to settle a land dispute with the Papal States. Fighter must get the document to a neutral archive before the antiquarian's lawyers file it in a Genoa commercial court, where it will be buried in a commercial case forever. *(Flag: outstanding quest seed — plan.md)*

### Book 5 — *The Funeral Games: Community, Ceremony, and the Burning Ships*

**Historical moment:** Book 5 is a breathing space — but Virgil uses it to establish what the Trojans actually *are* as a community. The funeral games for Anchises show who is generous, who is proud, who is faithful. Nisus tripping to help Euryalus in the foot race prefigures their death together in Book 9. The burning of the ships is Juno's last attempt to strand them in Sicily, and its resolution — rain from Jupiter saving most of the fleet — shows the limits of divine interference. The colony left in Sicily (Acesta) is the first permanent Trojan settlement: people who couldn't make it all the way, given their own city.

**Key phrases:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Certamine summo / procumbunt* | In the supreme contest they bend forward | The boat-race rowers at full effort; *procumbunt* — to fall forward, to lean until you're almost falling; effort as near-collapse |
| *Non illi se quisquam impune tulisset / obvius armato* | No one would have presented himself in arms against him with impunity | Anchises's praise of the young Marcellus (spoken from the future, in Book 6 — but the sentiment echoes here); what might have been |
| *Nisus amore pio pueri* | Nisus, in devoted love for the boy | The introduction of Nisus and Euryalus; *pio* — that word again; loyal love between soldiers is *pietas* too; the same virtue that drives Aeneas to leave Dido drives Nisus to fall for Euryalus |
| *Nec lacrimas caruere genae* | Nor did his cheeks lack tears | Nisus has tripped his rival to let Euryalus win; he is not ashamed; he is weeping with something that is not shame; victory and sacrifice coexist in the same wet face |
| *Nudus in ignota, Palinure, iacebis harena* | Naked, Palinurus, you will lie on an unknown shore | Aeneas's lament as the helmsman falls overboard; *ignota* — unknown, nameless; the horror is not only death but death in a place that has no name yet; no one will know where you are |
| *Heu nimium caelo et pelago confise sereno* | Alas, you who trusted too much in calm sky and sea | Aeneas's reproach to himself for trusting the calm that let Palinurus be taken; the irony: Neptune himself promised safe passage |

**Grammar note — *amore pio*:** Two words that together carry the poem's entire moral weight. *Amor* (love, desire) and *pius* (dutiful, loyal, pious) are usually opposed in the Aeneid — Aeneas's *amor* for Dido versus his *pietas* toward destiny. Here, in the foot-race, they fuse: Nisus's love for Euryalus is described as *pius*, loyal-love rather than erotic passion. The *amor pio* pair signals that the poem's central opposition is not absolute: love and duty can be the same feeling pointing in the same direction. It will not save them — they die together in Book 9 — but it is real and it is named correctly.

**Pivotal emotional beat:** Anchises's ghost appearing at night to Aeneas in Sicily. He says: *"Ditis tamen ante infernas accede domos, et Averna per alta congressus pete, nate, meos."* — But first go down to the house of Dis, and through deep Avernus seek our meeting, son. The word *nate* — son — is Anchises's word. He uses it every time he speaks to Aeneas from the dead. In Book 2 Aeneas carried his father on his shoulders out of Troy; now the father calls him son from below the earth and tells him to come down. The direction reverses. What goes up must eventually go down — and what went down can sometimes come back.

**Cognates to note:** *Nudus* → Italian *nudo*, Spanish *desnudo*, French *nu*, Portuguese *nu*, English *nude*. *Ignotus* (unknown) → Italian *ignoto*, Spanish *ignoto*, French *inconnu* (the prefix shifts but the root *notus* = known: *ig-notus* = not-known). *Arena / harena* (sand) → Italian *arena*, Spanish *arena*, French *arène*, English *arena* (the sand of the amphitheater floor).

**Quest seed — Book 5:** ***"Palinurus's Chart"*** — The helmsman Palinurus, before he fell overboard, had been tracking their course on a wax-and-wood navigational chart — the route from Sicily to Cumae, with his own hand-calculations of stars observed and currents noted. The chart remained on the helm-seat where he left it. Aeneas took it as a navigational record and later deposited it at the Cumaean temple with a note: *"navigavit usque ad sidera"* — he navigated as far as the stars. The chart, or a copy, survives in a monastic library in Campania. A medieval sailor claims he used it to make a crossing in 1334 and wants it repatriated to a sailor's guild in Naples. The Cumaean monastery wants it returned to their collection. Fighter escorts the chart to a maritime archive in Weimar. *(Flag: outstanding quest seed — plan.md)*

### Book 6 — *The Katabasis: Descent, Vision, and the Ivory Gate*

**Historical moment:** Book 6 is the structural and thematic center of the entire poem (it sits at the midpoint of 12 books). The descent to the Underworld was the defining test of the classical hero tradition — Orpheus, Heracles, Odysseus all went and came back. Virgil uses the tradition to do something different: Aeneas's descent is not heroic in the sense of a trial overcome. It is a *visitation* — he goes to receive instruction. What he receives is the parade of Roman heroes who have not yet been born. The Underworld is a vision of the future dressed in the grammar of the past.

**Key phrases:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Bella, horrida bella, / et Thybrim multo spumantem sanguine cerno* | Wars, terrible wars, and I see the Tiber foaming with much blood | The Sibyl's prophecy; *bella* repeated — the repetition is not emphasis, it's the accumulation of a certainty that won't go away; Italy will not be easy |
| *Facilis descensus Averno; / noctes atque dies patet atri ianua Ditis* | Easy the descent to Avernus; night and day the gate of dark Dis stands open | The Sibyl's warning; *facilis* — easy, unobstructed; the gate is always open; that is the danger; what is hard is not entering but leaving |
| *Sed revocare gradum superasque evadere ad auras, / hoc opus, hic labor est* | But to recall the step and escape to the upper airs — this is the task, this is the work | The second half of the warning; *hoc opus, hic labor* — the doubled emphasis; both words mean "work" but *opus* has grandeur (a work, an achievement) and *labor* has sweat (work as effort); together they say: coming back is both grand and grinding |
| *Ibant obscuri sola sub nocte per umbram* | They went, obscure, under the lonely night, through shadow | The descent begins; *obscuri* — not just dark but obscured, uncertain, hard to see; Virgil at his most musical; this line's sound enacts its meaning |
| *Infelix Dido, verus mihi nuntius ergo / venerat exstinctam* | Unhappy Dido — so the true news had come to me that you were dead | Aeneas seeing Dido's shade; he did not know she had died when he left; the news arrives here, in the Underworld, too late to change anything; *infelix* — unlucky, fated to be unhappy, the word Virgil uses for her always |
| *Coniunx ubi pristinus illi / respondet curis aequatque Sychaeus amorem* | Where her first husband answers her cares and matches her love in return | Dido returns to Sychaeus; *aequat* — equals, matches; Sychaeus was always enough; she gave Aeneas what she had already given once, and it was taken again |
| *Discite iustitiam moniti, et non temnere divos* | Learn justice from this warning, and do not despise the gods | Phlegyas's eternal cry in Tartarus; a lesson learned too late, repeated forever as the only form it can now take |
| *Tu regere imperio populos, Romane, memento; / hae tibi erunt artes* | Roman, remember to rule the peoples with your power; these will be your arts | Anchises's instruction; *memento* is an imperative of *meminisse* (to remember) — not a description but a command; *artes* — arts, skills, practices; the word that covers both high art and practical craft; Rome's empire is given a *technique* |
| *Parcere subiectis, et debellare superbos* | Spare the subject, and defeat the proud | The other half of the command; mercy and force as a *pair*, not alternatives; the formula for imperial policy stated as two infinitives of equal weight |
| *Manibus date lilia plenis* | Give lilies with full hands | Anchises weeping for Marcellus; *plenis* — full, not sparing; pour the flowers; it is one of the most purely elegiac moments in any epic |
| *Sunt geminae Somni portae... sed falsa ad caelum mittunt insomnia Manes* | There are two gates of Sleep... but through one the dead send false dreams upward | The Ivory Gate; Aeneas exits through it; scholars have argued the meaning for two thousand years |

**Grammar note — *hoc opus, hic labor est*:** Two demonstratives pointing at the same referent: *hoc* (neuter accusative: "this thing") and *hic* (masculine nominative: "this here"). *Opus* is neuter; *labor* is masculine — the demonstratives track their nouns. The grammar is precise and the precision mirrors the meaning: *opus* (the achievement: neuter, abstract) and *labor* (the work: masculine, physical) are distinct kinds of difficulty stacked on top of each other. The Sibyl's sentence says: it is simultaneously a technical achievement and a physical ordeal to return from below. Note that *descensus* (descent) is called *facilis* (easy) but receives no demonstrative pointer — it doesn't need emphasis. The hard thing earns the double emphasis.

**Pivotal emotional beat — the Ivory Gate:** Aeneas's last act in Book 6 is the most puzzling in the poem: *"his ubi tum natum Anchises unaque Sibyllam / prosequitur dictis, portaque emittit eburna"* — Anchises sends his son and the Sibyl out through the ivory gate. The two gates of Sleep are described just above: the gate of horn, through which true shades easily pass; the gate of ivory, through which the dead send *false dreams* upward. Aeneas — living, not a shade — exits through the gate of *false dreams*. Why? The great scholars give four answers:

1. **The Servian interpretation:** Everything Aeneas was shown is a vision of the future, and the future is technically false because it hasn't happened yet. The ivory gate marks prophetic vision as conditional, not actual.
2. **The physical explanation:** Aeneas is alive and cannot exit through the horn gate reserved for the properly dead; the ivory gate is the only exit available to the living.
3. **The political reading:** Virgil, writing under Augustus, is marking the whole Augustan program as "false dreams" — a subtle dissent embedded in the poem's structure.
4. **The poetic reading:** No reading is required. The gates are the poem's final threshold; exiting through the uncertain gate is the right note to end on for a poem about a future that is both promised and contingent.

Whichever reading you take, Aeneas carries everything Anchises showed him — all those future Romans, the whole parade — through the gate that the poem itself marks as *not quite real yet*.

**Cognates to note:** *Umbra* (shadow, shade, ghost) → Italian *ombra*, Spanish *sombra*, French *ombre*, Portuguese *sombra*, English *umbrella* (from *umbra* + diminutive *-ella*, a little shadow). *Porta* (gate) → Italian *porta*, Spanish *puerta*, French *porte*, Portuguese *porta*. *Labor* → Italian *lavoro* (work), Spanish *labor*, French *labeur*, English *labor*. *Anima* (soul, breath) → Italian *anima*, Spanish *ánima/alma*, French *âme* (from *anima* → *anma* → *âme*), English *animal* (a being with *anima*).

**Quest seed — Book 6 (A):** ***"The Sibyl's Sealed Jar"*** — Helenus warned Aeneas not to let the Sibyl write the prophecy on leaves (they scatter when the door opens). But the Sibyl wrote anyway — she always wrote, regardless. A sealed clay jar was found at Cumae in 1312 by a mason repairing the cave's outer wall. Inside: five wax tablets with text in archaic Latin. The tablets describe a prophecy received by a man named "Aeneas Anchises filius" — son of Anchises. A Neapolitan cleric has been holding them in private, afraid to submit them for authentication because they describe future events that have already happened (including Augustus and Marcellus), which means the tablets are either genuine Sibylline prophecy or the world's most accomplished forgery. In 1367 there is a small college of scholars trying to authenticate them. Fighter escorts the tablets from the cleric's private library to the Weimar archive before a Dominican inquisitor who considers them dangerous can confiscate them. *(Flag: outstanding quest seed — plan.md)*

**Quest seed — Book 6 (B):** ***"Phlegyas's Inscription"*** — In Tartarus, Phlegyas cries forever: *"Discite iustitiam moniti, et non temnere divos"* — "Learn justice and do not despise the gods." Ancient writers report that this line was inscribed over the entrance to more than one Roman court of law. One such inscription — on a lintel stone from a destroyed Roman basilica near Verona — was incorporated into the foundations of a church in the 9th century. A 1367 restoration project has uncovered it. The bishop wants to re-inscribe it above the church's north door. The city magistrates want it for the courthouse. A humanist scholar wants it for the municipal library. Fighter escorts the stone fragment with a sealed provenance note from the excavation to the Weimar archive before it is cut in half to give everyone their preference. *(Flag: outstanding quest seed — plan.md)*

### Book 7 — *Opening: Arrival at the Tiber*
#### (First 250 lines only — the opening of the second half of the poem)

**Historical moment:** Book 7 begins the second half of the Aeneid — the "Iliadic" half, the books of war. Virgil invokes a new Muse here: *"tu vatem, tu, diva, mone"* — *you*, goddess, instruct the prophet. The register shifts. The first six books were Mediterranean, elegiac, the grief of loss; Books 7–12 are Italian, martial, the grief of founding.

**Key phrases (opening of Book 7):**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Heus! etiam mensas consumimus* | Hey — we're even eating the tables! | Ascanius realizing the flat bread-platters are the "tables" the Harpies cursed them to eat; the fulfillment of prophecy in a boy's observation; fate arriving as a punchline |
| *Hic domus, haec patria est* | Here is the home, here is the fatherland | Aeneas recognizing Italy as arrival; not chosen by him — recognized; *patria* comes from *pater* (father), cognate with French *patrie*, Spanish *patria*, English *patriot* |
| *Ne pete conubiis natam sociare Latinis* | Do not seek to join your daughter in marriage to Latin men | Faunus's oracle to Latinus, forbidding him to give Lavinia to a local husband; the divine instruction that opens the path for Aeneas and closes it for Turnus |

**Grammar note — *hic domus, haec patria est*:** Two demonstratives: *hic* (masculine, pointing at *domus* — home, which is grammatically feminine and takes *haec*, a gender mismatch Virgil resolves by using the neuter-for-place meaning) and *haec* (feminine, matching *patria*). The line has no verb except *est* at the end — shared by both clauses. *This home, this fatherland — is.* The verb comes last in Latin sentence fashion but also because the declaration is: they exist. They are real. They are here.

**Quest seed — Book 7:** ***"Ascanius's Broken Plate"*** — The flat bread disc that Ascanius was eating from when he said *"heus, etiam mensas consumimus"* — a roughly circular piece of hardened grain-flatbread — was, according to a tradition preserved in a 5th-century commentary on Virgil, kept as a sacred object at the Trojan colony at Lavinium for several generations before being lost. A medieval relic-collector near the site claims to have found an archaic piece of fired ceramic bread-form inscribed with the letters *mensas* (tables) in archaic Latin. It may be a genuine 6th-century BC artifact. It may be a 14th-century fake. A Franciscan scholar wants it authenticated before a Roman aristocratic family claims it as their ancestral relic. Fighter escorts it to a neutral authentication source. *(Flag: outstanding quest seed — plan.md)*

### Book 8 — *Pallanteum: The Future Rome, the Alliance, the Shield*

**Historical moment:** Aeneas sails up the Tiber to find Evander, an Arcadian king ruling a poor settlement on the hills that will become Rome — the Capitol and Palatine are just wooded ridges. Evander shows Aeneas around the site, naming future landmarks that are now just trees and cattle. Venus then asks her husband Vulcan to forge Aeneas new armor. The book ends with Aeneas lifting the completed shield, which depicts the entire future history of Rome from Romulus and Remus to Augustus at Actium — and Aeneas cannot read any of it.

**Key phrases:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Aude, hospes, contemnere opes et te quoque dignum / finge deo* | Dare, guest, to despise wealth, and shape yourself also worthy of a god | Evander welcoming Aeneas to his poor house; *aude* is imperative of *audere* — dare; Hercules slept here; poverty as the proper inheritance of the great |
| *Aurea quae perhibent illo sub rege fuere / saecula* | Those were the golden ages, they say, under that king | Evander's account of Saturn ruling over the first Italians; the golden age is always the age that preceded us |
| *Miratur rerumque ignarus imagine gaudet, / attollens umero famamque et fata nepotum* | He wonders and rejoices in the image, not knowing what it contains, lifting on his shoulder the fame and the fates of his descendants | The Shield; *ignarus* — unknowing; the most concentrated statement of Aeneas's entire position: carrier of a future he cannot see |

**Grammar note — *fata nepotum*:** *Nepotum* is genitive plural of *nepos* — grandson, descendant. Latin *nepos* → Italian *nipote*, Spanish *nieto/a*, French *neveu* (nephew), English *nephew* (from Old French *neveu* from Latin *nepos*). Also directly → English *nepotism* (the practice of favoring nephews; from Italian *nepotismo*, originally referring to popes who gave church positions to their *nipoti*). The future Aeneas carries on the shield is genitive — it belongs to his *nepotes*, not to him.

**Pivotal emotional beat — the unknowing carrier:** The shield's final image is the Battle of Actium — Augustus triumphing over Antony and Cleopatra, the world's people parading in chains. Aeneas cannot read it. Virgil says he *rejoices* in the *image* (*imagine*) — in the beauty of the thing, not the meaning. He lifts it onto his shoulder. This is the poem's purest expression of *pietas* as burden: he carries something whose content is beyond him, because carrying it is his task. The poem's central paradox compresses into one sentence.

**Cognates to note:** *Gloria* (fame, glory) → Italian *gloria*, Spanish *gloria*, French *gloire*, English *glory*. *Aetas* (age, era) → English *age* (via French *âge*, from Latin *aetatem*). *Nepotum* → English *nephew*, *nepotism*. *Arma* (weapons, but also: implements, works) → French *arme*, Spanish *arma*, Italian *arma*, English *armory*, *armor*, *arms* (heraldic).

**Quest seed — Book 8:** ***"The Shield's Cartographer"*** — The shield depicts Rome's entire future as imagery Aeneas cannot decode — the Battle of Actium, Augustus's triumph, the parade of conquered peoples. A 14th-century Italian humanist has written a systematic commentary identifying each scene on the shield with a specific historical event and a specific location in Rome. A cardinal in Rome wants the commentary suppressed: several of its identifications draw embarrassing parallels between Augustus's civil-war victims and current political figures. The humanist has died; his student is trying to get the commentary to a northern archive before the cardinal's agents seize it. Fighter escorts the manuscript from Venice to Weimar. *(Flag: outstanding — plan.md FCO-10)*

### Book 9 — *Nisus and Euryalus: The Night Raid, the Betrayal of Light*

**Historical moment:** With Aeneas away gathering allies, Turnus attacks the Trojan camp. Per Aeneas's standing orders, the Trojans hold their walls and do not engage in the field. Two young warriors, Nisus and Euryalus — inseparably devoted friends — propose a night raid through the enemy camp to reach Aeneas and report the crisis. They gain approval, slip through the lines, massacre sleeping Rutulians, and are almost home when Euryalus, wearing a stolen shining helmet, is caught by reflected moonlight. Nisus escapes, comes back, throws himself into the enemy to save Euryalus, kills the man killing his friend, and dies. In the morning, the Rutulians mount both heads on spears. Euryalus's mother, watching from the walls, collapses. The poet addresses both boys directly: *fortunati ambo*.

**Key phrases:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Est hic, est animus lucis contemptor et istum / qui vita bene credat emi, quo tendis, honorem* | Here is a spirit, a scorner of life, who rightly believes that honor — the one you are seeking — is worth buying with one's life | Euryalus, refusing to stay behind; *lucis contemptor* — contemner of light; the word for life is *lux*, light; this warrior's courage is contempt for the light |
| *Me, me adsum qui feci, in me convertite ferrum* | Me, me — I am here, I did it; turn your iron on me | Nisus revealing himself to save Euryalus; the doubled *me, me* enacts the desperation; grammar: accusative of the pronoun used as the object of the command; the most immediate cry in the poem |
| *Fortunati ambo! Si quid mea carmina possunt, / nulla dies umquam memori vos eximet aevo* | Fortunate pair! If my songs have any power, no day will ever remove you from the ages of memory | The poet's direct apostrophe; *si quid mea carmina possunt* — a conditional clause that is also a prayer; Virgil is asking his poem to do something poems do not normally promise to do |
| *Volvitur Euryalus leto, pulchrosque per artus / it cruor, inque umeros cervix conlapsa recumbit: / purpureus veluti cum flos succisus aratro / languescit moriens* | Euryalus rolls in death, and blood runs along his beautiful limbs, and his neck collapses and reclines on his shoulders: as when a purple flower cut by the plow languishes in dying | The death of Euryalus; *pulchros artus* — beautiful limbs; *purpureus flos* — the purple flower; Virgil slows time into the image of a poppy falling |

**Grammar note — *me, me adsum qui feci*:** The doubling of the pronoun *me* before the clause is not grammatically required — Virgil uses it for emotional intensity. *Me* (accusative) is the object of *convertite ferrum* (turn your iron — towards me). *Qui feci* is a relative clause identifying Nisus as the agent: "I, who did it." The sentence is technically: "[Strike] me, me — [I am] here, [I am the one] who did it — turn the iron on me." Note *adsum* = *ad-* + *sum* — I am present, I am here. The prefix *ad-* (to, toward) gives English *adjacent*, *admit*, *advance*. *Adsum* is the grammar of showing up.

**Pivotal emotional beat — the mother on the walls:** After the night raid fails, the Rutulians parade the heads of Nisus and Euryalus on spears past the Trojan camp walls. Euryalus's mother — unnamed, present because she followed her son from Troy into exile — sees the head from the ramparts and collapses in grief. She cries: "Did I cross land and sea following you for this?" She is led away. The battle goes on immediately. Her grief is given twelve lines in the middle of an epic battle, and then the battle resumes. The juxtaposition is the point. The poem doesn't pause; war doesn't pause; grief fits itself into twelve lines.

**Cognates to note:** *Nox* (night) → Italian *notte*, Spanish *noche*, French *nuit*, Portuguese *noite*, English *nocturnal* (via Latin). *Luna* (moon) → Italian *luna*, Spanish *luna*, French *lune*, English *lunatic* (once believed moon-affected). *Tenebrae* (darkness, shadows) → English *tenebrous*. *Amicus* (friend) → Italian *amico*, Spanish *amigo*, French *ami*, English *amicable*.

**Quest seed — Book 9:** ***"Euryalus's Mother's Tablet"*** — Euryalus's mother appears in the poem only at her son's death. She had no name. She had followed the Trojan army from Troy through Sicily to Italy, living in the camp, weaving, because her son was there. She is led away from the walls by two men after her collapse. A tradition preserved in a late Roman commentary on Virgil says she survived the campaign, lived to old age in the Latin countryside, and that a wax tablet she was composing when the news came — addressed to no one, never finished — was kept in a Campanian village for several generations. A small monastery near Cumae preserves a wooden writing tablet, pre-Roman in style, found in a wall during repairs, with faint scratching that a scholar cannot fully read. Fighter escorts the tablet from the monastery to Weimar for examination, before the monastery is demolished to build a new road for a noble estate. *(Flag: outstanding — plan.md FCO-11)*

### Book 10 — *The Return of Aeneas / Pallas / Lausus and Mezentius*

**Historical moment:** Jupiter opens Book 10 with a divine council — Juno and Venus argue; he silences both with a famous declaration of divine neutrality (*fata viam invenient* — the fates will find a way). Aeneas returns with the Etruscan fleet. Young Pallas — Evander's son, entrusted to Aeneas — fights and is killed by Turnus, who strips his sword belt. Aeneas rages in revenge. Then the book's most morally complex moment: Aeneas wounds the tyrant Mezentius; Mezentius's son Lausus throws himself between them, heroically protecting his disgraced father with his body; Aeneas kills Lausus; seeing the boy's pallid face, Aeneas is overcome by the reflection of his own filial love and weeps. Mezentius, learning his son is dead, mounts his horse and rides to die beside him.

**Key phrases:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Fata viam invenient* | The fates will find a way | Jupiter ending the divine debate; three words; the most compressed statement of the poem's theological position; not reassurance but abdication of the question |
| *Stat sua cuique dies, breve et inreparabile tempus / omnibus est vitae: sed famam extendere factis, / hoc virtutis opus* | Each person's day stands fixed; brief and not-to-be-recovered is the time of life for all: but to extend fame through deeds — this is the work of valor | Jupiter comforting Hercules who cannot save young Pallas; *stat* — stands fixed; *inreparabile* — cannot be repaired or recovered; *fama* via *factis* — fame through deeds; this is the only extension of a short life |
| *Nescia mens hominum fati sortisque futurae / et servare modum, rebus sublata secundis* | The mind of men, unknowing of fate and of the fortune to come, cannot keep to the proper measure, when raised up by prosperity | Virgil's own aside after Turnus strips Pallas's belt; the narrator speaking directly; *nescia* — unknowing, the same root as *ignorans*; Turnus's error is already sealed in the object he has taken |
| *Ora modis Anchisiades pallentia miris* | The face of the son of Anchises [seeing] a face pallid in wondrous ways | Aeneas seeing Lausus dying; *pallentia* — becoming pale; the face of Lausus dying echoes the face of Anchises — his father — in Aeneas's memory; the grammar does the work of grief |

**Grammar note — *Nescia mens hominum fati*:** *Nescia* is an adjective meaning "not-knowing" — from *ne-* (not) + *scire* (to know). *Scire* gives: Italian *sapere/scire*, Spanish *saber*, French *savoir*, English *science*, *conscious*, *prescient*. *Nescia mens* — an unknowing mind — takes *fati* as an objective genitive: "unknowing of fate." Note how Virgil places the blame not on Turnus's wickedness but on the *structural* limitation of human minds — we cannot keep measure when things go well. *Sublata secundis* — lifted up by prosperity — the passive participle *sublata* from *tollere* (to lift), which gives English *exalt*, *extol*, *tolerate* (bearing a burden lifted onto you).

**Pivotal emotional beat — Aeneas over Lausus:** When Aeneas kills Lausus, he sees the boy's face turning pale and is struck by how much it resembles what he himself does — *pietas*, protection of a father. He says: "What shall I, what shall dutiful Aeneas give you, worthy of such a spirit? Keep your arms, since you delighted in them, and I send you back to the shades of your ancestors." Then he lifts the body from the ground himself. This is the poem's most morally complicated moment: the hero weeping over a boy he just killed, recognizing himself in the enemy. The war is not simple. It never was.

**Cognates to note:** *Fatum* (fate, oracle, spoken decree) → Italian *fato*, Spanish *hado*, French *fatalité*, English *fatal*, *fate*, *fatalism*. From *fari* (to speak): *fatum* is literally "the thing spoken" — the divine decree. *Virtus* (valor, excellence, manly quality) → Italian *virtù*, Spanish *virtud*, French *vertu*, English *virtue*, *virtuoso*. *Mors/mortis* (death) → Italian *morte*, Spanish *muerte*, French *mort*, English *mortal*, *mortuary*, *immortal*.

**Quest seed — Book 10:** ***"The Sword Belt of Pallas"*** — Turnus strips the sword belt of Pallas after killing him and wears it as a trophy. The belt is decorated with carved imagery of a famous mythological murder scene. This object reappears at the poem's final moment: Aeneas sees the belt on Turnus's shoulder and kills him rather than sparing him. A 14th-century metalworker in Florence has found, embedded in old masonry, a bronze fitting decorated in archaic style with a scene of a wedding banquet turning violent. He believes it is Roman work from the first century, possibly a surviving piece of luxury armor. A collector wants it as a relic of the poem. A cathedral chapter wants it as evidence of classical knowledge preserved in their city. A humanist thinks it belongs in an archive with proper documentation. Fighter escorts it to the Weimar archive before rival interests split it up. *(Flag: outstanding — plan.md FCO-12)*

### Book 11 — *The Funeral of Pallas / The Great Council / Camilla*

**Historical moment:** Book 11 opens with Aeneas preparing Pallas's body to be sent home to Evander. He wraps it in purple-and-gold cloth that Dido had made with her own hands as a gift for Aeneas — the cloth traveled from Carthage to Troy to Italy, and now becomes a young man's funeral shroud. Evander's grief. Then the Latin council: Drances attacks Turnus; Turnus defends himself brilliantly. Then the book's second half: Camilla, the Volscian warrior queen, raised in the wilderness by her exiled father, dedicated to Diana, leading cavalry brilliantly. She is killed by Arruns — a javelin thrown from ambush while she is distracted by the gold armor of a Phrygian priest. She dies whispering to her companion Acca: "Tell Turnus." Diana avenges her through her nymph Opis. Her last line: *vitaque cum gemitu fugit indignata sub umbras.*

**Key phrases:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Nulla salus bello* | There is no safety in war | Drances in the council; the most direct anti-war statement in the poem; three words; *salus* — salvation, safety, health, the word that gives Spanish *salud*, Italian *salute*; to say there is no *salus* in war is to say it brings neither health nor rescue |
| *Multa dies variisque labor mutabilis aevi / rettulit in melius* | Many a day and the shifting labor of changing time has brought many things back toward the better | Turnus's counter-argument; *mutabilis* — changeable; the classic rhetorical response: fortune turns; what seems like defeat is only one day |
| *Vitaque cum gemitu fugit indignata sub umbras* | And her life with a groan fled indignant beneath the shadows | The death of Camilla; *indignata* — indignant, feeling it unjust; life itself is offended by its own ending; this same line closes the entire poem, at Turnus's death |
| *Hactenus, Acca soror, potui* | This far, Acca, sister, I was able | Camilla's last words; *hactenus* — up to this point, as far as this; the most compressed statement of a life cut off; she is not describing death, she is describing a limit reached |

**Grammar note — *vitaque cum gemitu fugit indignata sub umbras*:** *Indignata* is a perfect passive participle used as an active participle — a quirk of Latin deponent verbs (verbs that look passive but function actively). *Indignor* (I feel indignation) is deponent: its perfect participle *indignata* means "having been indignant" but acts as "feeling indignant." Life fled *indignata* — life fled feeling wronged. This is not translation possible in English without awkwardness; the grammar says *life itself* has an emotion. *Dignus* (worthy) is the root: *in-digna-ta* means "feeling she/it is not treated as worthy." Cognates: English *indignant*, *dignity*, *disdain* (from Latin *dedignari*), *deign* (to consider worthy), *condign* (deserved).

**Pivotal emotional beat — Dido's cloth:** In the middle of Aeneas preparing Pallas's funeral, Virgil mentions that the purple-and-gold garment wrapped around the body was made by Dido with her own hands — a gift she gave Aeneas in Carthage, *laeta laborum*, happy in her labor. This cloth has traveled from Carthage, where Dido wove it, to the Italian battlefield, where Pallas died, and now wraps a boy Dido never met. The cloth is the poem's most precise object of accumulated grief: the work of a dead woman's hands, now in service to another death. It is never returned; it burns on the pyre.

**Cognates to note:** *Pax/pacis* (peace) → Italian *pace*, Spanish *paz*, French *paix*, English *peace* (via Old French *pais*), *pacify*, *pacific*. *Bellum* (war) → *bellicose*, *belligerent*, *duel* (from *duellum*, archaic Latin for *bellum* between two). *Signum* (sign, standard, signal) → Italian *segno*, Spanish *señal/signo*, French *signe*, English *sign*, *signal*, *significant*, *design*.

**Quest seed — Book 11:** ***"Acca's Message"*** — Camilla's last act is whispering her final instructions to her companion Acca. Acca then runs to find Turnus and delivers the message that changes the entire tactical situation. Acca is one of the poem's most invisible figures: she appears only to receive dying words and relay them. A carved votive stone from Apulia (southern Italy), dated to the first century BC, was found in 1341 near Arpi (a city mentioned in the Aeneid) naming a woman called Acca as a "bearer of final messages" to a military commander. A humanist scholar in Naples believes Virgil knew this inscription and modeled his Acca on a real person. The stone has been removed from its site and is in the possession of a noble family who want to sell it. Fighter escorts the stone — or a careful rubbing of it — from Apulia to the Weimar archive before it is sold to a foreign collector who will not allow access to scholars. *(Flag: outstanding — plan.md FCO-13)*

### Book 12 — *The Final Duel: Turnus, Aeneas, and the Weight of Memory*

**Historical moment:** Book 12 is the end. Turnus insists on single combat. The truce is arranged, then broken by the crowd — Turnus's divine sister Juturna orchestrates it. Aeneas is wounded by an anonymous arrow (its author never identified). Venus heals him. Aeneas tells Ascanius: *disce, puer, virtutem ex me verumque laborem* — learn valor from me, and true labor; learn luck from others. Aeneas and Turnus fight. Turnus's sword shatters (he grabbed the wrong sword in the chaos — his own charioteer's sword, not the Vulcan-made blade). Juno finally relents; Jupiter extracts her consent: the Trojans and Latins will merge, but under Latin language and customs — Troy dies, Rome is born. Jupiter sends a Fury to drive off Juturna. Turnus is cornered, wounded in the thigh. He falls to one knee. He begs mercy. Aeneas hesitates — almost relents — sees Pallas's sword belt on Turnus's shoulder; kills him. *Vitaque cum gemitu fugit indignata sub umbras.*

**Key phrases:**

| Latin | Literal | Spirit |
|-------|---------|--------|
| *Disce, puer, virtutem ex me verumque laborem, / fortunam ex aliis* | Learn, boy, valor from me and true labor; learn luck from others | Aeneas to Ascanius before the final battle; the most direct transfer of values in the poem; *virtutem* — not just courage but excellence; *verum laborem* — the true labor, not heroic glamor but real work; luck you can only learn by watching it happen to others |
| *Iam iam fata, soror, superant; absiste morari* | Now, now the fates prevail, sister; stop delaying | Turnus to Juturna, his immortal sister, when he realizes the Fury has come for him; *iam iam* — the doubled *iam* (now, now) is among Virgil's most characteristic devices for depicting urgency; the moment of surrender to what cannot be changed |
| *Equidem merui nec deprecor* | I have deserved it and I do not contest it | Turnus's plea to Aeneas; *merui* — I earned it; *deprecor* — I do not pray against it, I do not argue against it; the rarest form of courage in the poem: admitting the verdict |
| *Vitaque cum gemitu fugit indignata sub umbras* | And his life with a groan fled indignant beneath the shadows | The final line; the poem ends here; the same line used for Camilla; a woman warrior and a male warrior die the same death; Virgil's last word on the cost of the Aeneid |

**Grammar note — *disce, puer, virtutem ex me verumque laborem*:** *Disce* is the imperative of *discere* (to learn). *Puer* is vocative — "O boy." *Virtutem* and *laborem* are both accusative, both objects of *disce* — both things to be learned. *Ex me* — from me, from my example; *ex aliis* — from others, from their example. The sentence teaches by its own grammar: Aeneas is specific about what he has to offer (*virtus* and *labor*) and modest about what he cannot give (*fortuna*, which no one commands). *Discere* → Italian *discepolo* (disciple), English *disciple*, *discipline*, *learn* itself (via Germanic, but cognate to *discere*'s Indo-European root).

**Pivotal emotional beat — the sword belt and the end:** Turnus is on one knee, wounded, begging for his life. He invokes Aeneas's own dead father: "if your own father Anchises means anything to you, pity my father's old age." Aeneas pauses. Virgil makes him pause — *volvens oculos, dextramque repressit* — rolling his eyes [over the situation], restraining his right hand. Then he sees the belt. The sword belt Turnus stripped from Pallas and has been wearing in triumph. All of Aeneas's grief for Pallas floods back. He says: "Pallas kills you with this wound, Pallas takes payment from guilty blood." And kills him. The poem does not end with victory. It ends with the recognition that grief, once created, does not resolve — it waits in the objects of the world for the moment it can act again. Pallas's belt had been waiting on Turnus's shoulder since Book 10.

**Cognates to note:** *Furor* (rage, madness, divine fury) → Italian *furore*, Spanish *furor*, French *fureur*, English *fury*, *furious*. *Anima* (soul, breath, life) → Italian *anima*, Spanish *alma* (contracted), French *âme*, English *animal*, *animate*, *unanimous* (*unum animus* — one spirit). *Pietas* (duty to family, gods, country) → Italian *pietà*, Spanish *piedad*, French *piété*, English *piety*, *pity* (via Old French *pité*; pity began as a religious word — the capacity to feel what is owed to the gods and the dead).

**Quest seed — Book 12:** ***"The Fragment of the Final Line"*** — The Aeneid ends on the same line used for Camilla's death three books earlier: life with a groan fled indignant beneath the shadows. Ancient scholars noted this: the poem ends on an echo, not a resolution. A tradition says Virgil intended to revise the final scenes but died before doing so. A manuscript at Monte Cassino includes a marginal notation in a 12th-century hand, claiming to preserve an alternate version of the poem's final six lines, citing a lost 3rd-century commentary as its source. In the marginal alternate ending, Turnus speaks three words after being struck, before dying. The notation has created a heated dispute among a small circle of scholars in 1367: some argue it is a genuine survival; others say it is a medieval fabrication. The Monte Cassino abbot wants the dispute settled quietly before it draws attention from the curia. A humanist scholar who believes the alternate ending is genuine needs the manuscript transported to a neutral archive for examination. Fighter escorts the Monte Cassino fragment to Weimar, where the Archivus will not be pressured by either faction. *(Flag: outstanding — plan.md FCO-14)*

## FCO-02 — The Painter's Afterword

**Theme differentiation:** Cycle 1 (Sunt Lacrimae Rerum) showed grief preserved by enemies — the Trojans' suffering held in Carthaginian custody, witnessed involuntarily. Cycle 2 (Palinurus's Shore) showed a divine promise outlasting every human administrator. This seed enters the painting itself: the artist inside the frame of cycle 1's central image. Those murals were made by someone. That someone decided each morning whether to continue. His account of that decision is what this vignette carries — not grief itself, but the testimony of the craftsman paid to make grief visible to strangers. **Theme avoided from active threads:** all four active themes (grief-as-identity, long watch, grief-in-object, honor-vs-shame) plus all prior FCO angles. **New theme:** *The paid witness carries the document that makes the payment ethical; the category that receives it did not exist before it arrived.*

---

### Five-Act Vignette Play

*TOKEN: The Punic Painter's Account — a parchment document written in a hand that mixes late Latin with Aramaic characters for personal names. The painter's record of his commission: what the Punic priest asked for, what he painted, what he noticed about himself during the work, and one final sentence about what it means to be paid to depict grief. Rolled inside a bone cylinder sealed with wax pressed by a thumb.*

---

**ACT I — The Cylinder**

*The Punic Painter's Account, in a bone cylinder, unread in three centuries · held by Brother Anselm, monastery librarian · a Benedictine house on the Tunisian coast, the Hafsid plain behind it, afternoon heat, Byzantine mosaics in the cloister*

The Genoese factor has been at the gate for two days. He arrived with a letter of introduction and a purchase price written on a card tucked inside it. Brother Anselm has not said yes. Careful men say no slowly, with qualifications, until the better offer clarifies itself.

The fighter's Weimar commissioning letter is general — "historical documents of classical provenance." The factor's letter is specific: title, provenance, price. General authority arrived first. Specific money arrived more recently. Brother Anselm is deciding which matters more.

Outside the gate, the factor's hired man has been watching the cloister courtyard for two hours.

*The fighter understands: this is not about money. It is about who the librarian trusts to carry a thing that has waited three hundred years for the right hands.*

---

**ACT II — The Manifest**

*The Account, sealed in its cylinder, inside the fighter's coat · entered in the ship's cargo manifest as "unspecified ecclesiastical materials" · a Genoese trading vessel, the Strait of Sicily, two nights out of Tunis, cold stars*

The captain's second officer arrives at the fighter's cabin door at midnight. He is not hostile — he is curious in the way of men who have learned that curiosity is safer than ignorance when a vessel carries things incompletely understood.

He has heard something: the factor was left on the dock at Tunis, whatever came off the monastery road is now aboard, and it was worth abandoning a Genoese commercial agent to secure it. He wants to know what level of trouble he has agreed to carry.

*The fighter knows: he is not asking to see it. He is asking to be told enough to be careful in the right direction.*

---

**ACT III — Giovanni**

*The Account, unrolled once to confirm condition, rerolled and resealed · on a table in a Naples tavern near the port, morning · a painter named Giovanni sitting across from the fighter, hands in his lap*

Giovanni paints the interiors of Campanian churches — grief-scenes, mostly: the Lamentation, the Massacre of the Innocents, the Pietà. He has been doing this for twenty years. He heard about the account through the factor's Naples agent.

He says at once, unprompted, that he has not come to take it. He says it with the precision of a man who knows this will be doubted. What he wants: the last sentence. The one where the painter recorded what it was like to be paid to depict grief. Giovanni has been painting grief for twenty years without that sentence, and he would like to know whether anyone else found it as difficult as he has.

He does not ask to hold the cylinder. He says: *If you read it — read me that part.*

*The fighter has been carrying something in a closed fist for three days. Giovanni has been carrying something in his hands for twenty years. The fighter understands, with unexpected precision, what is being asked.*

---

**ACT IV — The Friar**

*The Account, sealed, in jurisdictional dispute · in the anteroom of the Dominican priory of San Pietro Martire, Naples · afternoon, tile floor, a young friar with a clean preliminary inquiry on the table*

The friar has filed a tribunal inquiry: "whether material dignifying pagan religious worship requires review prior to archive transfer." He is not wrong. The painter recorded that the Punic priest's instructions were holy — in the sense that holiness belongs to a man who believes — and that carrying them out changed something in the painter. That is pagan dignification. The inquiry is procedurally clean.

He does not want it burned. He wants it filed under a category the archive cannot independently receive — where the Church's review office must concur. He believes controlling the category is the same as controlling the truth. In many institutions, he is not wrong.

*The fighter knows the argument: a professional record of a commission is not a religious document. The painter is not worshipping. He is working. The category "artist's testimony" must exist before this friar's inquiry can fail.*

---

**ACT V — The New Register**

*The Account, delivered whole · on the intake desk of the Weimar archive · late afternoon, the city quiet outside*

The archivist reads the Account from beginning to end without speaking. He reads the last sentence twice. He sets it down and is quiet.

Then he opens a clean register. He writes at the top: *Artist's Testimony — Ethics of the Commission.* No such category existed in the archive this morning. He is making it.

He looks at the fighter. "Did someone try to reclassify this?" He is asking because the answer goes in the register.

The fighter tells him everything — the friar, the inquiry, the Naples argument, the lapsed notice.

The archivist writes for two minutes. When he closes the register, the Account is filed under a category that did not exist an hour ago, with the Naples inquiry noted in the archivist's own hand.

*The Account is in the archive. The category that receives it is new. The fighter carried not just the document but the argument that made it receivable. Both facts are true, and the second is the one the painter would have wanted.*

## FCO-04 — The Buthrotum Register

**Theme differentiation:** FCO-01 showed grief held in hostile custody. FCO-02 showed the craftsman who was paid to make grief visible. This seed enters the third form: grief organized into governance. Helenus built a miniature Troy at Buthrotum and administered it — issued founding laws, took a census. The register is the document of that administration. Its population roll names people who chose to live in a place that existed only because Troy was destroyed. **New theme:** *The replica community's founding document is the record of a refusal to accept destruction as final; the population roll is proof that some griefs are organized rather than carried.*

---

### Five-Act Vignette Play

*TOKEN: The Buthrotum Register — an 11th-century Byzantine parchment codex, 23 folios, Greek minuscule. Records the founding laws and population roll of Helenus's Buthrotum. The Byzantine scholar's marginal annotations note where the text agrees with Virgil and where it departs. The final folio: a population roll — names, families, occupations of people who chose to live in a replica of the city they lost. Bound in brown leather, the spine worn soft. Carried in a linen wrapping.*

---

**ACT I — The Misfiled Codex**

*The Register, in a linen wrapping, shelved under "Byzantine administrative documents — provenance unknown" · held by the Venetian archive, Ca' del Consiglio collection · a vaulted room above the canal, afternoon light through high windows, the smell of old vellum and tallow*

A young archive clerk named Catarina flagged the codex three weeks ago when she read enough Greek to recognize that the founding laws cited in the margins were not Byzantine at all — too old, too local, citing a community that no Byzantine official would have administered. She has requested a specialist review. The specialist has not yet come. A Venetian merchant family has submitted a purchase inquiry in the interval, citing commercial interest in Adriatic properties.

The purchase order goes to the archive director tomorrow morning. Tonight, Catarina is still at her desk.

*The fighter understands: the codex is technically available until the director countersigns the purchase order. Catarina knows this. She is still at her desk because she knows this.*

---

**ACT II — The Scholar's Margin**

*The Register, in the fighter's satchel, wrapped in linen · on the deck of an Adriatic coaster at dawn, three hours out of Venice, heading south*

A Greek scholar named Dimitrios Palaiologos — not of the imperial family, merely named for it — has been searching for the Buthrotum Register for fifteen years. He recognized the fighter's satchel from the archive courtyard. He is not going to take it. He is going to stand at the rail and tell the fighter, quietly, that there is a marginal annotation on folio 17 where the Byzantine scribe disagreed with Virgil about the layout of Helenus's court — and that he has been arguing in print for six years that Virgil's geography was wrong, and the answer to whether he was right is, at this moment, seven feet away inside a linen wrapping.

He asks for nothing. He has simply been searching for fifteen years and the object of that search is close enough to touch, and he is a man who believes in saying things plainly.

*The fighter looks at the sea. The fighter looks at the scholar. The register is not the fighter's to share permanently. But folio 17 is a page, not the whole.*

---

**ACT III — The Prior Claim**

*The Register, examined and rewrapped · on the Ragusa waterfront at midday · a man waiting beside a stack of crates who introduces himself as Brother Nicodemus, representing the monastery of the Holy Archangels, Butrint*

The monastery was the register's custodian for four hundred years before Venetian agents took it during a period of Adriatic disruption — Brother Nicodemus says "disruption" with the measured neutrality of a man who has described this event many times to people who were involved in it. He has a letter from his abbot. He has a chain of custody record. He has, in other words, a legitimate claim.

He does not want money. He wants the register returned to the place it was made — to a monastery overlooking the ruins of the city it documents, where the monks can read it as a record of their own community's ancient predecessor.

His claim is real. The Weimar commission is also real. The register cannot go to both places.

*The fighter must explain why a neutral archive serves the register better than local custody that was already disrupted once. It is a harder argument than it sounds.*

---

**ACT IV — The Name in the Roll**

*The Register, rewrapped after Ragusa, carried through the Dinaric passes toward the north · a mountain road, late afternoon, mist in the pines*

## FCO-05 — Barce's Account

**Theme differentiation:** FCO-01 showed grief held in hostile custody. FCO-02 showed the paid craftsman. FCO-04 showed grief become governance. This seed enters the fourth position: the survivor. Barce was the only person present throughout who lived to old age and chose to speak. *The fighter does not object. Barce did not dictate this account to have it filed quietly.*

## FCO-07 — The Sibyl's Sealed Jar

**Theme differentiation:** FCO-01: hostile custody. FCO-02: the paid craftsman. FCO-04: grief as governance. FCO-05: survivor contradicts the monument. This seed occupies the fifth position: the genuinely dangerous document. The cleric who has held the tablets for fifty years is not afraid they are false — he is afraid they are real. If the Sibyl correctly named Augustus and Marcellus, the prophetic tradition was authentic. *The fighter knows what the third category must be. The archivist knows too. He is waiting to be told to make it.*

## FCO-09 — Ascanius's Broken Plate

**Theme differentiation:** FCO-01: hostile custody. FCO-02: paid craftsman. FCO-04: grief as governance. FCO-05: survivor contradicts the monument. FCO-07: fulfilled prophecy. This seed occupies the sixth position: the object that proves a moment of recognition. The bread-platter is not proof of the destination — it is proof of the instant someone understood they had arrived. **New theme:** *The object that witnessed a recognition is different from the object that caused it; what the plate proves is not Aeneas's landing but Ascanius's understanding; the relic of comprehension is rarer than the relic of event.*

---

### Five-Act Vignette Play

*TOKEN: The Mensas Plate — a fired ceramic disk, slightly cracked along the left edge, inscribed with the single word MENSAS in archaic Latin capitals. Found near the ruins of Lavinium in 1361 by a relic collector named Orso di Palestrina. Orso has been holding it for six years while three parties dispute ownership. The disk is a bread plate — the right size and weight for one used at a meal. The word is written on the underside, as if inscribed after the fact, by someone who wanted the object to know what it meant.*

---

**ACT I — Orso's Problem**

*The Plate, wrapped in linen, in a wooden box under Orso di Palestrina's bed · a room above a relic-dealer's shop near the Lateran, Rome · late morning, the smell of old bone and wax*

Orso found the plate in 1361 at a site near Lavinium — a low field, some old foundation stones, a scatter of ceramic shards. He has been trying to sell it for six years and has instead accumulated three simultaneous claims and a growing fear that the plate is worth more trouble than any single buyer is worth.

The Roman aristocratic family of the Rufini claims descent from Ascanius through the Julian line and believes the plate is their ancestral property. A cardinal's antiquities office wants it for a new museum of classical relics. A Florentine humanist named Cosimo Bardi has published two papers arguing the inscription is 4th-century BC and genuine, and believes it belongs in a neutral scholarly archive.

Orso wants it out of his house. He will release it to anyone who can resolve one specific problem: if he gives it to one party, the other two will sue him. He needs a release that all three acknowledge.

*The fighter understands: Orso's problem is not the plate. It is the three claims. The Weimar commission supersedes all three only if the fighter can demonstrate that to each claimant.*

---

**ACT II — The Rufini Heir**

*The Plate, still under Orso's bed · in the Rufini palazzo, Trastevere · afternoon, good furniture, a young man who has been told this is his birthright*

Giovannino Rufini is twenty-three. He knows the family claim by recitation — he could not tell the fighter where Lavinium is without checking a map. He has been told the plate is an ancestral relic, which he believes, and he wants it, which is understandable, and he has also been told by his family's lawyer that the legal claim is solid, which it is not. The family's lineage documentation goes back to the 9th century, which does not establish a property right to a ceramic object found in a field in 1361.

He is not a bad man. He simply has a family claim and a lawyer who told him it was sufficient.

*The fighter must explain, with enough courtesy that Giovannino is not humiliated, that "ancestral relic" and "legal property" are not the same category — and that an archive benefits his family's claim to the lineage by preserving the object publicly under the name of the event.*

---

**ACT III — The Cardinal's Curator**

*The Plate, still under Orso's bed · in the cardinal's antiquities office, near the Vatican · morning, a man with excellent taste and institutional authority*

Monsignor Ferrante runs the cardinal's new museum program. He is intelligent, reasonable, and in possession of a letter from the cardinal's office that constitutes a formal acquisition request to any holder of the plate. He is also the third person Orso called, which means he knows about the Rufini claim and the Weimar commission.

He makes the fighter a direct offer: the cardinal's museum will list the Weimar archive as co-custodian, publish Cosimo Bardi's authentication papers alongside the display, and give the fighter a letter releasing all three claims — in exchange for the plate going to Rome rather than Weimar.

It is a good offer. It is not what the commission says.

*The fighter must decline the offer and explain why the archive's neutrality is worth more to the plate than a beautiful room in a cardinal's museum — and why "co-custodian" with an institution that can override the arrangement is not the same as neutral custody.*

---

**ACT IV — Cosimo Bardi**

*The Plate, finally in the fighter's hands, out of Orso's house · in Cosimo Bardi's rented study near the Pantheon · evening, books on every surface, a man who has been waiting six years*

Cosimo Bardi published his first authentication paper in 1362, his second in 1364. He has been cited, disputed, praised, and ignored. He has not seen the plate in person — he worked from Orso's description and a rubbing. He is fifty-four and he is very calm about the fighter being in his study with the plate under one arm, because he spent six years preparing for this possibility and he knows exactly what he wants to say.

He says: "The word is written on the underside. Not on top, where a diner would see it. On the underside, where only someone who turned it over after the meal would find it." He says: "Someone inscribed it afterward. Not to mark the plate — to mark the moment. The plate itself didn't know what it meant while the meal was happening."

He holds the plate — the fighter lets him hold it, briefly — and looks at the word.

"The underside inscription means someone understood after the fact. Someone turned the plate over and wrote what it was. That person is the document. The plate is just the surface they wrote on."

*The fighter receives this. It is the most important thing anyone has said about the plate in six years.*

---

**ACT V — What the Plate Is**

*The Plate, carried north to Weimar · on the archive intake desk, morning · the archivist reading Cosimo's two authentication papers side by side with the physical object*

The archivist turns the plate over. He reads the underside. He reads Cosimo's papers. He is quiet for a long time.

"I could file this as: Aenean relic, Lavinium provenance, genuine. That is the obvious category." He sets it down. "But what Bardi argues — that the inscription was added after the meal by a witness — means the object's value is not the meal. It is the inscription. It is someone's decision to write *mensas* on the bottom of a plate so it would know what it had been."

He looks at the fighter. "Where does an object go that was inscribed by someone who understood a moment after it occurred? That is a different category than relic."

*The fighter knows: Cosimo said it already. The plate is the surface the understanding was written on. The category the archivist needs is: record of comprehension, not record of event.*

## FCO-14 — The Fragment of the Final Line

**Theme differentiation:** The six prior FCO vignettes cover: hostile custody, the paid craftsman, the replica city, the survivor's contradiction, fulfilled prophecy, and the relic of comprehension. This seed occupies the seventh position: the suppressed voice. The Aeneid ends with Aeneas striking Turnus while Turnus is already pleading — the most debated ending in classical literature. A margin annotation in a Monte Cassino manuscript preserves three words Turnus speaks after being struck, citing a lost commentary. **New theme:** *The alternate ending that survived in a margin is the document that decides whether the epic ends in justice or in fury; the three words are never quoted, only witnessed; what they do to everyone who reads them is the evidence.*

---

### Five-Act Vignette Play

*TOKEN: The Monte Cassino Fragment — a single folio of parchment, cut from a larger manuscript, containing a margin annotation in a hand different from the main text. The annotation reads: "Serv. comm. lib. III deperdit." followed by six lines of verse. Three of those words are what Turnus speaks after being struck. No one who has read them has described them the same way twice. All of them have been unable to forget them. Carried in a sealed letter-fold, the folio pressed flat inside.*

---

**ACT I — The Abbot's Quiet Problem**

*The Fragment, sealed in the letter-fold, in the Monte Cassino scriptorium safe · the abbot, Father Benedetto Aiello, sitting across from the fighter at a plain table · Monte Cassino hilltop, late morning, wind at the windows*

The annotation was found four months ago by a novice transcribing an inventory of the manuscript collection. The novice showed it to the librarian. The librarian showed it to the humanist Piero della Rocca, who had been visiting the monastery for three weeks studying Virgil manuscripts. Piero has since published a letter — widely circulated among Florentine humanists — stating that he has seen evidence of an alternate ending to Book XII and requesting neutral authentication.

The abbot did not authorize this letter. He is not angry about it. He is worried. If the fragment is genuine, the monastery has been inadvertently harboring a challenge to the canonical Aeneid for an unknown number of centuries. If it is forgery, someone forged it in the Monte Cassino scriptorium. Neither outcome is comfortable.

He wants it out of his house and in a place that can absorb the controversy without requiring him to take a position.

*The fighter understands: the abbot is not trying to suppress the fragment. He is trying to hand the problem to an institution better equipped to hold it.*

---

**ACT II — The Cardinal's Brief**

*The Fragment, in the fighter's satchel, letter-fold sealed · on the road south toward Naples, afternoon · a courier at a crossroads with a letter from the office of Cardinal Matteo di Viterbo*

The cardinal's brief is polite and specific: the fragment should be delivered to the Vatican library rather than to a foreign archive, as it constitutes a primary document of Roman literary heritage. The cardinal's office has jurisdiction over classical manuscripts held in the Italian states by virtue of a 1341 papal letter, a copy of which is attached to the brief.

The 1341 letter does indeed give the Vatican library priority claim over certain categories of classical manuscripts. The Monte Cassino fragment is not clearly in those categories — it is a margin annotation, not a primary text. But the argument is close enough that a Roman court might not distinguish it.

*The fighter must demonstrate, specifically, that a margin annotation citing a lost secondary commentary is not covered by the 1341 letter's classification — and that sending it to a foreign archive for neutral authentication is legally consistent with Vatican interests.*

---

**ACT III — What Piero Saw**

*The Fragment, sealed, carried south · in a Naples tavern where Piero della Rocca is waiting · evening, the bay lit by late light*

Piero della Rocca has been in Naples for three weeks waiting to hear whether his letter produced results. He is fifty-eight. He has published seventeen papers on the Aeneid's final books. He wrote the circulating letter not to create controversy but because he knew that if he did not announce the fragment's existence, it would disappear.

He has read the three words once. He describes what happened when he read them: he set the manuscript down, stood up, walked to the window, stood there for some time, returned, and read them again. The second reading was the same as the first. He says: "I have been arguing for forty years that the ending is wrong. Not morally — structurally. That Virgil died before he finished it and what we have is the draft, not the intention." He says: "The three words are not a different ending. They are the ending Virgil already wrote, made visible."

He does not ask to read the fragment again. He already knows what it says.

*The fighter looks at the sealed letter-fold. The fighter has not read it. The fighter chooses, at this moment, not to.*

---

**ACT IV — The Courier Returns**

*The Fragment, still sealed · on the road north from Naples, in the Apennine foothills · a courier from Monte Cassino, riding south toward the fighter*

The abbot has reconsidered. Not the release — he stands by that. But he has spoken with the monastery's legal counsel, who has advised that once the fragment is classified in the Weimar archive, the Monte Cassino scriptorium will be permanently associated with scholarship that challenges the canonical epic. Generations of monks will have to explain to visiting scholars and church officials why their library produced a challenge to Virgil.

The courier carries the abbot's letter asking for the fragment's return. The abbot says plainly: he is not asking for suppression. He is asking for time to prepare the monastery for what the authentication will mean.

*The fighter must explain, in terms the abbot can accept, why there is no safe version of "time to prepare" — why the fragment's existence is already public from Piero's letter, and why delay now only means the archive receives it without the abbot's authorization, which is worse for the monastery than releasing it cleanly.*

---

**ACT V — What They Do**

*The Fragment, finally unsealed · on the Weimar archive intake desk · morning*

The archivist breaks the letter-fold's seal. He reads the annotation. He reads the six lines. He reads the three words.

He sets the fragment down. He sits with it for a moment.

Then he takes out the archive register and writes, very carefully: *Marginal annotation — variant final passage, Book XII. Citation: Servius, third book, lost commentary. Alternate lines: six. Classification: disputed authenticity, primary evidence.*

He looks at the fighter. "I wrote 'disputed authenticity' because I cannot in good conscience write 'genuine' without formal review. But I am noting in the secondary record—" he turns to a second page, "—that the annotation's hand is consistent with 9th-century Monte Cassino script, the parchment is consistent with that period, and the citation to Servius's lost third book matches two other known fragmentary references to that commentary in unrelated manuscripts."

He closes the register. He does not say what the three words are. He says: "They change the ending. Not the outcome — the ethics of it." He pauses. "Virgil would have known what he was doing."

*The fragment is in the archive. The three words are in the register's secondary record. What Turnus said after being struck will not be lost again.*

## Cycle 11 — Angle: *Phlegyas's Inscription — The Warning That Belongs to Whoever Reads It*

**What this angle reveals that cycles 1-10 missed**: Phlegyas is punished in Tartarus for burning Apollo's temple. His punishment is to shout his warning endlessly: *Discite iustitiam moniti et non temnere divos* — learn justice, having been warned, and do not scorn the gods. Vergil says this line was inscribed over Roman courthouses. A stone lintel from a Roman basilica near Verona has been found in a church foundation during a 1367 restoration. The bishop sees a religious warning. The magistrates see a legal admonition. The humanist sees a pagan text. Each of them wants the stone above a different door. The archive wants it because the stone cannot be cut into three pieces without losing the thing that makes each reader right.

**Themes avoided:** all ten prior FCO themes; any theme about disputed ownership (this is about institutional purpose, not property).

**New theme chosen:** "The inscription that reads differently from every doorway; the archive is the institution that holds it without claiming to know which reading is correct."

### 5-Act Vignette: *Phlegyas's Inscription*

#### The Token

The Phlegyas Lintel: a carved stone lintel, Roman-period, found in a Verona church foundation during a 1367 restoration. Inscription: *DISCITE IVSTITIAM MONITI ET NON TEMNERE DIVS* (archaic spelling). Stone is intact, both ends complete. The inscription is in a public hand — designed to be read from the street.

*The Phlegyas Lintel · a stone slab, about half a meter long, wrapped in canvas · Verona church construction site*

---

#### Act I — Verona (VRN)

*The construction site. The mason Ser Benedetto found the lintel during foundation excavation three days ago. He has had three visitors: the bishop's representative, the city magistrate's clerk, and the local humanist scholar Giacomo Fracastoro. All three want the stone. All three have left without it because Ser Benedetto wants someone to resolve the dispute before he gives it to anyone.*

He is a practical man who does not want to be the person who made the wrong choice. He hands the stone and its documentation over to the Fighter, who has come before any of the three visitors returned with offers.

`grantItem: "Phlegyas Lintel"`

*The Phlegyas Lintel · accepted, canvas wrapper · Fighter's hands · Verona construction site*

**Skill Check — History DC 12:** *Identify the inscription's source — the specific Vergil line and its context in Book 6 — and understand why each of the three readers sees a different text.*

*pass:* The bishop reads: "Do not scorn the gods." A religious injunction. The magistrates read: "Learn justice, having been warned." A legal precept. The humanist reads: "Phlegyas in Tartarus." A classical reference to punishment and memory. *All three are correct. The inscription is genuinely ambiguous between its registers. Phlegyas was shouting all three things simultaneously. That is the punishment.*

*fail:* The inscription identified as a Vergil quotation. Multi-register significance not assembled.

---

#### Act II — Verona (VRN)

*The bishop's representative, Father Lorenzo, catches up with the Fighter before the city gate. The bishop has a stronger claim than the other two, he argues: the stone was found in church foundations; the church has primary custody of things found within its structural boundary.*

He is not wrong about the custody argument.

*The Phlegyas Lintel · in the canvas, Father Lorenzo blocking the city gate · Fighter · Verona city gate*

**Skill Check — Persuasion DC 12:** *Argue that a stone found in a church foundation was not placed there by the church — it was incorporated from an earlier structure; the custody claim derives from the church's use of the stone, not the stone's origin.*

*pass:* He considers. The stone is Roman. The church is not Roman. "The foundation incorporated it." He pauses. "We incorporated it without knowing what it was." He lets the Fighter pass. *He is a theologian. He can follow the argument. He is not happy about it.*

*fail:* He imposes a two-day canonical delay pending the bishop's review. Released when the bishop decides the archive claim is valid.

---

#### Act III — Verona Road (VRN)

*The humanist scholar Giacomo Fracastoro is on the road north with a student. He has been following the dispute. He wants the stone for his library. He makes an elegant argument: the inscription is a Vergil quotation; the correct custodian of Vergil quotations is a classical library, not a church or a courthouse.*

He is charming, erudite, and has a specific argument.

*The Phlegyas Lintel · in the canvas, Giacomo making his case on the road · Fighter · road north of Verona*

**Skill Check — Persuasion DC 13:** *Argue that the inscription's value comes from its three-way ambiguity — placing it in a classical library resolves the ambiguity in favor of one reading; the archive preserves all three.*

*pass:* He pauses. He is an honest scholar. "The ambiguity is the data." He says it before the Fighter does. He lets the stone go with a note: *I will cite the classification in my commentary.* *He reached the argument himself. That is why he let it go.*

*fail:* He retains a full wax rubbing of the inscription. His commentary will cite a private note.

---

#### Act IV — Bologna Road (BOL)

*Two men from the Veronese city magistrate's office. The magistrate has decided that a Roman courthouse inscription belongs in the current courthouse by continuity of function. They have a formal writ.*

They have legal authority within Veronese jurisdiction, which ends at the city limits. They are two hours' ride past the city limits.

*The Phlegyas Lintel · in the canvas, both men with the magistrate's writ · Fighter · road south of Bologna*

**Hybrid — Persuasion DC 12 first; fail → Combat:**

*Persuasion pass:* Argue that the writ's jurisdiction ended at the city limits two hours ago; the writ is a Veronese document and they are not in Verona. *They look at each other. They know this.* They turn back.

*Persuasion fail → Combat:* 2 city agents, AC 12, HP 18. *failText:* They take the stone back to Verona. The road north has waystation factors. *passText:* Both down or withdrew.

---

#### Act V — Weimar (WM)

*Archivus Sweelinck unwraps the canvas. He reads the inscription. He reads the Aeneid, Book 6, line 620.*

"Phlegyas is shouting in Tartarus," he says. "He burned Apollo's temple. He shouted this as his punishment for eternity." He reads it again. "The bishop reads: 'do not scorn the gods.' The magistrates read: 'learn justice.' The humanist reads: 'Phlegyas.'" He sets down the stone. "They are all right. Phlegyas was a king who was also a criminal who was also a warning. The inscription does not choose. It was designed to be read from the street, by anyone who walked past." He opens the intake book. "The archive is the only institution that can receive this without being required to choose which door it belongs above."

He writes: *Multi-Register Inscriptions: inscriptions that can be accurately read as religious, legal, and literary texts simultaneously; primary evidence for the public use of literary quotation in administrative and devotional spaces; the ambiguity is the primary datum, not a problem to be resolved.*

"You receive the Phlegyas Inscription Record."
