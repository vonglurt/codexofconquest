**Brothers Grimm | 1812 (collected); 1857 (final edition) | Folk Tales | 547 KB — over threshold, drawn from training knowledge**

## Three-Pass Summary

### Pass 1 — Full Spoiler Telling with Scene Notes

**The Collection's Architecture**
The Grimms gathered oral tales throughout Germany and shaped them into literary form across seven editions. The 1812 first edition had 86 tales; the 1857 seventh edition had 200. The tales are not uniform in tone — some are comic, some horrific, many both — but they share a structural grammar: displacement and restoration, with the path of restoration always running through a witness no one thought to suppress.

**The Frog King** — A king's daughter drops her golden ball into a well. A frog retrieves it in exchange for her promise to let him be her companion. She breaks the promise after getting the ball back. The frog appears at the palace and the king forces her to honor it. In the original 1812 version, she throws him against the wall in fury and he transforms. In later versions, a kiss. The prince explains he was cursed by a witch.

**Faithful John (Der treue Johannes)** — The old king, dying, asks his most faithful servant to protect his son and show him the palace's rooms — except one, which contains a portrait of a princess so beautiful the prince would risk everything for her. John shows all the rooms; the prince sees the portrait through a keyhole and falls ill with love. John arranges the voyage to win the princess. Returning, John overhears three ravens prophesying: the wedding horse is enchanted and will kill the prince, the wedding robe will kill him, and the first dance will give him a death-sleep. John can prevent each if he acts; if he tells anyone why, he will turn to stone. He kills the horse, seizes and burns the robe, dances with the bride until the prince goes cold — then cuts open two children of the royal couple and bathes the prince in their blood. Restored, the prince is about to execute John for the murders. John explains everything just before the axe falls and turns to stone. The children are miraculously restored when the prince uses the same cure.

**Godfather Death (Der Gevatter Tod)** — A poor man with thirteen children cannot find a godfather. Death offers to stand for the last child; the poor man accepts because Death is fair — takes rich and poor alike. Death gives his godson the ability to see whether a patient will live or die: if Death stands at the head, the patient is doomed; at the feet, they can be saved. He must never cure the doomed. He does so twice — once for a king, once for a princess he loves — by turning the patient in bed. Death takes him after the second violation. The tale ends with the godson's candle guttering out.

**Rapunzel** — A sorceress takes a child from parents who stole her rampion. She raises the girl in a tower with no door or stairs, accessible only via Rapunzel's long hair. A prince finds her. They plan escape. The sorceress learns of it, cuts off Rapunzel's hair, banishes her to a desert. When the prince comes up the hair, the sorceress grabs him and shoves him into thorns below — he is blinded. He wanders for years. He finds Rapunzel in the desert; her tears fall on his eyes and restore his sight.

**Allerleirauh (All-Kinds-of-Fur)** — A dying queen makes her king promise to marry only a woman as beautiful as herself. Their daughter grows up matching this description. The king decides to marry her. She demands impossible gifts — dresses in the colors of sun, moon, and stars — and finally a cloak made of all the furs in the kingdom, hoping to make the demand fail. All are provided. She escapes disguised in the fur cloak as a scullion, enters service at a foreign court. Three times she attends the lord's ball in the sun, moon, and star dresses; each time she returns to her fur disguise. The lord, increasingly obsessed, discovers her identity when she hides a gold ring in his soup and he finds it.

**The Juniper Tree (Von dem Machandelboom)** — One of the darkest. A woman kills her stepson, chops him up, makes him into a stew, feeds it to his father. The daughter hides the boy's bones under the juniper tree. A bird emerges from the tree — a beautiful bird with extraordinary voice — and sings a song naming what was done to it:

> *My mother she killed me, / My father he ate me, / My sister, little Marlene, / Gathered up my bones, / Tied them in a silken scarf, / Laid them beneath the juniper tree.*

The bird gives gifts to those who treated it kindly, drops a millstone on the stepmother, who is crushed. The boy returns to his human form.

**The Six Swans** — A king's six sons are transformed into swans by their stepmother. The sister learns she can break the curse if she sews six shirts of star-flowers in six years without speaking or laughing. While she works in silence, she is accused of killing her children (who were secretly taken by the stepmother), condemned as a witch, sentenced to burn. She is at the stake when her brothers fly overhead; she throws the shirts over them. Five are fully restored. The sixth lacks one sleeve — his arm remains a swan's wing. The stepmother is burned in the witch's place. The sister can finally speak.

**The Goose-Girl** — A princess is sent to marry a foreign prince, accompanied by her horse Falada and a maid. On the road, the maid forces the princess to swap clothes and identities; she coerces an oath of silence from the princess. At the court, the maid presents herself as the princess. The true princess is given the goose-boy's job. The maid, fearing Falada will speak, orders him slaughtered; the head is nailed above the city gate. Every morning, passing the gate, the princess says:

*Alas, Falada, there thou hangest.*
Falada's skull answers: *Alas, young Queen, there thou goest! If this thy mother knew, her heart would break in two.*

The goose-boy Conrad tries to touch her golden hair; she commands the wind to blow his hat away. The king watches from a window. He asks why she speaks to the skull; she says she is sworn not to speak her griefs to any living being. He suggests she tell the iron stove. She goes to the stove alone and tells everything; the king's ear is at the pipe outside. He acts. The maid confesses (when asked what punishment a woman who did such-and-such should receive — she names her own sentence without knowing it). The maid is killed. The princess is restored.

**Rumpelstiltskin** — A miller claims his daughter can spin straw into gold. The king threatens her life if she cannot. A small man appears and spins the gold for escalating prices: a necklace, a ring, and finally the promise of her firstborn child. She becomes queen. The child is born. The small man comes for it; she weeps. He relents on one condition: if she can guess his name within three days, she can keep the child. On the third day, a servant reports overhearing a small man singing his name while dancing around a fire in the forest. She guesses correctly. The small man, in fury, tears himself in two.

**Iron John (Iron Hans)** — A wild man, iron-colored, is captured from a forest that has devoured men. A young prince's golden ball rolls into the cage; he frees Iron John to retrieve it. Iron John takes him into the forest, gives him a spring of gold to guard (he must not let anything fall in). He fails three times, gets stained golden. Iron John sends him away; he enters service at a foreign court as a gardener's boy. In three battles he appears as a golden knight (calling on Iron Hans) and helps the king win victories he cannot explain. Eventually he reveals himself. Iron Hans is revealed as an enchanted king freed by the boy's act.

**Structural Patterns Common to the Collection**:
- *The impossible witness*: a severed skull speaks, a bird's song names the killer, bones arranged in soil tell the story, a deep well remembers
- *The name as the whole power*: Rumpelstiltskin has no power once named; every enchanted prince becomes himself when his name is spoken over him
- *The spoken truth in a non-court venue*: the truth is said, but not in testimony — it is said to a skull, sung by a bird, whispered to a stove; the court receives only the transcription of an overheard confession
- *The task with the technical loophole*: the impossible task is always completed by means the poser did not anticipate
- *The helper who cannot explain*: Faithful John cannot say why he acts; the six swans' sister cannot speak; the truth is preserved in silence and explained only at the threshold of death

### Pass 2 — Character and Pattern Deep-Dives

**The Silenced Rightful Person**: Grimm protagonists are consistently able to speak the truth and consistently prevented from speaking it in a venue that counts. The princess has sworn an oath. The sister cannot speak for six years. The murdered boy's soul is in the bird's body. The constraint is usually precise: *to any living being*, which is why speaking to the skull or the stove or the fire is not a violation. The antagonist always suppresses the direct venue and misses the oblique one.

**The Impossible Witness**: The severed horse skull, the singing bird, the bones in the soil, the tree that grows from the grave — these are witnesses that were not alive to threaten or bribe. The killer of Falada thinks a dead horse cannot testify. The stepmother who dismembered her stepson thinks dead bones have no voice. These antagonists reason correctly about the silencing of human witnesses and incorrectly about the persistence of the world's memory in non-human form.

**The Intermediate Figure**: Someone always hears the impossible witness. The king at his window. The farrier who works at the stable. The priest who finds the bones. The servant who overheard the dancing in the forest. The intermediate figure did not go looking for the testimony; the testimony reached them accidentally because they were in the right place. Their job is not interpretation but transcription: they write down what they heard the impossible witness say.

**The Antagonist's Error**: In every case, the villain suppresses the obvious and forgets the peripheral. The maid orders Falada killed but does not account for the gate, the skull's proximity to the daily route, or the king's habit of watching from the window. Rumpelstiltskin dances in the forest because he is certain no one is there. The stepmother of The Juniper Tree cooks the bones but misses the ones the daughter gathered. The error is always the same: a mistaken model of where testimony can come from.

### Pass 3 — Pivotal Scene: Falada's Gate

Every morning the goose-girl passes through the city gate under the severed skull of her horse. She has no other living witnesses. She is bound by oath not to speak her grief to any living being. But the skull is dead. So she speaks to it.

She says: *Alas, Falada, there thou hangest.*
The skull says: *Alas, young Queen, there thou goest. If this thy mother knew, her heart would break in two.*

She has been doing this for weeks. The skull confirms her identity — calls her *young Queen* — every morning. No one is listening for it. But the king watches from his window. He sees the exchange. He does not yet understand it. He asks her about it afterward; she says she cannot speak her grief to any living being. He suggests the iron stove. She goes to the stove in a private room and tells everything into it — the theft of her identity on the road, the forced oath, her real name and her real father's name — as a confession to an inanimate object that cannot be a witness in court.

The king's ear is at the pipe outside. He has heard everything. He takes what he heard — his own record of what she said to the stove — and acts on it.

The testimony that undoes the impostor was: first spoken to a dead horse. Then repeated to an iron stove. Then received by a king who was eavesdropping on both. The maid never had a chance to suppress it because it was never in a suppressible venue.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **"Falada Speaks"** — A merchant's daughter has been displaced by a maid who stole her identity papers. The daughter now tends geese at a city livery stable. Her father's original account record is hidden in a false-bottomed feed bin at the stable. For forty days she has been speaking her true name and her claim every morning to a horse's skull nailed above the stable gate — not to any human, but because the skull is the only witness she trusts. The stable farrier has been listening. He had his observation notarized: forty days of morning testimony spoken to a dead skull, in her voice, her name, her father's name. The Fighter carries this notarized attestation — together with the recovered account record — from the stable district to the civil chancery before the impostor's guild registration closes the case permanently.

2. **"The Name in the Forest"** — A creditor who ruined a family operates under a false guild name. A charcoal-burner who lives in the forest outside the city overhears him singing his real name while dancing at a fire at midnight. The charcoal-burner doesn't know what it means but he wrote it down. The Fighter carries the written name from the forest edge to the guild arbiters before the bond is transferred into anonymity.

3. **"The Third Task"** — A poor family was given a contract with three tasks as conditions of debt-release. They completed two. The third: an independent witness must countersign the completion document. No one in the family qualifies — they are interested parties. The Fighter witnesses and countersigns. The signed document must reach the lord's notary before the deadline, against the lord's agents who want the deadline to expire.

4. **"Faithful John's Account"** — A guard was condemned for what looked like three acts of treason. He could not explain himself — explaining would have caused the very deaths he was preventing. The danger has now passed; the explanation is now possible. He has written the full account, sealed it, addressed it to the lord who condemned him. The Fighter carries it from the dungeon to the lord's hall; the condemned man cannot go himself and the lord's household will not accept a message from someone already condemned.

5. **"Godfather's Ledger"** — A physician's gift came with precise terms that he documented carefully: every patient he declined to treat when death stood at the head, every patient he did treat when death stood at the feet. His records prove he obeyed the terms and was not the cause of the deaths attributed to him. The Fighter carries the sealed ledger from the physician's house — where he is too ill to carry it himself — to the guild arbiters before the false accusation is finalized.

6. **"The Bones' Song"** — A man was murdered; his family claims natural death. A priest found the bones under a tree and, following the local custom of listening for what the soil's arrangement says, wrote down what the bones' position described — the angle of the fatal blow, the direction of the approach, the timing indicated by the undergrowth patterns. It is not confession evidence; it is environmental testimony. The Fighter carries the priest's written account to the magistrate before the estate is sealed and the family's version becomes the official record.

7. **"All-Kinds-of-Fur"** — A noble woman hiding as a kitchen scullion slipped a signed contract proving her identity into a soup served at the lord's table. The lord found it, didn't know what it was, filed it in his household records. Her real family hired the Fighter to recover it from the household records office before the quarterly purge of unclaimed filed documents.

## 5-Act Vignette Play

*A German imperial city in the grey of early morning. The stable district is at the city's periphery — horse-smell and mud, the sound of hooves on wet cobblestone, iron fittings and cheap tallow. The Fighter has been sent by a reforming notary who has seen the attestation papers and believes they are legally sound — but cannot go himself.*

---

**ACT 1 — Stable District (STB)**
*The farrier is a broad man with soot-stained forearms who greets the Fighter at the back gate of the livery yard. Above the main gate arch, visible from fifty feet away, a horse's skull is nailed in the traditional way — a stable custom, an old protection. The skull is white with age and the weather. He doesn't look at it.*

"She talks to it every morning," he says. "She's done it for forty days. Not to me — she doesn't know I can hear her from the stall. She speaks her name. Her father's name. What was taken from her and who took it." *He hands the Fighter a folded paper and a flat oilskin pouch.* "The paper is my attestation. I had the notary come two weeks ago — I told him exactly what I heard and he put his mark on it. The oilskin has her father's original account record, the one from the false-bottomed feed bin. I found it when I cleaned that bin out. Forty days of her speaking her name to a dead skull. It sounds like madness but it isn't."

*He is not afraid of the Fighter. He is afraid the arbiters will laugh.*

**TOKEN GRANT:** The Farrier's Attestation — a folded paper with the notary's mark and date, recording forty consecutive mornings of the merchant's daughter speaking her name, her father's name, and the nature of her claim to the horse's skull above the stable gate. The oilskin pouch holds the original account record.

**SKILL CHECK:** Insight DC 12
- *onPass:* The Fighter understands what the farrier is actually afraid of: not disbelief in his honesty, but disbelief in the legal standing of testimony overheard from the direction of a dead horse. Fighter explains: the attestation is not "the skull testified" — it is "I, the farrier, heard this woman speak these words on these dates in this location." The skull's presence is irrelevant to the admissibility of his observation. The farrier hands over both documents without further reservation.
- *onFail:* The Fighter can't quite articulate the distinction. The farrier hands over the documents anyway but folds the attestation separately and asks the Fighter to lead with the account record at the guild court, not the skull paper.

**ITEM GRANT:** A small pot of stable-saddle oil — the farrier's payment for the notary, which the notary returned as a joke, and which the farrier has been meaning to use up ever since.

---

**ACT 2 — Stable Gate to Guild Court Road**
*The gate between the stable district and the civic streets is watched. The impostor's allies are careful people; the stable-district entry log is their instrument. A young watchman in a city cloak is posted at the outer gate with a logbook and the specific, unhurried patience of someone who has been paid to delay.*

"Documents leaving the stable district require a transit log entry," he says. "Contents and destination." He opens the book and looks at the Fighter pleasantly.

The transit log entry will be relayed to the impostor's allies within the hour. It will tell them exactly where the Fighter is going and when they left.

**SKILL CHECK:** Persuasion DC 13
- *onPass:* The Fighter points out that the transit log covers commercial goods, not legal correspondence addressed to guild arbiters. The watchman's authority is stable-district commercial traffic; guild-addressed notarial documents are outside his mandate. The watchman knows this; he was hoping the carrier wouldn't. He logs the Fighter as "courier for guild business" and waves them through — the log entry says nothing about the documents' contents or origin.
- *onFail:* The watchman demands to see the oilskin pouch's outer label. The farrier labeled it correctly: "sealed notarial document, guild-addressed, tamper-evident." The watchman logs it as "sealed document, content unknown, destination guild court." He is polite. The entry is made. It will be reported.

**ITEM GRANT:** A transit log receipt — shows time of exit from the stable district. If the impostor's representative later claims the documents were altered in transit, the time-stamp contradicts any alteration argument.

---

**ACT 3 — Guild Court (GLD)**
*The guild arbiters' hall: two senior arbiters at a table, lamps burning against the morning grey, the smell of beeswax and old paper. The impostor's representative is already seated. He has filed a pre-emptive motion challenging the identity claim as a fabrication, and he has brought the impostor's own account record — guild-registered two months ago under her name — as proof of the official record. He is calm. He expected this.*

When the Fighter presents the farrier's attestation, the representative raises his hand: "I'd like to see the provenance of this alleged prior witness." He reads the attestation. He looks up. "This documents testimony addressed to a horse's skull. My client's registration predates any human witness to this woman's claimed identity. The account record was a plant."

*One arbiter's mouth twitches slightly. The other is writing.*

**SKILL CHECK + COMBAT:** Persuasion DC 14
- *onPass:* The Fighter presents the dating argument: the notary's date-mark on the farrier's attestation precedes the impostor's guild registration by thirty-eight days. The merchant's daughter was claiming her identity to a dead skull before she knew anyone would ever register a competing claim — before the dispute existed in any formal sense. That is the definition of a prior consistent statement. The arbiter who was writing stops writing and looks at the representative. The representative has no counter. The arbiters issue a transfer order directing the civil chancery to receive the evidence for formal determination.
- *onFail:* The representative's argument catches traction; the arbiter with the twitching mouth defers. The representative signals. Two hired men from the corridor — the impostor's household livery, blunt instruments. **2× household retainers, AC 12, HP 16.** They are here to take the documents "for safekeeping pending guild review," not to kill anyone. The meaning of safekeeping is clear.

**ITEM GRANT:** An arbiter's transfer order — directing the civil chancery to receive the evidence. The Fighter must carry this with the oilskin and the attestation to the chancery.

---

**ACT 4 — Guild Court to Civil District (CI)**
*The market road between the guild hall and the Civil District is crowded at mid-morning: fish carts, grain-sellers, a line of pilgrims at the church corner, a tanner's boy with a stack of hides taller than himself. The watchman's log entry has been reported. Two men in the impostor's household colors are standing at the road's narrows with a freshly-stamped civil injunction: signed by a sub-magistrate, ordering the "unauthorized carrying of contested documents pending formal guild determination."*

*Service of the injunction requires the server to read the document aloud to the carrier and confirm acknowledgment. An acknowledged injunction stops the delivery. An unacknowledged one is formally incomplete.*

*The Civil District gate is two hundred yards ahead. The market is between here and there.*

**SKILL CHECK:** Athletics DC 13
- *onPass:* The Fighter moves through the market at the speed of someone who grew up in crowds — threading between the grain carts, cutting behind the pilgrims' slow column, emerging at the far side of the tanner's stack. The server cannot read a moving target in a crowd without acknowledgment. The Fighter reaches the chancery gate before the injunction is completed.
- *onFail:* The server manages to read the first lines aloud and demands acknowledgment at the gate, simultaneously. The chancery door-officer is watching. The Fighter must argue that the arbiter's transfer order — a guild instrument — supersedes a civil sub-magistrate's injunction in guild-originating cases before the door-officer accepts either paper. It is a correct argument; the door-officer makes it reluctantly.

**ITEM GRANT:** A chancery gate-receipt — time-stamped at the moment the documents crossed the threshold. If the injunction was not acknowledged before this moment, the time-stamp establishes priority.

---

**ACT 5 — Civil District (CI)**
*The chancery's senior officer is a careful man who reviews documents the way a jeweler reviews stones: by light, by angle, by weight in the hand. He lays out the arbiter's transfer order, the farrier's attestation, the oilskin account record, and the gate-receipt. He also has the injunction, which arrived by messenger three minutes after the Fighter entered the building. He has made a note of the time difference.*

"Standard procedure for contested identity documents," he says, not looking up from the attestation, "requires the claiming party to appear in person for authentication. She is not here." *He sets the attestation down. His pen is still. He has not moved to the rejection stamp.*

The claiming party cannot appear. She would be arrested on the impostor's prior civil claim — which may itself be fraudulent, as the thirty-eight-day gap demonstrates. His standard procedure exists to prevent fraud. It is being used to enable it. He knows this. His pen has not moved.

**SKILL CHECK:** Insight DC 12
- *onPass:* The Fighter sees it: the officer wants cover to proceed, not an argument for proceeding. He knows the law. He needs the procedural hook — a reason to apply the exception without exposing himself. The hook is in the attestation itself: the farrier's record establishes identity through independent prior observation, not through the claiming party's appearance; the statute's appearance requirement is addressed to cases where no prior independent witness exists; and the claiming party's inability to appear is created by the opposing party's own prior civil action, which means requiring appearance is allowing one party to use their own legal instrument to defeat the remedy. The officer makes a small notation on the register. He opens the ledger. He stamps the attestation received.

  The account record is registered under the merchant's daughter's name. The impostor's registration is flagged for review. A provisional certificate of authentic identity is issued, to be delivered by court messenger.

- *onFail:* Authentication is suspended pending personal appearance. The documents are filed — correctly, permanently, not destroyed — but inactive. The merchant's daughter will need another path.

**TOKEN TAKE:** The Farrier's Attestation — filed into the chancery's evidence register, sealed with the senior officer's receipt stamp. The oilskin account record is registered separately. Both are permanent record. The Fighter leaves with an empty hand and the gate-receipt as their only documentation of the transaction.

## Cycle 2 — Faithful John's Account

**What cycle 1 missed:** Cycle 1 took the oblique testimony angle — the merchant's daughter spoke her name to a dead skull because no living court would receive her, and the farrier overheard what was never directed at him. The mechanism of truth was indirect displacement: the venue was wrong, the witness was not alive, and the account arrived through institutional transcription of an overheard accident. Cycle 2 takes the structurally opposite angle. Faithful John knew exactly what he was doing, could have named each act precisely, and chose silence because the explanation would have destroyed what his silence protected. The account exists now because the danger no longer exists. This cycle is about the explanation that was itself the deepest form of the service, delivered after the fact to make permanent the knowledge of what kind of loyalty the prince received.

---

### Pass 1 — Token

**Faithful John's Account** is a sealed folio — eight pages, careful ink, John's own mark. It names the three acts of apparent treason in order: the wedding-horse clothes and why he burned them; the wedding robe and why he seized it; the first dance and why he danced until the prince went cold. Each paragraph names what he knew would happen and why he acted before the prince could see the danger. The folio ends with one sentence: *I do not ask forgiveness for what I would do again.* It cannot be opened by the carrier. Its value depends entirely on arriving intact, in the prince's hands, with the seal John placed on it.

---

### Pass 2 — Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **"Faithful John's Account"** ← **CHOSEN / USED (cycle 2)**
2. "The Name in the Forest" — a creditor operates under a false guild name; a charcoal-burner overheard him singing his real name at a midnight fire in the forest and wrote it down without knowing what it meant; Fighter carries the written name to the guild arbiters
3. "The Third Task" — a debt-release contract requires an independent witness to countersign the third completion; no family member qualifies; Fighter witnesses and signs; the signed document must reach the lord's notary against agents sent to let the deadline expire
4. "Godfather's Ledger" — a physician documented every patient he declined to treat when death stood at the head and every patient he treated when death stood at the feet; his ledger proves obedience to the terms of a compact no one else can audit; Fighter carries it to the guild arbiters before the false accusation is finalized
5. "The Bones' Song" — a man murdered and called naturally dead; a priest found the bones and wrote down what their arrangement said — angle of blow, direction of approach, timing from the undergrowth — environmental testimony from material positions; Fighter carries it to the magistrate before the estate is sealed
6. "All-Kinds-of-Fur" — a noble woman hiding as a kitchen scullion slipped a signed contract proving her identity into a soup served at the lord's table; the lord filed it in household records; Fighter recovers it before the quarterly purge
7. "Rumpelstiltskin's Debt" — the small man's contract was valid; the queen owes the fee; the contract is in the small man's personal archive and no one will accept its legitimacy; Fighter carries it to the kingdom's debt arbiters — this inverts the tale's sympathies entirely and is the most legally uncomfortable option

### Pass 3 — Theme Check

Existing threads do not cover: the explanation withheld not from weakness but as an extension of the service it would have ended; the silence that was itself the act of loyalty; the account delivered after the protection it would have destroyed is no longer in danger. Closest existing thread: *the word the dying man cannot carry himself* (HFT cycle 2) — but that is recognition, not explanation; Ring could not outlive the moment, not a choice. Here the agent chose silence, survived it, and now names what the silence was. Distinct thread: **The service that could not be named while it was being performed: the explanation delayed until the protection it would have destroyed is safe; the account that arrives after the fact to make permanent the knowledge of what kind of loyalty the prince received.**

## 5-Act Vignette Play

*A German castle, late afternoon. Three corridors from the dungeon-room where the prince's restored servant sits at a writing table in a room that is not technically a cell but cannot be left publicly without triggering the execution order that was never formally rescinded. The prince is in his great hall, alone with two household men and the kind of quiet that comes after a long and strange event. The Fighter has been sent by no one in particular — just given the folio and told which hall.*

---

**ACT 1 — John's Restoration Room (DNG)**

*The room has a writing table and a narrow window and smells of cold stone and fresh ink. Faithful John is seated at the table, looking healthier than a man who was stone for some years has any right to look. He seals the folio as the Fighter enters — presses his ring into the wax and holds it for a count of three. Then he looks up.*

"He restored me," he says. "Which is how I know he would want to understand." *He holds the folio across the table.* "He will not receive it from my hand — that would require him to formally acknowledge an encounter, and the execution order is technically still open. You are not me. You are someone with a sealed personal letter." He does not say from whom. "Do not say it is from this room. Say it is from a household correspondent who prefers not to be named."

*He has not asked if the Fighter will do this. He is simply explaining the form.*

**TOKEN GRANT:** Faithful John's Account — sealed folio, eight pages, John's ring in the wax at the fold.

**SKILL CHECK:** Insight DC 12
- *onPass:* The Fighter sees the distinction before John finishes. This is not a plea — he doesn't need acquittal; the prince already knows what he did and restored him. He needs the prince to know the *shape* of the loyalty he received: what John knew, what he chose, what each act cost him. The account is a gift, not a petition. The Fighter takes the folio without asking what it says.
- *onFail:* Fighter starts to say something about vindication. John stops him: "Read nothing into it. Carry it sealed. Deliver it to his hands, not his chamberlain's. Say nothing about the content." The instruction is clear enough; the Fighter takes the folio.

**ITEM GRANT:** John's writing-press seal — left on the table, not taken; John pushes it toward the Fighter in case it's needed to identify the mark at the corridor log. The Fighter should leave it here; John presses it back.

---

**ACT 2 — Castle Household Corridor (CHC)**

*The head steward stands at the corridor junction with a log-book open on the standing desk beside him. He is a deliberate man who has managed this household's message traffic for twenty years. He needs an origin for every letter in his log. "Unnamed" is not a category his log has room for.*

"Where does it come from?" he asks. He holds the lamp close to the folio wrapper — he cannot read through the seal but he can count the pages through the oilskin. "Eight pages isn't a personal note."

*He is not hostile. He is doing his job. But a log entry naming the west corridor restoration room would be retrievable by anyone who wanted to know why a sealed folio reached the prince this afternoon.*

**SKILL CHECK:** Deception DC 12
- *onPass:* "Household correspondent, private matter, personal seal." The Fighter delivers each phrase with the specific inflection of someone who has said it before on behalf of people who knew what they were doing. The steward writes: *personal correspondence, sealed, personal mark, origin unspecified.* He does not look pleased. He lets them through.
- *onFail:* The steward is more careful than that. "Unspecified origin is noted as a verbal representation, not a log entry — I'll need your name as the carrier attesting to private character of contents." He notes the Fighter's name and dates it. Retrievable later. Not stopped now.

---

**ACT 3 — Prince's Antechamber (ANT)**

*The chamberlain is at the antechamber door: a thin man who has understood for twenty years that his job is to protect the prince from arriving at strong emotions without preparation. He has opinions about when sealed folios should be delivered. He has a process.*

"I'll bring it to him during the evening session," he says, reaching for the folio with the practiced ease of a man who has intercepted hundreds of things at this door. "He'll be in the right frame of mind."

*He means: he will open it first. He will assess whether it is emotionally ready. Then he will describe the content to the prince before the prince reads it — which is to say, the prince will receive the chamberlain's version of what John sealed, not the sealed thing itself.*

**SKILL CHECK:** Persuasion DC 13
- *onPass:* "The sender required delivery to his hand, unopened, at his own reading pace." The chamberlain's hand pauses on the folio. The Fighter names the category: personal correspondence bearing the sender's mark, addressed to the prince's personal hand, is outside the chamberlain's editorial authority by household charter. The chamberlain knows this. He removes his hand from the folio with the deliberate unhurry of a man noting for the record that he was not consulted. He opens the door.
- *onFail:* The chamberlain will not move without knowing the origin. Fighter names it: "The restored man in the west wing." The chamberlain's face changes. He is still for three seconds. Then he opens the door without speaking — the phrase is a household code that the whole castle understands but no one says aloud. The Fighter will be remembered for knowing it.

---

**ACT 4 — Hall Entrance (ANT → PRH)**

*Two palace guards stand at the great hall's double doors: not there because they were told to stop anyone, but because a person carrying a sealed document toward the prince during informal afternoon hours is the kind of thing that standard institutional caution treats as requiring a moment's pause. They are doing their job. The folio needs to arrive before the prince leaves for the evening session in twenty minutes.*

**COMBAT:** Palace Guards ×2, AC 13, HP 24
*These are not enemies. They are the last formal gate between here and the prince's hands. They will stand there until they are moved. The folio does not get through a closed door.*

---

**ACT 5 — Prince's Great Hall (PRH)**

*The hall is large and quiet — a fire at one end, two household men near the far wall, the prince at the high seat with an open book he was not reading. He looks up when the Fighter enters. He looks at the folio. Something in his face does what a man's face does when he has been expecting something he didn't know he was expecting.*

"Who sends this?"

"A household correspondent who prefers not to be named."

*He takes the folio. He breaks the seal himself — John's ring, he knows the mark — and reads. Not quickly. At the pace of a man who already suspects what this is and needs to confirm each sentence before going to the next. His face does not change very much. Something in his hands changes.*

**SKILL CHECK:** Composure DC 11
- *onPass:* The Fighter does not watch. Holds the wall beside the door. Does not add anything. Does not look toward the folio as if to prompt a response. The prince reads all eight pages. He sets the folio on the arm of the high seat. The Fighter is already near the door. The prince says: "Wait." Fighter waits. The prince is silent for thirty seconds. Then: "Tell him I read it." That is all.
- *onFail:* Fighter adds something small — a look toward the folio, a half-gesture that carries the implication: *he didn't want anything from this.* The prince hears the implication accurately, and having heard it named, does not say the seven words he was going to say. He sets the folio on the arm of the high seat. He says nothing. The Fighter leaves.

**TOKEN TAKE:** Faithful John's Account — left on the arm of the prince's high seat, seal broken, all eight pages read. The Fighter leaves with empty hands.

**ITEM GRANT:** The Prince's Instruction — a verbal message to be carried back to the restoration room: *"Tell him I read it."* Not written down. Carried in the Fighter's memory.
