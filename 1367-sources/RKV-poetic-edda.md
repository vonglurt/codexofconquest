## Cycle 1 — Pass 1: Full Telling

### Historical and Literary Context

The Poetic Edda is a collection of Old Norse poems preserved primarily in the Codex
Regius (*Konungsbók*) — a manuscript discovered in Iceland in 1643 and later given to
the Danish king Frederik III, which is why it is sometimes called the King's Manuscript.
The poems themselves are much older than the manuscript: linguistic and stylistic
evidence suggests composition between roughly 900 and 1200 AD, with some poems possibly
preserving even older oral material. Compiled in one manuscript but not authored by
one author, the Poetic Edda is the most important source for our knowledge of
pre-Christian Norse religion, cosmology, and heroic legend.

The collection is traditionally divided into **mythological poems** (*guðakvæði*) and
**heroic poems** (*hetjukvæði*). The mythological poems describe the gods, their deeds,
and the structure of the cosmos. The heroic poems tell the stories of Sigurd, Helgi,
Gudrun, and the fall of the Niblungs — the same material as the Volsunga Saga and the
Nibelungenlied, but in verse form that is often older than the prose retellings.

The Poetic Edda's relationship to the Prose Edda (BOO) is that Snorri Sturluson used
the Poetic Edda poems as sources for his prose retellings, quoting them extensively in
the *Skáldskaparmál*. What the prose summarizes, the poems perform. The difference in
register is essential: where Snorri explains, the poems enact.

### The Major Poems

**Völuspá (The Seeress's Prophecy).** The most celebrated poem in Old Norse literature.
A *völva* (seeress) speaks — presumably called up from the dead by Odin — and recites
the entire history of the cosmos: the creation, the gods' youth, the first war, the
golden age, the Aesir's corruption, Baldr's death, Ragnarök, and the renewal of the
world afterward. The poem is addressed to Odin, who knows what she is about to tell
him. He listens anyway.

The opening line — *Hljóðs bið ek allar helgar kindir* ("I ask all sacred beings to
listen") — is the standard opening of a formal recitation. The völva commands
attention. She does not ask for it.

The poem's greatest section is the Ragnarök sequence: the cock Gullinkambi crows in
Ásgarðr, another cock crows in Hel, the world-serpent twists on the ocean floor, the
wolf Fenrir breaks his chain, Loki escapes, the Naglfar sails. And then the renewal:
the earth rises from the sea, the fields unsown bear grain, the gods meet again in the
grass. Baldr returns from Hel. The world is new.

*Hljóðs bið ek* — the völva demands silence. She is about to tell everything.

**Hávamál (Sayings of the High One).** A wisdom poem attributed to Odin, in two parts.
The first part is practical wisdom: advice on how to behave as a guest, how to drink
in moderation, how to deal with enemies, how to keep a secret. It is earthy, pragmatic,
and often wry — the voice is not of a distant god but of a man who has traveled widely
and been humiliated a few times.

The second and more famous section describes Odin's self-sacrifice on Yggdrasil:

*I know that I hung on the windy tree / nine long nights, / wounded with a spear, given
to Odin, / myself to myself, / on that tree of which no man knows / from what roots
it rises.*

He hangs. He gazes down. He takes up the runes. He cries aloud with the taking.
He falls. He has what he came for.

*Hávamál* also contains the famous *Ljóðatal* — a catalog of eighteen magical runes
and what each one can do: protect, heal, read minds, calm seas, bind enemies.

**Vafþrúðnismál (The Sayings of Vafþrúðnir).** Odin goes in disguise to contest
wisdom with the giant Vafþrúðnir. They exchange cosmological knowledge — each naming
things they know about the world's making and end. At the final question, Odin asks:
what did he whisper in Baldr's ear before the funeral pyre? Vafþrúðnir immediately
knows he has been speaking to Odin himself — no one else would know to ask this — and
the contest ends. The poem does not say what Odin whispered. This is deliberate.

**Skírnismál (The Lay of Skírnir).** Freyr sees the beautiful Gerðr from his high
seat and falls immediately and completely in love. He cannot go himself; he sends his
messenger Skírnir. Skírnir offers gifts; Gerðr refuses. Skírnir threatens; Gerðr
refuses. Skírnir performs a curse-magic — carving runes that will make her mad with
longing for no one, marked by ugliness, shunned by everyone — and she agrees. She
will come to Freyr in nine days.

The poem is uncomfortable. Skírnir coerces Gerðr's consent through a magical curse;
her agreement is under duress. The poem does not comment on this. It presents the
successful courtship as the successful completion of a difficult mission.

**Þrymskviða (The Lay of Þrymr).** The Poetic Edda's comic masterpiece. Thor wakes
to find Mjölnir gone. Loki borrows Freyja's hawk-shape and flies to Jötunheimr to
investigate. The giant Þrymr has the hammer buried eight leagues under the earth.
He will return it for one price: Freyja as his bride.

Freyja is outraged and refuses. The gods deliberate. Heimdall suggests: dress Thor
as the bride and Loki as the bridesmaid. Thor objects strenuously; the gods convince
him. He is dressed in bridal linen, a veil, keys at his belt, woman's dress.

At Þrymr's hall, the disguised Thor eats one whole ox and eight salmon and drinks
three barrels of mead at the betrothal feast. Loki explains this to an increasingly
suspicious Þrymr as "the bride hasn't eaten for eight days out of bridal excitement."
Þrymr reaches under the veil to kiss his bride and recoils from the burning red eyes.
Loki explains: "she hasn't slept for eight nights." When Mjölnir is brought forward
to bless the marriage — standard Norse wedding ceremony — Thor grabs it, kills Þrymr,
kills everyone else in the hall, and goes home.

The poem is a perfect joke about Thor's dignity and Loki's quick invention. It is also
the clearest instance in the Eddas of violence as comic punchline.

**Lokasenna (Loki's Flyting).** At Ægir's feast hall, Loki returns after being ejected
for killing a servant and begins a systematic *flyting* — a ritualized exchange of
insults — with each god present. He accuses each one specifically and accurately:
Odin of unfair battle-outcome choosing, Frigg of sleeping with her brothers-in-law,
Freyja of sleeping with her brother Freyr, Njörðr of being used as a chamber-pot by
Skadi's kin, Tyr of having lost his hand and his wife both. The accusations are
specific, pointed, and — crucially — the gods do not deny them. They object to Loki
saying these things aloud. Thor arrives and threatens Loki until he leaves.

*Lokasenna* is fascinating because Loki's power here is entirely verbal: he cannot
be stopped by force because that would mean the accusations are confirmed. He can
only be driven out by a threat that outweighs his speech, and only Thor's blunt
"I'll kill you" serves as an argument he'll accept.

**The heroic poems (Sigurd cycle).** *Reginsmál*, *Fáfnismál*, *Sigrdrífumál*,
*Brot af Sigurðarkviðu*, *Guðrúnarkviða*, *Atlakviða*, *Hamðismál* — these cover
the Sigurd material from a different angle than the Volsunga Saga: more compressed,
more emotionally intense, the voice often Gudrun's own lament rather than a narrator's
account. *Atlakviða* is considered one of the oldest poems in the collection and covers
the feast of Atli with a bleakness that makes the prose versions look almost cheerful.

**Völuspá hin skamma (The Short Seeress's Prophecy)** and other minor poems — these
fill out the mythological section with material about the origins of various beings
and events.

### Themes and Morals

The Poetic Edda does not argue a single thesis; it is a collection. But its dominant
voice — heard most clearly in *Völuspá* and *Hávamál* — is the voice of knowledge in
the face of doom. The seeress speaks *Völuspá* to Odin knowing he cannot use the
information; she cannot not speak it. Odin hangs on Yggdrasil knowing the cost;
he cannot not pay it. *Hávamál* advises the traveler on how to behave — with the
implicit assumption that behaving well will eventually be insufficient against fate.

The Eddic world is not resigned; it is lucid. The gods know what is coming. They do
not give up. They act as if action matters, because it does, even when it cannot save
them. This is the specific texture that distinguishes Norse myth from Greek tragic
structure: the Greeks argue whether fate can be changed; the Norse assume it cannot
and act anyway.

### Chapter Notes — by Major Poem

**Völuspá** — The creation, the golden age, the first war (Aesir vs. Vanir), Baldr's
death as the beginning of the end, Ragnarök in full, the renewal. The structuring
arc of the whole mythology in one poem.

**Hávamál** — Practical wisdom in the first section; the rune-learning in the second.
Odin's self-sacrifice is the center: the price of knowledge is the self.

**Vafþrúðnismál** — The wisdom contest. Odin wins the contest with the one question
only Odin could ask. The unanswered question (what did he whisper to Baldr?) is the
poem's most powerful moment.

**Grímnismál** — Odin in disguise, described by the fire, describing the nine worlds
to a boy who does not know who his guest is. The knowledge given to someone who doesn't
yet know how to use it.

**Skírnismál** — Freyr's love and the coerced courtship. The curse-runes that make
consent possible without making it free.

**Þrymskviða** — Thor as bride. The hammer recovered through the comedy of disguise
and the violence of revelation.

**Lokasenna** — The truths no one wanted said aloud. Loki's power is accuracy.
Thor's solution is blunter than argument.

**The heroic poems (Sigurd cycle)** — The same events as FRO, but in the first person
of the grieving, the compressed lyric of *Gudrun's Lament*, the bleakness of *Atlakviða*.

## Cycle 1 — Pass 2: Characters

**The Völva (Seeress)** — The speaker of *Völuspá*. She is presumably dead — called
up from the underworld or from her grave mound — to speak the complete history of the
world to Odin. She begins: *"I remember the giants born of old / those who long ago
reared me up."* She has been here since the beginning. She knows everything. The poem
does not describe her voice; it enacts it.

**Odin in *Hávamál*** — The god of wisdom here is not the Allfather of mythology but
the voice of hard-won experience. He has made mistakes. He has been rejected by a woman
(the *Billings mær* episode: he waited for her all night and the next day found a dog
tied to the bed). He drinks carefully. He knows when to speak and when not to. The
rune-learning section makes this personal: everything he knows cost him.

**Loki in *Lokasenna*** — The Poetic Edda's Loki is more purely verbal than his
prose counterpart. He is not yet a world-destroyer; he is a tongue that cannot stop.
His accusations at Ægir's feast are all true. The gods who object to him do not say
he is lying. They say he is out of order. He continues. He is driven out by Thor's
threat, which is the only argument better than his speech.

**Thor in *Þrymskviða*** — Magnificent and hilariously put-upon. He cannot find the
hammer without Loki's help. He cannot retrieve it without wearing a bridal veil. At
every step of the disguise he wants to abandon it and smash something, and at every
step Loki tells him to keep going. When the moment comes, he abandons the restraint
with spectacular completeness.

## Cycle 1 — Pass 3: The Seeress Speaks

The völva's opening is one of the great theatrical demands in world literature. She
commands silence from all the sacred beings — gods, giants, humans — and then begins
to speak the world's history.

She is not reporting what she has seen. She is performing what she knows, in the formal
shape of prophetic verse, to the one being who has arranged to hear it. Odin is present.
He knows what she is going to say. He paid for it.

She begins with darkness and void. She describes the first beings, the first worlds,
the making of Midgard and humanity, the golden age when the gods played games in the
meadow with golden pieces. Then: the first war (between the Aesir and the Vanir). The
first witch burned three times and rising three times. The beginning of the end of the
golden age.

Then Baldr's death, which she does not dwell on but which she locates precisely as the
hinge.

Then the winter sequences and the final catalog of Ragnarök: Fimbulwinter, the wolves
catching the sun and moon, the world-serpent roiling the ocean floor, the Naglfar
sailing, the fire from Múspell. The gods fight and fall, one by one, exactly as they
were expected to fall.

And then — in a few stanzas that feel different in tone from everything that preceded,
lighter, full of a strange tentative hope — the world renewed. The earth rises. The
fields bear grain unplanted. The gods meet again and find the golden pieces of their
games scattered in the new grass. Baldr comes back.

*She sees, coming up / a second time out of the sea / the earth from the waves,
green and growing again.*

She speaks the renewal. Odin has paid for the whole vision — not just the doom but
what comes after. Whether he did this to know the doom or to know that something comes
after the doom is not stated.

*"Now I must sink,"* she says. And the poem ends.

What she received for the prophecy is described in *Völuspá*'s opening stanzas: Odin
gives her rings and necklaces. The price of the world's total history is some jewelry.
This is either deliberately ironic or the most matter-of-fact transaction in mythology.
Both, probably.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

**1. After Völuspá** — The seeress has spoken. Odin goes. She sinks back to wherever
she came from. But the rings he gave her — the price of the prophecy — must be
returned to a specific place for the transaction to be complete. The Fighter carries
the rings to the stone at the world's edge.

**2. Loki's Escape from Ægir's Feast** — Loki has said everything he came to say.
Thor threatens him. He leaves. As he goes, he drops one thing — a carved rune-chip
with the most dangerous of his accusations inscribed on it. It must not reach the
gods. The Fighter carries it before someone finds it.

**3. Thor's Veil** — Thor, disguised as Þrymr's bride, has left his red beard only
partially concealed and a breeze is coming up. Loki needs something to hold the veil
down until the hammer appears. The Fighter carries a weighted veil-pin from the
wagons at the back of the procession to Loki's hands before Þrymr looks up.

**4. The Unanswered Question** — Odin asked Vafþrúðnir what he whispered in Baldr's
ear. The answer exists somewhere — the giant knew, even if he couldn't say it. A
carved bone found in the ruin of his hall has the answer on it. The Fighter carries
the bone to a place where it can be safely read.

**5. The Runes' Origin** — Odin has just cut himself down from Yggdrasil with the
eighteen runes in him. He does not yet know how to use them. He needs to carry them
to the first stone where they will be taught to humanity. The Fighter escorts the
newly rune-carrying Odin through the section of Yggdrasil's roots where Níðhöggr
gnaws — the serpent that would eat the runes if it could reach them.

**6. The Hammer Blessing** — Mjölnir is brought out at Þrymr's feast to bless the
marriage. At the moment Thor grabs it, the hammer is briefly not in any hand — in the
air between the priest's hands and Thor's reach. The Fighter must ensure it is not
dropped and lost in the chaos that follows.

**7. Gudrun's First Lament** — Gudrun sits beside Sigurd's body at dawn and cannot
weep. The other women try and fail to make her cry; grief trapped is grief that grows
inside. Gullrond lifts the cloth from Sigurd's face. Gudrun weeps. The Fighter must
carry Gudrun's weeping — her lament, inscribed in that moment by a scribe who heard
it — from the hall to the place where it will be preserved.

about a seeress speaking forward-facing prophecy that cannot change what it predicts.

delivered to change a specific person's mind; the seeress's prophecy is a total vision
of history delivered to someone who cannot use it to change anything.

its outcome; the *Völuspá* prophecy changes nothing.

**New theme introduced by RKV:**
*"The prophecy that must be spoken even though it changes nothing: truth that has no
utility but cannot be withheld"* — the völva speaks *Völuspá* to Odin knowing he
already knows everything she is about to say; he cannot prevent Ragnarök; she cannot
withhold the vision; both of them understand this, and the speaking happens anyway,
because some truths exist to be spoken, not acted on; the Fighter carries the proof
of the transaction — the rings Odin paid — to the place where payments are kept.

This is distinct from all 17 existing themes.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: "After Völuspá"

**The Token · A pair of rings — gold, plain, old as the world's first transaction ·
given by Odin to the seeress as the price of the world's total history ·
she no longer holds them · they must reach the stone at the field's edge
where all such payments are kept**

---

### Act I — The Empty Ground
*The Rings · in the fighter's palm · the heath where the seeress was, pre-dawn ·
Odin gone, the grass flattened where they stood, the rings on the ground*

The heath is empty. The grass is flattened in a rough circle — the shape of where
they stood. In the center of the circle: two rings, gold, lying in the grass.

You did not see the encounter. You were on the road when the light changed, when the
air became the kind of air that means something is being said that cannot be taken
back. By the time you reached the heath, they were gone. The rings were there.

Someone left a message — not in words but in what was left behind. The rings must go
somewhere. You pick them up.

*They are cold. They have been warm recently.*

---

### Act II — The Road Through the Village
*The Rings · in the fighter's coat pocket · the village on the road between the heath
and the field's edge, morning market*

The village has woken up. The morning market is assembling. The light is different
today — the specific quality of light after something large has happened somewhere
in the invisible register of things. Not everyone feels it. A few people do.

An old man at the market corner is one of them. He can see the rings through your
coat somehow — or see the weight of them. He wants to know where they came from.

*He is not threatening. He is one of the people who knows what kind of rings
these are and where they belong. He might be the right person to tell.*

---

### Act III — The River Crossing
*The Rings · in the fighter's hand · a river ford, the field's edge visible across it ·
the water running fast for early morning*

The ford. Runoff from somewhere upstream has brought the water up — knee-deep now,
fast-running, the bottom uneven. The field on the far side is visible: a standing stone
at its edge, a pale greyish shape in the morning light.

Carrying small, precious things through fast water requires a specific kind of attention.
Both hands occupied or both hands free.

*The rings are small. The water is fast. The field is close.*

---

### Act IV — The Standing Stone
*The Rings · almost delivered · in the fighter's hands · the standing stone at the
field's edge, carved, older than the road*

The standing stone at the field's edge is carved with marks that are older than the
current system of runes — an older system, a prior language, the notation of a
transaction type that has been occurring since before anyone now living was born.

There is a hollow at the stone's base: a natural gap between the stone and the
ground. Other things have been placed there before. There is a method to this.

The old man from the village is here. He has been here longer than you have. He is
watching. He is not in the way. He is a witness.

*How rings are placed in such a hollow is a matter of form. Form matters to this
kind of payment.*

---

### Act V — The Placement
*The Rings · the last inch · between the fighter's fingers and the stone's hollow ·
the standing stone, dawn light, the old man watching*

The field is quiet. The old man is standing back. The morning light is the specific
flat light of early dawn before color comes in.

You place the rings in the hollow. There is a moment — you feel it through your
fingers — when the rings settle in as if the hollow was made for them, which it was.

The payment is complete. The transaction is recorded — not in writing, not in anyone's
memory, but in the account-keeping that this stone performs, which is the oldest kind.

The old man nods once. He begins walking back toward the village.

*You receive the Stone-Witness's Carved Chip — a small fragment of the standing stone's
carved surface that came away in your hand when you placed the rings; a small proof
of the placement, warm now from your grip.*
