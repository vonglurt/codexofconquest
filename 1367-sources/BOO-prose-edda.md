## Cycle 1 — Pass 1: Full Telling

### Historical and Literary Context

Snorri Sturluson composed the Prose Edda (*Snorra Edda*) in Iceland around 1220 — a
remarkable act of cultural preservation. By 1220, Christianity had been the official
religion of Iceland for two centuries, and the old mythological vocabulary that formed
the basis of skaldic verse was becoming opaque. A poet who used kennings like "Ymir's
skull" (the sky) or "Odin's gift" (a poem) needed an audience who knew Ymir and Odin.
Snorri wrote the Prose Edda partly as a textbook for poets — explaining the kennings
requires explaining the myths, which requires telling the stories.

The result is the primary surviving prose account of Norse mythology: the creation
of the world, the gods, the monsters, and the eventual destruction and renewal at
Ragnarök. Without the Prose Edda, much of what we know about Norse myth would be
fragmentary. Snorri was aware of the tension in his project: he was a Christian
writing a loving, detailed account of a pagan mythological system he officially rejected.
His framing device in *Gylfaginning* — the myth is told by three mysterious beings to
a king who may be being tricked — gives him some cover: these are stories, not doctrine.

The Prose Edda has three major sections:

**Gylfaginning** (The Tricking of Gylfi) — King Gylfi of Sweden travels to Ásgarðr
in disguise to learn the secrets of the Aesir gods. He encounters three figures —
High, Just-as-High, and Third — who answer his questions. This is the primary narrative
of Norse cosmology.

**Skáldskaparmál** (The Language of Poetry) — A dialogue explaining the kennings of
skaldic verse. Contains extensive embedded mythology, including the story of the mead
of poetry.

**Háttatal** (A Catalog of Meters) — A technical poem demonstrating 102 verse forms.
Primarily of interest to poets and scholars.

### The Stories in Full

**The Creation.** In the beginning there is Ginnungagap — the yawning void. To its
north is Niflheim, a realm of ice and cold. To its south is Múspellsheim, a realm of
fire. The rivers of Niflheim flow into the void and freeze. The heat of Múspellsheim
contacts the ice and the melting produces Ymir, the first frost-giant, and the cow
Auðhumla who nourishes him. Auðhumla licks the ice; from the ice she licks comes
Búri, ancestor of the gods. His descendants Odin, Vili, and Vé kill Ymir, whose blood
drowns almost all the giants. From Ymir's body they make the world: his flesh the
earth, his blood the sea, his bones the mountains, his skull the sky, his brain the
clouds, his eyebrows the fence around Midgard (the realm of men). The dwarves were
made from the maggots in Ymir's flesh.

**The Cosmology.** Yggdrasil, the world-ash, stands at the center of nine worlds. Its
roots reach to three wells: Urðarbrunnr (the Well of Fate, where the three Norns sit
weaving destiny), Mímisbrunnr (Mímir's Well of wisdom, where Odin sacrificed his eye
for a drink), and Hvergelmir in Niflheim. The serpent Níðhöggr gnaws the root in
Niflheim. Four stags eat the branches. The eagle at the top and the serpent below
exchange insults through the squirrel Ratatoskr who runs up and down the trunk.

**The Gods.** The Aesir are the primary pantheon: Odin (Allfather, god of wisdom,
war, and poetry; sacrificed himself on Yggdrasil for nine nights to win the runes),
Thor (thunder, the strongest, enemy of giants, wielder of Mjölnir), Frigg (Odin's
wife, goddess of foresight), Freyr and Freyja (from the Vanir, the other divine family,
joined to the Aesir by treaty), Loki (shape-changer, divine trickster, father of
monsters, eventually the engine of Ragnarök), Baldr (the most beloved of the gods,
shining, good, fated to die), Tyr (sacrifice and law, who lost his hand to the wolf
Fenrir), Heimdall (watchman of the gods, guardian of the Bifrost bridge), Höðr
(Baldr's blind brother), and others.

**The Death of Baldr.** This is the Prose Edda's central narrative and the hinge of
everything that follows. Baldr begins to have dreams of his own death. Frigg, his
mother, goes to all things in existence and extracts oaths not to harm him: fire and
water, iron and all metals, stones, earth, trees, diseases, beasts, birds, and
serpents. Everything swears. The gods then amuse themselves by throwing things at
Baldr, who is now invulnerable. It is a parlor game of invincibility.

Loki disguises himself as an old woman and visits Frigg. Through conversation, he
discovers that Frigg did not bother to ask the mistletoe — it seemed too young and
small to bother with. Loki takes a sprig of mistletoe and goes to where the gods are
at play. He finds the blind god Höðr standing apart. He guides Höðr's hand to throw
the mistletoe at Baldr.

The dart goes through Baldr. Baldr falls. He is dead.

The gods are silent. They cannot avenge the death in the sacred space where they stand,
and they cannot undo it. Odin speaks a word in Baldr's ear before the funeral — the
saga does not say what it was.

Baldr's wife Nanna dies of grief at the pyre and is burned with him. The pyre is so
large that Hringhorni, the greatest of ships, is used. The dwarf Litr runs in front
of it and Thor kicks him into the fire. This is presented without explanation.

Odin places his gold ring Draupnir on the pyre. Draupnir drips eight new rings every
ninth night — it is the ring of endless wealth.

Hermoðr rides to Hel on Sleipnir to beg for Baldr's return. Hel says: if everything
in the world weeps for Baldr, he can return. If one thing refuses, he stays.

Frigg sends messengers in every direction asking all things to weep. Everything weeps:
trees, stones, metal, animals, the earth itself. On their return journey, the messengers
find a giantess named Þökk sitting in a cave. They ask her to weep. She refuses.
She says: *Baldr's life was nothing to me. Let Hel keep what she has.*

Her refusal is sufficient. Baldr stays in Hel until after Ragnarök.

It is Loki. Everyone understands this at the time and the narrative confirms it. Loki
in the shape of Þökk is the one thing that would not weep.

**The Mead of Poetry.** This is told in *Skáldskaparmál*. The Aesir and the Vanir made
a truce. As a seal of the peace, all gods spat into a vessel. From this mingled spittle
was made Kvasir — a being so wise that he could answer any question. Kvasir traveled
the world spreading wisdom. Two dwarves, Fjalarr and Galarr, killed him and drained his
blood into two vats and a pot. Mixed with honey, the blood became the mead of poetry:
anyone who drinks it becomes a skald or a scholar. The dwarves told the gods that Kvasir
had choked on his own wisdom.

The mead passed through several hands by murder and theft until it ended up with the
giant Suttungr, who locked it in a mountain cave guarded by his daughter Gunnlöð.
Odin, in the form of Bölverkr, worked for Suttungr's brother Baugi for a summer and
earned the right to try to retrieve the mead. He drilled into the mountain with an
auger called Rati, transformed into a serpent, and slithered inside. He spent three
nights with Gunnlöð and drank all three vessels. He transformed into an eagle and flew
back to Ásgarðr with the mead in his crop. Suttungr pursued him as another eagle. Odin
spit the mead into containers the gods had prepared. In his haste, some fell onto the
earth below: this is inferior poetry, available to anyone. The good mead he gave to the
gods and to certain worthy mortals — the skaldic gift of poetry.

**Ragnarök.** The Prose Edda's account is the most complete surviving description. The
sequence: Fimbulwinter (three years of winter without summer); the breaking of all oaths
and bonds; Loki escapes his imprisonment and sails to the final battle on the ship
Naglfar made from the nails of the dead; Fenrir breaks his chain; Jörmungandr rises
from the ocean; the Midgard serpent poisons the sea; the Bifrost bridge breaks under the
weight of Múspell's fire-riders. At the field of Vígríðr the final battle is fought.
Thor kills Jörmungandr and walks nine paces before dying from its venom. Odin is swallowed
by Fenrir; Víðarr kills Fenrir in revenge. Freyr is killed by Surtr because he gave away
his sword to win Gerðr. Loki and Heimdall kill each other. Surtr floods the world with
fire.

Then the world is renewed: the earth rises from the sea. Baldr and Höðr come back from
Hel. Surviving gods find the golden game-pieces the Aesir used for their games — scattered
in the old grass, undamaged.

**The frame collapse.** At the end of *Gylfaginning*, the hall vanishes and Gylfi stands
in an empty field. The three figures are gone. He goes home and tells men these stories,
which is how they became known.

### Themes and Morals

The Prose Edda's deepest argument is about knowledge and its cost. Odin hangs on
Yggdrasil and sacrifices his eye for wisdom: every gift of understanding is paid for.
The mead of poetry is made from murder — the wisest being ever made is killed to produce
the liquid that enables wisdom in others. The cosmos is intrinsically mortgaged: the
present is bought with a price that will eventually be called in at Ragnarök.

Baldr's death is the exception that proves the rule. He is the one being in the world
who is only good. When he dies, something essential goes out. The gods' inability to
grieve properly — they know Loki was Þökk, and they cannot act on this knowledge within
the rules of the sacred space — is the deepest image in the text of the cosmos's
structural unfairness.

### Chapter Notes — by Section

**Gylfaginning: The Frame** — Gylfi's journey to the hall, the three figures who answer
his questions. The frame establishes: this is a story about information obtained under
false pretenses, which may or may not be reliable. Snorri is careful to make this a
fiction-within-fiction.

**Gylfaginning: Creation** — Ymir, Auðhumla, Búri, the world-body. The cosmological
violence of making: the world is a dead giant.

**Gylfaginning: The World-Tree and the Cosmology** — Yggdrasil, the nine worlds, the
wells, the inhabitants of each region. The structural grammar of the cosmos.

**Gylfaginning: The Gods** — A catalog of the Aesir with their roles and attributes.
Loki's children — Fenrir, Jörmungandr, Hel — assigned to their prison roles.

**Gylfaginning: Baldr's Death** — The central mythological tragedy. Mistletoe, Höðr,
the pyre, Hermoðr's ride, the weeping of all things, Þökk's refusal.

**Gylfaginning: Loki's Punishment** — Loki is captured, bound with the entrails of his
son, a serpent dripping venom over his face. His wife Sigyn holds a bowl; when she
empties it, venom falls and Loki writhes and causes earthquakes.

**Gylfaginning: Ragnarök** — The sequence of the world's end, weapon by weapon.

**Gylfaginning: The Renewal** — Baldr's return, the new world, the golden pieces found
in the old grass.

**Skáldskaparmál: The Mead of Poetry** — Kvasir, the murder, the vats, the mountain,
Odin's theft, the gift.

**Skáldskaparmál: Kenning catalog** — The embedded stories explaining kennings for
the sea, for gold, for battle, for men and women.

## Cycle 1 — Pass 2: Characters

**Odin (Allfather)** — The god who never stops paying for knowledge. He sacrificed
his eye at Mímir's well, hung himself on Yggdrasil for nine nights to learn the runes,
and knows Ragnarök is coming and cannot stop it. He speaks a word in Baldr's ear before
the funeral and the word is never revealed. He is the god of poetry and war and wisdom
and also the god who permitted the world's most beloved being to die. The Prose Edda
treats this not as evil but as the cost of being the Allfather: he knows what is
necessary and pays it.

**Baldr** — The simplest and most absolute figure in the pantheon: good, beloved,
shining. The saga describes him with a luminosity that belongs to fairy tale, not history.
He is the only thing in the world that everyone agrees is worth preserving, and he is
the first thing lost. His absence after his death is the wound the cosmos cannot close
until renewal.

**Loki** — The engine of the Prose Edda's tragedy, and the most complex being in it.
He assists the gods repeatedly, creatively, and with genuine affection — and then he
kills Baldr. The transition from helper to destroyer is not clearly motivated in the
text; the saga treats it as inevitable without quite explaining why. He is the cosmos's
principle of change made personal: things change because Loki changes them, and he
cannot not change them.

**Frigg** — Her mistake is asking everything except the mistletoe, which she thought
too young and small to matter. This is not negligence but the error of a mother who
cannot imagine the world being as cruel as it is. She asked almost everything. The gap
between *almost* and *everything* is the entire tragedy.

**Þökk** — The giantess who will not weep. She appears for one stanza and decides the
fate of Baldr, the world's griever, all the gods. The text confirms she is Loki.
She is the smallest unit of refusal that the cosmos can produce — a single voice in a
cave on the way back from everywhere — and it is sufficient.

**Hermoðr** — The one who rides to Hel. He is barely characterized; he is the messenger-
function in human form. He rides Sleipnir, asks for Baldr, receives the condition. He
is the person who has to come back and tell Frigg that there is a condition, and then
the condition is not met.

## Cycle 1 — Pass 3: Þökk's Cave

The messengers have been everywhere. They are returning from every direction with the
same report: weeping. Trees, stones, metals, diseases, animals — the whole inventory
of creation. Everything the messengers found added to the count of the world's grief.
The bowl they carry is nearly full.

There is one more stop. A cave in the rocks at the side of a mountain road. A giantess
sitting inside, called Þökk. She is large and grey and is warming her hands at a small
fire. She looks up when the messenger comes to the cave mouth.

The messenger explains: Baldr has died. Hel will return him if everything weeps. Will
Þökk weep for Baldr?

She considers this.

Then she says — and the Prose Edda gives her exact words in verse — that Baldr was
nothing to her. Let Hel keep what she has. She does not weep.

The messenger looks at her.

The saga does not describe what the messenger sees in her face or how long the messenger
looks. It only says that Þökk refuses, and that her refusal is understood to be Loki's
work.

What would it mean to *see* it? To look at a large grey giantess warming her hands and
recognize, in the specific quality of her indifference, the specific quality of Loki's
indifference — to know that this is not a giantess who didn't care about Baldr, but
someone who cared very much about keeping Baldr in Hel?

The bowl doesn't fill. Baldr doesn't return. But the messenger knows what the refusal
was and who made it.

This knowledge is not nothing. It is, in fact, the only true account of what happened.
Odin's ravens bring him news, but they bring him events. Only the messenger was in
the cave when Þökk refused. Only the messenger can testify to what was in her eyes.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

**1. Þökk's Cave** — Frigg's messenger carries a bowl that accumulates the world's
grief as each thing weeps for Baldr. The last stop is a cave on a mountain road where a
giantess named Þökk refuses — and the Fighter must look at her face in the firelight
and recognize what she is.

**2. The Mead-Theft Night** — Odin has drilled into Suttungr's mountain with the auger
Rati. He is inside, negotiating with Gunnlöð over the mead of poetry. The Fighter waits
outside at the drill-hole to warn him if Suttungr returns, carrying the auger as the
token — the only way back out.

**3. Loki's Capture** — After the gods learn what Loki did at Baldr's funeral, they
chase him. He transforms into a salmon and hides in a waterfall. The Fighter must carry
the net the gods made (the first net, invented for this purpose) to the waterfall without
Loki seeing it coming.

**4. The Rune-Learning** — Odin hangs on Yggdrasil for nine nights, pierced with a
spear, learning the runes. Someone must bring him water at the end of the ninth night.
The Fighter carries a horn of water to the tree, past the roots where Níðhöggr gnaws
and the eagle waits.

**5. The Bound Fenrir** — The gods bind Fenrir with the ribbon Gleipnir. But someone
must carry the ribbon to where Fenrir is, and Fenrir will only allow himself to be bound
if a god places a hand in his mouth as a pledge. The Fighter carries Gleipnir and must
decide who to ask.

**6. Draupnir's Return** — Hermoðr returns from Hel carrying Draupnir (the gold ring
Odin placed on Baldr's pyre) as Baldr's token of thanks. The ring must reach Odin
before sunrise or the gods will interpret its delay as a bad omen. The Fighter carries
it through the pre-dawn hours of Ásgarðr.

**7. The Golden Pieces** — After Ragnarök, two surviving gods find the golden
game-pieces the Aesir used for their games — scattered in the new grass. The Fighter
carries them back to the rebuilt hall, one by one, because each piece is a name
and each name that arrives changes what can be remembered.

**Themes I am avoiding:**

"Grief carried in an object" (INV, BHD, GDN) — the grief-bowl accumulates weeping, but
those quests are about objects that hold a dead person's presence. This quest is about
the process of collective consent being broken by a single refusal.

"Ancient witnesses" (KIR) — that quest is about testimony accumulated across deep time;
this is about one present-moment refusal negating all accumulated assent.

"Truth delivered posthumously" (LGW) — Baldr is dead but the truth is about who killed
him, not about delivering his truth after death.

**New theme introduced by BOO:**
*"The one who will not weep: when a single refusal outweighs the world's willingness"*
— Frigg asks everything to weep; everything agrees; one voice in one cave says no; the
world's entire accumulated grief cannot outweigh it; the Fighter witnesses the refusal
and recognizes it for what it is; the recognition is the only possible form of success
when the outcome cannot be changed.

This is distinct from all 15 existing themes.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: "Þökk's Refusal"

**The Token · A shallow carved bowl, half-full with what the world has given —
a stone's cold drop, a birch tree's resin, a raven's tear in the right season ·
carried from thing to thing by Frigg's messenger · nearly full now ·
one more thing to ask**

---

### Act I — The Commission
*The Bowl · empty · in Frigg's hands · Ásgarðr, the hall, after the pyre has gone cold*

The hall is full of gods who cannot speak. Baldr is dead. The pyre is ash. The smoke
is still in the room.

Frigg is standing. She is the one who asked everything to swear, and she asks you
now — not a god, not someone with anything to lose — to carry the bowl to every
living and unliving thing on the road north, and ask each thing to add to it.

She carves the bowl from ash-wood herself, there in the hall. She hands it to you.

*"Everything agreed to swear. Everything should be willing to weep. Come back when
it is full."*

She does not say: come back when Baldr is free. She says: come back when it is full.
She already suspects the bowl will come back not-full. She sends you anyway.

*The bowl is light in your hands. It is the lightest thing you have ever been given.*

---

### Act II — The World That Weeps
*The Bowl · filling · in the fighter's hands · a woodland road, stone and birch,
morning light*

The trees weep when you ask them. The stones give a cold drop when you hold the bowl
to them. Animals come to the bowl without being asked — a deer bows its head over
the rim, a raven lands on the bowl's edge and bends its neck. The world is grieving.
Baldr was loved by everything that had the capacity to love.

But one thing will not weep at first. A thorn-bush at the road's edge. It says: I was
never warm enough in Baldr's light. I grew in shade. Ask the trees who grew in sun.

It is small. It seems small. But every refusal now matters.

*The bowl is almost full. You have one stop left, at the end of the north road.*

---

### Act III — The Mountain Pass
*The Bowl · nearly full · in both hands, held level · a rockslide blocking the
mountain pass, loose stone, unstable ledge*

The north road goes through a pass between two peaks. The pass is blocked by a recent
rockfall — a slope of loose shale and larger stones, unstable underfoot, no path clear.
The bowl cannot be tilted. The bowl cannot be put down: on the slope there is nowhere
flat to set it.

Somewhere behind the rockfall, the cave is visible — a dark mouth in the cliff-face
on the other side.

*One wrong step and the bowl tips. The world's grief spills on a mountain in the dark
and no one sees it.*

---

### Act IV — Þökk's Cave
*The Bowl · full to the brim · in the fighter's hands · a cave in the cliff-face,
a grey giantess and a small fire*

The cave is low. The giantess is very large and sits folded, warming her hands. She
looks up when you come to the cave mouth. She has grey eyes and the particular
stillness of someone who has been waiting for this specific visitor.

You ask: will she weep for Baldr?

She says, very clearly, that Baldr was nothing to her. Let Hel keep what she has.

She says this in verse. It is polished verse — carefully composed, not improvised.

You look at her face.

*Something in the firelight. Something in the quality of the stillness. Not a giantess
who didn't care about Baldr. Someone who cared very much about something else.*

---

### Act V — The Return
*The Bowl · full of everything except one drop · in the fighter's hands · Ásgarðr,
Frigg's hall, the assembled gods*

Frigg is standing in the same place she was when you left. All the gods are present.
They can tell from your face that the bowl is not complete.

She looks at the bowl. She looks at you.

You tell her what you saw in the cave — not just the refusal, but what the refusal
looked like. The polished verse. The specific quality of the indifference. The grey
eyes in the firelight.

She already knew. She did not need you to tell her. But she needed a witness who was
not a god, who was not already a party to what comes next, who can say: I was in the
cave and this is what I saw.

*The bowl is taken. You receive Frigg's Silver Hairpin — pressed into your hand
without a word, the small token of what the night required.*

## Cycle 2 — The Mead-Theft Night

**What cycle 1 missed:** Þökk's Refusal was the outside of divine grief — the grief-embassy, the accumulated world-weeping, the one voice that undid all of it. The Mead-Theft Night reveals the interior of Odin's acquisition of poetry itself: what it cost, who was paid, and what the Fighter's role was in holding the extraction point open during the most dangerous negotiation in Norse mythology. Cycle 1 was about what the world refuses. Cycle 2 is about what the world requires in silence.

**New angle:** The vigil at the drill-hole — Odin is inside Suttungr's mountain with the mead of poetry, negotiating. The exit is the hole he bored with the auger Rati. The Fighter holds the auger at the mouth of that hole for three nights. When Suttungr returns, the auger is the only thing between the hole staying open and the hole being sealed shut with Odin still inside.

---

### Pass 1 — Token

**TOKEN: Rati the Auger** — the great boring-drill used to pierce through Hnitbjörg, Suttungr's mountain vault. Odin gives it to the Fighter before he transforms into a worm and enters the hole. It is the extraction mechanism: the guarantee that the inside act has a path out. The Fighter holds it for three nights at the drill-hole mouth. When Suttungr's mountain-guard comes and when Suttungr himself arrives in the last seconds, the auger is braced across the hole entrance as a bar and a brace. When Odin exits as an eagle, the auger is released. The token's task is complete at the moment the eagle clears the mountain top.

---

### Pass 2 — Seven Storyline Ideas

**1. Þökk's Cave** *(USED — Cycle 1)* — Frigg's grief-embassy. The bowl. The one who would not weep.

**2. The Mead-Theft Night** *(CHOSEN — Cycle 2)* — Odin inside Suttungr's mountain. Fighter holds the auger at the drill-hole for three nights. When Suttungr returns, the auger is the brace that keeps the exit open.

**3. Loki's Capture** — The first net, carried to the waterfall without Loki seeing it coming.

**4. The Rune-Learning** — A horn of water carried to Yggdrasil at the end of the ninth night.

**5. The Bound Fenrir** — Gleipnir carried to where Fenrir is; the Fighter must decide who to ask to place a hand in his mouth.

**6. Draupnir's Return** — Hermoðr returns from Hel with Draupnir. Ring must reach Odin before sunrise.

**7. The Golden Pieces** — After Ragnarök, scattered golden game-pieces in new grass. Each piece is a name.

### Pass 3 — Theme Check

Existing themes through RIX cycle 2: grief in an object, loyalty under the long watch, honor vs. shame, love and fate intertwined, truth delivered posthumously, healing through hostile law, carrier becomes hunted, token's journey, ancient witnesses, corruption wears mercy's face, fate through the broken thing, heroism earns protection for none, invitation as weapon, poem ransoms a life, anonymous sympathy, enabling condition (VBY cycle 2), pyre-arrangements, proxy errand, absent jurisdiction, scattered truth, last gift against design, credential carried before the man, one who will not weep.

**Themes I am avoiding:** "The enabling condition" (VBY cycle 2) — that was the torch-carrier, the one who makes the interior act possible by staying still and lit; this is different because the auger is not just enabling but is the guaranteed exit route — the inside act has no completion without it. "Loyalty under the long watch" (LHR, STN, TBS) — those were about faithfulness over a sustained period; this is about a single technical precision at a single critical moment after three nights of waiting.

**New theme introduced by BOO cycle 2:** *The vigil at the extraction point: the tool that must stay in exactly one position while the world rearranges itself around it; the carrier who does nothing for three nights and everything in one second* — Odin bored the hole and entered; the inside act is entirely his; the Fighter's three nights of waiting are purely maintenance of a position; but the one second when Suttungr arrives and the auger must be braced is the entire commission; without that second the hole closes, the mead stays inside, and poetry belongs to giants permanently.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: "The Mead-Theft Night"

**The Token · Rati the Auger · a boring-drill the length of a man's arm ·
iron head still trailing mountain dust · warm from the work it just finished ·
pressed into the Fighter's hands by a one-eyed man
who has just transformed into a worm and is about to enter a mountain ·
what it opens must stay open**

---

### Act I — The Commission
*Rati the Auger · still warm from boring · Hnitbjörg's outer slope,
the drill-hole mouth, pine-smoke from Suttungr's hall below*

The one-eyed man hands you the auger and does not explain very much.

He has borrowed it from Baugi, Suttungr's brother, after a summer of work in the fields under a false name, and he has used it to bore through the granite of Hnitbjörg in a single night. The hole is there — a clean dark circle in the stone, about the width of a man's wrist. He hands you the auger.

*"If Suttungr comes, I need the hole open. Don't let anyone seal it. If you hear a specific sound—"* he describes a sound: rock grinding against rock, from below the tree-line, a specific cadence that means the giant is returning from the coast road, not the high path. *"That sound. Then brace the auger across the mouth. Do not remove it until an eagle has gone over the ridge."*

He does not explain the eagle. He transforms into a worm. He enters the hole. He is gone.

The mountain is quiet. The drill-hole mouth is in front of you and the auger is in your hands and you now know exactly one thing you are supposed to do.

**DC 12 Insight — hold the specific sound in memory; know the difference between that sound and all the other sounds this mountain makes.**

*If you fail:* You hold the sound pattern for one night, then two; on the third night the memory blurs and you add too much margin. When Suttungr returns you are uncertain and braced early, which costs you.

*If you pass:* The sound is exact in your mind. You could identify it at half-distance on a windy night. You settle in front of the drill-hole and wait.

---

### Act II — Three Nights
*Rati the Auger · in the Fighter's hands, then leaned against the stone ·
the Hnitbjörg slope, first night, second night, third night*

The first night is cold and the pine trees move in a way that sounds like something approaching. It is not. The second night is still and one of Suttungr's field-workers passes on the low path with a lamp, not looking up. The third night it rains and the sound of the rain on the stone combines with the sound of the mountain settling and with the sound of your own breathing and with the sound of something you can't quite identify below the tree-line.

Nothing has happened. Three nights of nothing. The auger is propped against the stone beside you. The drill-hole is exactly as it was. The mountain has not changed.

*The third night the rain stops before dawn. The mountain is completely quiet. This is
not the silence of nothing happening. This is the silence of something about to.*

**DC 12 Composure — hold the vigil for three nights without drifting; stay calibrated, not tense.**

*If you fail:* By the third night you are over-alert, jumping at every sound. When the real sound comes you process it a beat late.

*If you pass:* Three nights. You have not moved from the drill-hole mouth except to eat and to relieve yourself thirty feet away, always in sight of the hole. You know this silence. You are ready.

---

### Act III — The Sound
*Rati the Auger · in the Fighter's hands · pre-dawn, the slope above the tree-line*

There. That sound.

Rock against rock from the coast road, the specific cadence he described — not the high path, the coast road, which means Suttungr did not go where he was supposed to go, he went where he always goes when he wants to check on the vault, and now he is three hundred feet below the tree-line on the direct route to the hole.

You have the sound correct. You now need to confirm it is Suttungr and not the mountain itself, not a patrol, not Baugi, not someone with torches who is simply walking a road and making noise.

*The cadence comes again. Regular. Purposeful. Heavy. This is not random movement.*

**DC 13 Perception — distinguish Suttungr's specific approach from every other thing this mountain produces before dawn.**

*If you fail:* You wait too long to confirm and lose ground. You get the auger braced but in a rushed way that costs you leverage.

*If you pass:* You hear the pattern three times in six minutes and it is exactly what he described. You lift the auger, brace it horizontally across the drill-hole mouth, and wait.

---

### Act IV — The Guard
*Rati the Auger · braced across the drill-hole mouth · the slope, pre-dawn,
footsteps now twenty feet below the ridge*

Suttungr did not come alone. Ahead of him — a scout, one of his mountain-guards, who reaches the ridge before the giant does and whose job is to check the outer slope for anything that doesn't belong.

He reaches the ridge and he sees you. He sees the hole behind you. He sees the auger braced across it.

He knows what the hole is and who bored it and what is inside. He does not speak. He moves.

**Combat — Suttungr's Mountain-Guard (AC 14, HP 26, weapon: stone-headed war-club)**

*If you fail (reduced to 0 HP):* The guard gets the auger clear of the hole. By the time you recover, he has half-sealed the entrance with a wedged stone. When Suttungr arrives, the hole is compromised and the eagle's exit is damaged.

*If you pass:* The guard goes down before the ridge. You get back to the drill-hole and re-brace the auger. Suttungr's heavy steps are on the path below. You hear him coming.

---

### Act V — The Exit
*Rati the Auger · braced across the drill-hole mouth · dawn breaking over the ridge ·
Suttungr's hand reaching for the stone above the hole*

Suttungr is at the ridge. He is not looking at you — he is looking at the hole, at the auger braced across it, and his hand goes to the stone above the entrance to lever the auger free.

From inside the hole — sound. A dry, hard sound, the sound of something very large moving very fast through a very small space, getting larger.

You hold the auger.

Suttungr's hand is on the stone. He is enormous and he is angry and he is faster than you and in approximately two seconds he will have the auger free and the hole sealed and whatever is inside will stay inside.

The sound from inside the hole is louder now. It is almost here.

*Rati the Auger is braced between both your hands and the stone walls of the hole.
The whole commission reduces to this: hold it for two more seconds.*

**DC 11 Composure — do not release until the eagle is past.**

*If you fail:* You release a half-second early. The auger swings free. Suttungr's hand closes on the stone. The hole narrows — not sealed, but damaged — and the eagle comes through at a wrong angle. Three drops of mead fall to the ground. Odin flies on, carrying the rest. Three drops are enough for lesser poets. You watch them soak into the stone.

*If you pass:* You hold it. The eagle comes through the hole at full speed and goes over the ridge in one motion — enormous, each wingbeat a concussion against the air, the smell of stone and mead and ancient mountain-dark — and it is gone before Suttungr's hand can close. You release the auger. Suttungr stands at the drill-hole with nothing in it and the sky empty above him.

You pick up Rati from the ground where you dropped it. It is yours now. He won't ask for it back.

You have a small clay vessel in your belt-pouch — you don't know when it got there, but it's sealed with beeswax and something inside it moves like liquid. One drop of mead landed on your hand when the eagle passed. You pressed it into the vessel before you thought about it. It is the skaldic mead. A single drop. The Aesir will not know. It is enough to make one poem true.
