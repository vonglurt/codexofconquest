## Pass 1 — Full Telling (All Twelve Tales)

### Introduction (Lady Charlotte Guest)

Guest argues that the Welsh *Mabinogion* is the source-bed of medieval Arthurian romance — that Chrétien de Troyes drew directly from Welsh originals transmitted through Norman-Welsh contact. She distinguishes two layers in the collection: the older Four Branches (purer Celtic myth, animistic and strange) and the Three Romances (already dressed in Norman tournament culture, already pointing toward Lancelot and the Round Table). The primary manuscript is the Red Book of Hergest, composed roughly 1375–1425 but drawing on material centuries older.

### The Three Romances

**The Lady of the Fountain**

Kynon rides a mysterious road into the wilderness on the advice of a black giant; finds a magic fountain; pours water on a marble slab with a silver bowl — and a Black Knight appears to defend it. Kynon is defeated and returns to Arthur's court. Owain son of Urien goes the same road, fights the Black Knight, mortally wounds him, and follows him into a castle — trapped between two falling portcullis-slabs, the dying knight inside, Owain inside with him. The knight's handmaiden Luned gives Owain a ring of invisibility; he watches the knight's funeral procession, falls in love with the Countess of the Fountain, and with Luned as go-between marries her and takes up the defense of the fountain.

Owain returns with Arthur's court for a visit and forgets to come back for three years. A messenger strips the ring from his hand in the hall and publicly shames him. He goes mad in the wilderness, running naked with the beasts. A noblewoman finds him, applies the ointment of the Isle of Morven to his temples while he sleeps; he wakes healed. He rescues a lion from a serpent; the lion follows him as a companion. He defeats the persecutors of Luned, who has been condemned to burn. He kills a savage black giant who was enslaving the daughters of a displaced earl. He returns to his Countess and is reconciled. Welsh source of Chrétien de Troyes' *Yvain, or the Knight with the Lion*.

**Peredur Son of Evrawc**

Peredur is raised in the wilderness by his widowed mother, who hid him from combat so he would not die like his father and brothers. He sees Arthur's knights ride past and follows them to court on an emaciated horse. He is mocked by Kay. He kills the Red Knight with a sharpened stake thrown through his eye — the only target not armoured. He learns knightly skills from two uncles. At the first uncle's hall he sees the Grail procession: a bleeding lance, and a large silver salver bearing a man's severed head in a pool of blood. He does not ask the meaning, because his uncle advised him not to ask too many questions — and this failure causes grief to ripple across the land.

He makes a vow of silence until the lady Angharad Law Eurawc says she loves him. He kills the Addanc — a cavern monster — with a magic stone that makes him invisible (so he can see without being seen). He defeats the Empress's champions and lives with her for fourteen years. He returns to Arthur. He learns the Castle of Wonders and its processions held a mystery: his cousin's severed head was carried there by the Witches of Gloucester, who also lamed his first uncle and caused his cousin's death. He brings Arthur's army; the witches are destroyed. Welsh Percival — darker and stranger than the French *Perceval*, without a religious resolution.

**Geraint Son of Erbin**

At an annual hunt a strange knight and maiden accompany a dwarf who will not say who his master is. A maiden of Gwenhwyfar's retinue goes to ask; the dwarf strikes her. Geraint follows to avenge the insult, penniless; at an earl's dispossessed court he finds Enid and her ruined father, Earl Ynywl. The next morning is the Sparrow-Hawk tournament: the knight of the sparrow-hawk is Edeyrn son of Nudd, who humiliated Ynywl and took his lands. Geraint borrows Ynywl's damaged arms, wins the tournament, sends Edeyrn back to Arthur to make amends, restores the earl's lands, and takes Enid as wife.

He returns to his father's kingdom in Cornwall. He becomes absorbed in love, neglects tournaments and the defence of his domain. One morning he wakes and hears Enid quietly lamenting that the people blame her for his softness. He suspects infidelity. He takes her on a dangerous road journey and commands her not to speak. She disobeys his command again and again — warning him of ambushes ahead each time — and each time he punishes her for speaking while also being saved by the warning. He is apparently killed fighting giants; Enid screams; Earl Limours seizes her and strikes her; Geraint revives at her voice and kills the earl. A "Little King" helps him onward. They reach the enchanted game: a hedge of mist around a pavilion; all champions who enter are killed by the master inside. Geraint breaks the enchantment by defeating the master. They are reconciled. He governs wisely until death.

### Kilhwch and Olwen

The oldest tale in the collection — the most distinctly Celtic in voice and structure. Kilhwch's stepmother places a *tynged* (destiny/curse) on him: he will love no woman except Olwen, daughter of Yspaddaden Penkawr. Yspaddaden is a giant whose eyelids are so heavy they must be propped up with forks. He will die the day Olwen marries.

Kilhwch goes to Arthur's court and invokes the help of every hero by name — a list of 250 champions, each more improbable than the last, running to three solid pages. Arthur cannot find Olwen, but sends companions: Kai (who can hold his breath for nine days, who grows as tall as a tree in the rain, whose heat and cold never fail him), Bedwyr the one-handed, Gwrhyr who speaks all tongues, Gwalchmai who never refuses a quest, and others.

They find Custennin the shepherd, whose 23 sons Yspaddaden has already killed. Custennin's wife hides them in a chest; they meet Olwen herself in the courtyard — four white trefoils spring from the earth wherever she steps. She loves Kilhwch but says her father will kill any suitor; he must ask for her formally.

Three days of negotiation with Yspaddaden: each day he grants audience, throws a poisoned dart, and is hit by it thrown back. He gives an enormous list of impossible conditions — items needed for the wedding feast and to groom his own hair with comb, razor, and scissors that are carried between the ears of Twrch Trwyth, the great boar. The list is medieval literature's great comic catalogue of impossibilities: the leash of Cwrs Cant Ewin, the collar of Canhastyr Canllaw, the cauldron of Diwrnach Wyddel, the blood of the Black Witch, the harp of Teirtu, and dozens more, each dependent on the one before.

The quest unfolds in episodes:

*Gwrnach's sword:* Kai enters Gwrnach's hall claiming to be a sword-polisher. He polishes half the blade, praises the work, is given the sword to finish — and cuts off Gwrnach's head.

*The rescue of Mabon:* To hunt Twrch Trwyth they need Mabon son of Modron, but Mabon was taken from his mother three nights after birth and no one alive knows where he is. Gwrhyr must follow the chain of the oldest living creatures — each one knows a little more, each is older than the last, each sends the questioners on to the next. The Ousel of Cilgwri has been filing its beak on a smith's anvil since before this valley had trees; it does not know, but the Stag of Redynvre is older. The Stag has seen an oak grow from acorn to ancient ruin; it does not know, but the Owl of Cwm Cawlwd is older. The Owl has seen three forests grow and die in this valley; it does not know, but the Eagle of Gwern Abwy is older. The Eagle once struck a salmon in Llyn Llyw, thinking it the oldest thing; the salmon pulled the eagle down and only released it after the eagle removed fifty spears from its back. The Salmon of Llyn Llyw is the oldest. It swims each tide to the walls of Gloucester, and there it finds "such wrong as I never found elsewhere." Kai and Gwrhyr ride on the salmon's two shoulders to the prison wall. Mabon cries from inside the stone. By fighting will he be freed. Arthur storms the castle; Kai breaks through the dungeon wall and carries Mabon on his back.

*Ants rescue the flax-seed:* Gwythyr saves an ant-hill from fire; the ants bring nine bushels of flax-seed — every grain, except one brought by a lame pismire just before midnight.

*Dillus Varvawc:* Kai and Bedwyr spot Dillus roasting a wild boar on Plinlimmon. They wait until he falls asleep, then pluck his beard alive with wooden tweezers and kill him. The leash made from a living beard is necessary — a dead man's beard would be brittle. Arthur mocks Kai in a verse-englyn. Kai never forgives him; thenceforth he never comes to Arthur's aid in any trouble.

*The Cauldron of Diwrnach Wyddel:* Arthur sails to Ireland with a small force and demands the cauldron. Refused. Bedwyr seizes it. Llenlleawg Wyddel brandishes Caledvwlch (Excalibur). Diwrnach and his household are slain. Arthur returns with the cauldron full of Irish money.

*The Hunt of Twrch Trwyth:* Twrch Trwyth was once a king transformed into a boar for his sins. He has seven young pigs. His bristles are silver wire; he glitters through the forest. Gwrhyr, in bird-form, asks him to yield the comb and scissors. He refuses: "To take them he must take our lives." He and his seven cross from Ireland to Wales and begin devastating the country. Arthur hunts them across all Wales: battles in Cwm Kerwyn, Pelumyawc, Aberteivi — many of Arthur's best men slain, including Gwydre son of Arthur himself. The seven young pigs are killed one by one. Twrch Trwyth is cornered at the Severn estuary; men and horses and dogs seize him and plunge him under. Mabon snatches the razor; Kyledyr Wyllt takes the scissors. He regains his footing and flees to Cornwall. Kacmwri is dragged into the deep by two millstones; Osla Kyllellvawr drowns when his scabbard fills with water and drags him down. The comb is finally taken in Cornwall. Twrch Trwyth is driven into the sea, never seen again.

*The Blood of the Black Witch:* She lives in the cave at Pen Nant Govid on the confines of Hell. Two sets of warriors are driven out. Arthur himself goes to the cave door and cuts the witch in two with his dagger Carnwennan.

*Resolution:* Yspaddaden is shaved to the bone. His daughter is Kilhwch's. Goreu son of Custennin — the survivor of Yspaddaden's mass-killing of Custennin's sons — beheads the giant and hangs his head on a stake. The castle and treasures are taken. Kilhwch wins Olwen. Arthur's host disperses, each man to his own country.

### The Dream of Rhonabwy

Rhonabwy, sent to find a fugitive in Powys, shelters in a filthy hovel and falls asleep on a yellow calf-skin. He dreams of the glittering world of Arthur before the Battle of Badon. The tale is a sustained exercise in heraldic description: horses with parti-colored coats in impossible combinations, knights in armour of layered colors with scarves of specific hues, banners and helmets and clasps described in obsessive detail. The dream-world is more brilliant than the waking world can hold.

He meets Iddawc Cordd Prydain — "Iddawc the Churn of Britain." Iddawc was the messenger sent from Arthur to Medrawd before the Battle of Camlan to seek peace; instead of delivering Arthur's conciliatory words, he delivered the harshest insults he could devise. He caused Camlan. He spent seven years doing penance at Llech Las in North Britain; he was pardoned. He introduces Rhonabwy to Arthur's assembled host.

The dream's central action: Arthur and Owain play chess while Owain's Ravens and Arthur's men fight a battle in the field around them. Pages come three times to tell Owain his Ravens are being slaughtered; Arthur tells him "play." Pages come three times to tell Arthur his men are being killed; Owain tells him "play." Finally Arthur crushes the chessmen to dust. Owain orders Gwres son of Rheged to lower the banner. Peace is made. Greek tribute arrives — twenty-four asses with gold and silver. A truce is granted to Osla Gyllellvawr; the tribute goes to the bards. The host marches to Badon.

Rhonabwy wakes on the calf-skin, having slept three nights and three days. The tale closes: "And this is the reason that no one knows the dream without a book, neither bard nor gifted seer; because of the various colours that were upon the horses, and the many wondrous colours of the arms and of the panoply, and of the precious scarfs, and of the virtue-bearing stones."

### The Four Branches

**Pwyll Prince of Dyved (First Branch)**

Pwyll is hunting in Glyn Cuch when he steals prey from another man's hounds — brilliant white hounds with red ears, marking them as Otherworld animals. The owner is Arawn, King of Annwvyn (the Welsh Otherworld). They strike a bargain: Pwyll will live in Annwvyn for a year in Arawn's form, defeat Arawn's enemy Havgan at the annual ford-combat with one blow only (a second blow heals Havgan instantly), and govern Arawn's kingdom including sharing Arawn's bed without touching his wife. Arawn will govern Dyved as Pwyll.

Pwyll keeps every condition. He defeats Havgan with a single blow; when Havgan begs to be finished, Pwyll refuses — and Havgan dies from the one wound. He sleeps chastely beside Arawn's queen the whole year. Arawn, governing Dyved, is praised by Pwyll's nobles as the wisest and most generous lord they have ever known. When they meet again in the wood, each gives the other gifts of horses, hounds, and hawks; their friendship is confirmed. Pwyll is called "Pwyll Head of Annwvyn" from that time.

At the Gorsedd of Arberth — a mound where either wonders or blows are guaranteed to any who sit there — Pwyll sees a mysterious lady on a white horse, moving at a slow even pace. No horse can catch her no matter how fast it runs. On the third day Pwyll calls out: "For the sake of him you love most, stop!" She stops: she is Rhiannon, daughter of Heveydd Hên, and she has ridden to find Pwyll, to escape a marriage she does not want. She has loved him from afar. They agree to meet at her father's court in a year.

At the feast at Heveydd's court, Pwyll rashly promises "whatever you ask" to a young man in satin who turns out to be Gwawl son of Clud — the suitor Rhiannon has rejected, who now claims her and the feast as his prize. Rhiannon saves the situation: she gives Pwyll a magical bag that cannot be filled, and a plan. A year later Pwyll returns in rags, asks for a bagful of food, reveals the bag can never be filled until a man of wealth and rank steps in with both feet. Gwawl steps in. Pwyll upends the bag; Gwawl disappears inside it. Pwyll blows his horn; his hundred knights descend from the orchard. Each knight kicks and beats the bag. "What's in the bag?" "A Badger!" — the first time the game of Badger in the Bag was played.

Gwawl is released on pledges; he goes away nursing bruises. Pwyll marries Rhiannon.

Three years pass without an heir; the nobles pressure Pwyll to take another wife. He buys a year. A son is born; the six women watching the mother all fall asleep. When they wake, the child is gone. Fearing punishment, they smear blood from a dog-litter on Rhiannon's face while she sleeps, and tell the court she ate her child. Rhiannon accepts a penance rather than fight: she sits by the horse-block at the palace gate every day, tells the story of her supposed crime to all who will listen, and offers to carry guests on her back into the palace for seven years.

At the same time: Teirnyon Twryv Vliant watches his mare on May Day night, when every year the foal vanishes. He cuts off the claw of whatever creature reaches through the window; finds an infant boy in satin swaddling at the door. He and his wife raise him as Gwri Wallt Euryn — Golden Hair. The boy grows at three times normal speed. After years, Teirnyon recognizes Pwyll's face in the child; he returns him to Narberth. Rhiannon's penance ends. The boy is named Pryderi — "anxiety" — for the word Rhiannon cried when she received the news of his return. Pwyll dies; Pryderi inherits and expands his kingdom.

**Branwen Daughter of Llyr (Second Branch)**

Bran the Blessed (Bendigeid Vran), crowned king of all Britain, sits on the rocks of Harlech. Thirteen Irish ships approach; Matholwch, King of Ireland, comes to ask for Branwen as wife. She is one of the three chief ladies of the Island of the Mighty; no damsel in the world is fairer. The marriage is agreed.

Evnissyen — Bran's half-brother, defined at his first appearance as "the man who would cause strife between his two brothers when they were most at peace" — was not consulted. He finds Matholwch's horses and mutilates them all: lips cut to the teeth, ears to the head, tails to the back, eyelids to the bone. The horses are useless.

Matholwch prepares to leave in silence. Bran sends after him: he offers new horses for every ruined one; a silver staff as tall as Matholwch; a gold plate as wide as his face; and the Cauldron of Rebirth — a great cauldron from Ireland originally, brought to Britain by Llassar Llaesgyvnewid and his wife who escaped from an iron house that the Irish tried to burn them in. The cauldron's property: any slain warrior cast into it overnight will rise fully healed the next day, but will never speak again.

Matholwch accepts; the marriage is restored. Branwen goes to Ireland with him and is loved by all. She bears a son, Gwern. Two years later, Irish nobles shame Matholwch for accepting compensation after the insult instead of taking revenge. He demotes Branwen: she is sent to the kitchen, ordered to cook for the court, and given a box on the ear each day from the butcher. The sea is blockaded so that no word can reach Britain. This lasts three years.

Branwen raises a starling in the cover of the kneading trough. She teaches it to speak, to know what manner of man her brother is. She writes a letter, ties it to the wing-root. The starling finds Bran at Caer Seiont in Arvon and alights on his shoulder. He reads the letter. He summons the armies of all Britain.

Bran crosses the Irish Sea by wading it — he is too large for any ship. His army crosses the shallow water. The Irish see a great forest moving on the sea; a mountain moving beside it, with a lofty ridge and a lake on each side. Branwen, asked what this means: "The yards and masts of ships. My brother Bran — no ship can contain him. His eyes, one on each side of his nose, are the two lakes beside the ridge." Bran lies across the Linon river, which has a loadstone at the bottom blocking all ships; his warriors cross on his body as a bridge. "A chief should be a bridge for his people" — so the saying was first uttered, and is still used.

Matholwch offers terms: give the kingdom of Ireland to Gwern, Branwen's son. Bran accepts on Branwen's advice, to prevent further destruction. A house is built vast enough for Bran. But the Irish place 200 armed men in leather bags hung on the pillars, one man per bag. Evnissyen, entering first, goes through the hall feeling each bag. "What is in this bag?" "Meal, good soul." He finds the man's head inside, squeezes it through the bag until his fingers meet in the brain through the bone. He does this to all 200, singing an englyn over the last.

The feast proceeds; sovereignty is given to Gwern. Bran calls the boy to him; Manawyddan holds him; Nissyen holds him; the boy is beloved by all. Evnissyen calls for the child — "though he were not king of Ireland, yet willingly would I fondle the boy." "Cheerfully let him go to thee," says Bran. Evnissyen seizes Gwern by the feet and throws him headlong into the fire. Branwen lunges for the fire; Bran catches her with one arm, lifts his shield with the other.

Battle. The Irish use the Cauldron of Rebirth to restore their dead overnight; they rise able-bodied but silent. Evnissyen, watching, says in his heart: "Alas that I should be the cause of bringing the men of the Island of the Mighty into such a strait. I will find a deliverance." He hides among the Irish dead; two Irishmen throw him into the cauldron. He stretches himself until the cauldron splits into four pieces and his own heart bursts with it. The cauldron is destroyed; this turns the tide.

Seven men survive: Pryderi, Manawyddan, Gluneu, Taliesin, Ynawc, Grudyen, Heilyn. Bran is wounded in the foot by a poisoned dart. He commands: cut off my head. Carry it to the White Mount in London and bury it there, face toward France. You will be on the road a long time. Seven years feasting at Harlech; the Birds of Rhiannon will sing to you, and the head will be as pleasant company as when it was on my body. Then fourscore years at Gwales in Penvro; you may remain as long as the third door — the one facing Cornwall and Aber Henvelen — remains shut. Do not open it. When you open it, sorrow will return, and you must go to London to bury me.

Branwen is the eighth. They land at Aber Alaw in Talebolyon. She looks toward Ireland; she looks toward the Island of the Mighty. "Alas! Woe is me that I was ever born; two islands have been destroyed because of me." She utters a great groan; her heart breaks. They bury her by the Alaw in a four-sided grave.

The seven carry the head to Harlech. Seven years of enchanted feasting; the Birds of Rhiannon sing from over the sea. Then fourscore years at Gwales; the hall with two open doors and the third closed; no memory of grief, no awareness of passing time; the head as living company. At the end of fourscore years, Heilyn son of Gwynn says: "Evil betide me if I do not open that door." He opens it. Grief floods back — every loss felt fresh, as if it had just happened. They march to London and bury the head in the White Mount. "The third goodly concealment; and it was the third ill-fated disclosure when it was disinterred — for no invasion came to this island while the head was there."

In Ireland: only five pregnant women survived, in a cave in the wilderness. Their sons grew up, took each other's mothers as wives, and repopulated Ireland. Five sons became the five provinces. They found gold on the battlefields and grew wealthy.

**Manawyddan Son of Llyr (Third Branch)**

The seven have buried the head. Manawyddan stands in London with nothing; his cousin Caswallawn has conquered Britain by invisible sword. Pryderi offers him Dyved and Rhiannon. Manawyddan marries Rhiannon. The four friends — Pryderi, Kicva, Manawyddan, Rhiannon — feast and hunt. Their friendship is complete; they are never separated day or night.

At the Gorsedd of Arberth: a peal of thunder, a fall of mist so thick no one can see another. When it lifts, the province of Dyved is empty. Every human, every animal, every building — gone. The four alone remain, surrounded by empty stone halls and wild deer. They live for two years on hunting and wild honey. Then they go to England.

In Hereford they become saddle-makers; Manawyddan paints the housings with blue enamel so fine that no saddler in town can compete. The craftsmen plot to kill them. "Let us go to another town," says Manawyddan, against Pryderi's will to fight. In the next town they make shields; same result. In the third town they make shoes with gilded clasps; same result. Each time Manawyddan refuses confrontation and suggests departure.

They return to Dyved. Manawyddan plants three wheat crofts; they thrive. The first night at harvest — the crop is stripped overnight, down to bare straw, every ear taken. The second crop the same. The third night he watches: an army of mice, more than can be counted, each one climbing a stalk and cutting off an ear and carrying it away. He chases them; he cannot catch any — except one, sluggish and fat. He puts it in his glove, ties it shut.

He takes the mouse to the Gorsedd of Narberth to hang it — giving it the full dignity of a criminal's execution. A scholar approaches: offers him a pound to release it. He refuses. A priest: offers three pounds. He refuses. A bishop with a full retinue of sumpter-horses arrives and offers seven pounds, then four-and-twenty, then all the horses and all the baggage. Each time: refused. "What then would you have?" "That Pryderi and Rhiannon be free." Done. "And the enchantment removed from Dyved." Done. "And never laid again." Done. "And no revenge taken on any of us." Done. "Now — tell me who the mouse is." "She is my wife." "And why did she come to rob me?" "To despoil you. I am Llwyd son of Kilcoed; I enchanted Dyved to avenge Gwawl son of Clud, for the game of Badger in the Bag that Pwyll played upon him in the court of Heveydd Hên."

Manawyddan sets the mouse free. Llwyd strikes her with a wand; she becomes a young woman, the fairest ever seen. The land around them fills with people and cattle. Pryderi had been held in Llwyd's palace with the knockers of the gate hung around his neck; Rhiannon had carried the collars of the asses used to haul hay. "And such had been their bondage."

**Math Son of Mathonwy (Fourth Branch)**

Math son of Mathonwy rules Gwynedd. He cannot exist unless his feet rest in a maiden's lap — except in war. His foot-holder is Goewin, the fairest maiden of her time. His nephew Gilvaethwy is so consumed with love for her that his colour and spirit visibly fail. His brother Gwydion — the greatest enchanter in the world and the best teller of tales — sees this. He schemes.

Gwydion tells Math that Pryderi's court in the south has a new kind of animal — pigs, sent from Annwvyn by Arawn. He will obtain them. He and eleven men go to Pryderi's court in the guise of bards. Gwydion tells stories so well that Pryderi is enchanted by him. In the morning Gwydion offers an exchange: twelve gilded horses, twelve greyhounds with gilded leashes, twelve shields — all made from fungus by his magic. He knows the illusion will only last one day. They flee north with the pigs. The illusion dissolves; Pryderi raises twenty-one cantrefs and marches after them. While Math is away at war, Gilvaethwy violates Goewin in Math's own chamber. Math wins the war; Gwydion kills Pryderi in single combat by magic force (Pryderi's grave is at Maen Tyriawc above Melenryd). The men of the South go home in grief.

Math returns to Goewin's report. He marries her immediately to restore her honor. He waits for Gwydion and Gilvaethwy to come back, then transforms them with his magic wand: into a pair of deer (year one — they return with a fawn, Hydwn); into a pair of wild hogs (year two — they return with a piglet, Hychdwn); into a pair of wolves (year three — they return with a cub, Bleiddwn). Three sons, each taken and baptized by Math. "The three sons of Gilvaethwy the false, the three faithful combatants, Bleiddwn, Hydwn, and Hychdwn the Tall." Then Math restores them: "For the wrong that ye did unto me, sufficient has been your punishment and your dishonour."

He needs a new foot-holder. Gwydion suggests his own sister Arianrod. Math tests her virginity with his wand: she steps over it and births two sons at once. Dylan leaps immediately into the sea, takes its nature, swims as well as the best fish — called Dylan the Son of the Wave. He is later killed by his uncle Govannon: "the third fatal blow." The second form Gwydion snatches and hides in a chest. He grows at double speed: one year passes, he seems two; four years later, he seems eight.

Gwydion takes him to Arianrod. She will not name him: "*Tynged*: he shall have no name unless he receives it from me." Gwydion transforms himself and the boy into cobblers on a boat offshore; Arianrod comes to be fitted for shoes; the boy shoots a wren precisely between sinew and bone. Arianrod, startled, exclaims: "With a steady hand did the lion aim at it!" So naming him: Llew Llaw Gyffes — the Lion with the Steady Hand. Second *tynged*: no arms unless Arianrod gives them. Gwydion conjures a phantom fleet besieging her castle; in the panic she arms Llew herself. Third *tynged*: no wife of the race of earth.

Math and Gwydion make Blodeuwedd — Flower-Face — from oak blossom, broom blossom, and meadow-sweet. She is given to Llew; he rules Ardudwy in prosperity.

One day, Gronw Pebyr the hunter passes with his dogs. Blodeuwedd hears his horn and sees him cross the yard. They fall in love at first sight. He stays three nights; they plan Llew's death. She pretends anxiety for Llew's safety, asks how he might come to harm. He cannot be killed inside or outside, on horse or on foot; only by a spear made solely on Sunday over the course of a year, while he stands with one foot on a bath's edge and the other on a goat's back. Gronw spends a year making the spear. Blodeuwedd prepares the trap and the bath. Llew takes the position — "to show her how it would look" — and Gronw flings the spear from the hillside. It pierces him; he flies away as an eagle.

Gwydion follows a sow who eats putrid flesh fallen from an eagle in an oak tree. He sings three englynion — one verse for each branch of the tree — to call the eagle down step by step: *Oak that grows between the two banks — darkened is the sky and hill — shall I not tell him by his wounds, that this is Llew?* The eagle descends to Gwydion's knee. He strikes it with his wand; Llew returns to his own form — nothing but skin and bone. He is healed over months by the physicians of Gwynedd.

Blodeuwedd flees with her maidens across the mountain. Walking backward in terror, they fall into a lake and drown — all except Blodeuwedd herself, whom Gwydion catches. "I will not slay thee, but I will do unto thee worse than that. I will turn thee into a bird, and because of the shame thou hast done unto Llew, thou shalt never show thy face in the light of day, and all the other birds shall harry thee wherever they find thee. And thou shalt not lose thy name." Blodeuwedd is the Welsh word for owl. The owl is hated by all birds for this reason to this day.

Gronw asks to meet Llew for the return blow, and asks only that he may place a stone slab between himself and the dart. Llew agrees. He throws; the dart pierces the slab and passes through Gronw's back. Gronw is slain. The stone — Llech Gronw, with the hole through it — still stands on the bank of the Cynvael river in Ardudwy. Llew rules Gwynedd prosperously thereafter.

### The Dream of Maxen Wledig

Maxen, Emperor of Rome, falls asleep hunting on a warm afternoon while his companions shield him from the sun. He dreams of a journey westward up a river to the highest mountain in the world, then down to a great sea, a fleet, one ship — he crosses to the fairest island in the world, traverses it, comes to a castle at the mouth of a river. Inside the castle: two auburn-haired youths playing chess on a silver board with golden pieces; a hoary-headed man in an ivory chair carving chessmen; and a maiden on a chair of ruddy gold. She is more beautiful than the sun. She rises; he puts his arms around her neck. The dogs bark; the spears clash; he wakes.

He cannot eat or speak for a week. His page — who is king of the Romans — brings the wise men: "Send messengers for three years." Messengers search the world; they find nothing. The page suggests Maxen return to the spot where he slept and follow the dream-route. Thirteen messengers travel west across the sea to the Island of Britain, to Snowdon, to Arvon, to Aber Sain (Caernarvon) at the river mouth. They find the hall. They find the two youths at chess. They find the old man. They find the maiden — Helen, daughter of Eudav — in a chair of ruddy gold, exactly as in the dream. "If the emperor loves me, let him come here to seek me."

Maxen invades Britain, conquers it from Beli's sons, finds Helen. She asks as her bridal gift: Britain for her father, plus three chief castles at Caernarvon, Caerleon, and Carmarthen; earth from Rome brought for the highest castle so it will be healthy for the emperor to walk on. Roads are built connecting the castles — they are called the Roads of Helen Luyddawc because she was of British blood and the men of Britain would have built them for no one else.

Seven years pass. Rome declares a new emperor and sends an ultimatum. Maxen marches back through France; besieges Rome for a year without progress. Helen's brothers Kynan and Adeon arrive with a small British force; they measure the walls by night, build ladders for every four men, attack at midday while both emperors are at dinner. Rome falls in a morning; the rival emperor is slain. Maxen retakes his throne and gives Kynan and Adeon an army for any conquest they choose. They conquer through Europe; kill all the men; keep the women. Eventually Kynan cuts out all the women's tongues so they cannot corrupt his men's language. The men of Armorica (Brittany) are thus "Britons" — from a people who silenced their women to preserve their speech.

### Lludd and Llevelys

Lludd son of Beli is King of Britain; his beloved brother Llevelys is King of France. Britain is afflicted by three plagues: (1) The Coranians — a people so sensitive that they can hear any word carried on the wind, making them impossible to deceive or attack; (2) A shriek on every May Eve across every hearth — men and women lose strength and sense, animals lose vigor, the earth and water are left barren; (3) A giant who comes by night and, through sleep-enchantment, carries off all provisions — no matter how much is prepared, nothing survives to morning.

Lludd sails secretly to France; he and Llevelys meet alone at sea. They speak through a brass horn. A demon in the horn perverts their words into hostility; wine washes it out. Llevelys gives him: (1) An insect to bruise in water and cast over a mixed assembly — it kills the Coranians without harming Britons; (2) Measure the island; its center is Oxford. Dig a pit there; fill a cauldron with the best mead and cover it with satin. Watch through the night — the two fighting dragons (one British, one foreign) that cause the May-eve shriek will fall upon the satin, drink the mead, and sleep. Bury them in a stone kistvaen at the strongest place in Britain — they are buried at Dinas Emrys (later Dinas Emreis) in Snowdon; no plague came to Britain while they remained there; (3) Stay armed, keep a cauldron of cold water nearby, and when sleep-enchantment comes, plunge yourself into the cold water. The giant will come in heavy armour carrying a hamper. When he puts the food in the hamper, challenge him; he will fight; he will lose; he will offer to be your faithful vassal.

Lludd executes all three remedies exactly. Britain is freed. He rules prosperously until the end of his life.

### Taliesin

Caridwen, wife of Tegid Voel on Lake Tegid, has an ugly son Avagddu — "so ill-favoured that she feared no one of noble birth would have aught to do with him." She resolves to give him what beauty cannot give: all knowledge. She brews the Cauldron of Inspiration and Science for a year and a day; three drops of the liquor will carry all wisdom. Gwion Bach, a boy from Powys, stirs the cauldron; a blind man Morda tends the fire. Near the end of the year, three drops fly out and fall on Gwion's finger. He licks them instinctively. He receives all knowledge — including the knowledge that Caridwen will kill him.

He flees. She pursues. A great shape-shifting chase: Gwion becomes a hare; she becomes a greyhound. He becomes a fish; she becomes an otter. He becomes a bird; she becomes a hawk. He drops onto a barn floor and becomes a grain of wheat among thousands. She becomes a black hen and finds him out and swallows him. She bears him nine months. When he is born he is so beautiful she cannot kill him; she wraps him in a leather bag and casts him into the sea on April 29.

Elphin, unlucky son of the wealthy Gwyddno Garanhir, is given the weir to draw for one year — his father's gift to him, hoping luck will come. The weir holds no fish; he finds only the leather bag. He opens it: "Behold a radiant brow!" He names the baby Taliesin. He carries the infant home on horseback as gently as if in the easiest chair in the world. Taliesin immediately sings a consolation verse:

*"Fair Elphin, cease to lament! / Weak and small as I am, / On the foaming beach of the ocean, / In the day of trouble I shall be / Of more service to thee than three hundred salmon."*

Elphin prospers. When Taliesin is thirteen, Elphin goes to the Christmas court of Maelgwn Gwynedd and boasts — fatally — that his wife is more virtuous than the queen, and his bard wiser than all the king's bards. He is imprisoned. Maelgwn sends his libertine son Rhun to dishonor Elphin's wife; Taliesin disguises a kitchen-maid as his mistress, loads her hands with rings. Rhun drugs her and cuts off her finger with the signet ring. Elphin, shown the evidence, refutes it in three observations: the ring would never have fit his wife's hand; his wife always pares her nails before Saturday; this finger kneaded rye dough within three days. Elphin is re-imprisoned.

Taliesin goes to Maelgwn's court and crouches in a corner near the bards. As the twenty-four official bards march past him on their procession to the king's table, he makes faces at them — playing "Blerwm, blerwm" with his lip and finger. Each bard immediately begins doing the same thing before the king, babbling and mouthing nonsense. The king is enraged; a squire hits the chief bard Heinin Vardd with a broom. Heinin explains: "There is a spirit in the corner there in the form of a child." Taliesin is brought forward and asked to account for himself. He sings his origin-poem:

*"I was with my Lord in the highest sphere on the fall of Lucifer into the depth of hell; I have borne a banner before Alexander; I was in Canaan when Absalom was slain; I was at the place of the crucifixion of the merciful Son of God; I have been three periods in the prison of Arianrod; I was in Asia with Noah in the ark — I was originally little Gwion, and at length I am Taliesin."*

He calls down a windstorm that shakes the castle. Maelgwn releases Elphin. Taliesin bids Elphin wager the king that his horse is swifter. He gives the jockey twenty-four burnt holly-twigs and tells him to strike each of the king's horses with one as he overtakes it, then to drop his cap when his own horse stumbles. At the spot marked by the cap, they dig: a cauldron full of gold. "Behold a payment for having taken me from the weir and reared me from that time until now."

## Pass 2 — Characters and Pivotal Choices

**Rhiannon** is a woman who arrived on a horse no one could catch and stopped only when directly asked. She chose Pwyll herself, engineered her own rescue from Gwawl, and when her son vanished and the women accused her of eating him, she did not fight the accusation. She accepted penance instead: seven years sitting by a horse-block, carrying guests on her back, telling the story of her supposed crime to every stranger who would hear it. She holds herself open to witnessing rather than to protest. The enchantment later gives her a second humiliation — ass-collars. Both times she is freed not by her own power but by someone finally doing the right thing. She is the great image of grace under prolonged injustice maintained not as martyrdom but as patient dignity.

**Branwen** is called "the fairest damsel in the world" and positioned as the cause of two islands' destruction — and her final speech accepts this causality without deflection. She did not mutilate the horses (Evnissyen did), did not choose the marriage (Bran arranged it), did not enrage the Irish nobles (Evnissyen's original insult did). She is the site of the conflict, not its agent. What she does under degradation: she raises a starling. She teaches it to speak and to know her brother's nature. She writes a letter and ties it to the bird's wing. An act of small persistent care in the face of humiliation — and it sets in motion the destruction of both islands. Landing at Aber Alaw she looks in both directions and does not blame the one man who actually caused the catastrophe. She takes the whole weight of it and her heart breaks.

**Evnissyen** is the machine of catastrophe — defined as a quarreler, deployed as an unstoppable force of destruction. He mutilates horses because no one consulted him. He kills Gwern because no one thought to watch him. He is not blind to his effect; when he sees the cauldron restoring Irish dead while the British pile up as corpses, he says in his heart: "Alas that I should be the cause of this." He hides among the Irish dead, is thrown into the cauldron, and stretches himself from inside until it shatters — and his own heart bursts. One act of genuine sacrifice in a life of spectacular harm. The tale does not redeem him but gives him this one moment of deliberate death as atonement.

**Manawyddan** is the most purely patient character in the collection. After Bran's death he has no kingdom and makes no complaint. When Dyved is emptied by enchantment he does not rage; he hunts and goes to England and makes saddles, shields, shoes — always a craftsman so good that the established trade threatens him. Each time, against Pryderi's desire to fight, he says: "Let us go to another town." When the mice strip his corn he catches one and prepares to hang it with the full solemnity of judicial execution. The scholar, priest, bishop, horses, gold — he refuses them all. He is not waiting for a price; he is waiting for the truth. He is the opposite of Evnissyen: a man of extraordinary restraint who wins everything without violence by outlasting the enchanter's patience.

**Gwydion** is the best storyteller in the world and the most accomplished cheat. He gets Pryderi's sacred pigs by manufactured illusion; his scheme triggers a war and the death of Pryderi by magic. He facilitates his brother's violation of Goewin. He is transformed into animals for three years as penance. After restoration, he is the man who spends weeks following a sow through the valleys of Gwynedd to find his nephew rotting in an eagle's form in the top of an oak tree. Who sings three careful poems — one verse per branch — to call the eagle down to his knee. He is a villain and a devoted surrogate father in the same body.

**Llew Llaw Gyffes** is a man who was never supposed to exist. His existence is contested from birth; his mother's three *tyndgedau* leave him nameless, unarmed, wifeless — each curse broken through trickery rather than confrontation. His wife is made of flowers. His death is geometrically impossible: neither inside nor outside, neither mounted nor on foot, struck only by a spear built on Sundays over a year. He becomes a wounded eagle in an oak tree, dropping rot. He is restored by song — brought down branch by branch. He kills Gronw through a stone slab. He rules Gwynedd prosperously. He is a figure of absolute survival: every attempted erasure fails.

**Caridwen** pursues the grain of wheat she swallows to the bottom of every transformation. She will not let the theft go. When she births Gwion Bach reborn, she cannot kill him because of his beauty — so she casts him into the sea, delegating the killing to chance and the ocean. He survives on those terms too. She is the only figure in the collection who loses absolutely and is not consoled.

**Taliesin** knows everything — because the knowledge was taken accidentally and by an act of reflex. Three drops on a finger, a lick from heat, and suddenly the whole shape of the world is available to him. He was never meant to receive it. Caridwen was brewing the cauldron for her ugly son. Taliesin's entire identity rests on an accident that he had no right to. He is reborn through the sea and a fisherman's weir. He silences twenty-four court bards by making faces. His origin-poem claims presence at every major event in sacred and secular history — as if the accidental gift organized itself into the mind of the world's best witness. He is the oldest voice that remembers, made from a child who licked something hot without thinking.

## Pass 3 — The Salmon of Llyn Llyw (Elaborated Scene)

The Eagle of Gwern Abwy has told them: once he struck a salmon in Llyn Llyw, thinking it the oldest creature alive. The salmon dragged him underwater. They made peace. The eagle removed fifty spears from the salmon's back; the salmon sent word that it would guide them. So they go together to the river.

Kai and Gwrhyr Gwalstawt Ieithoedd — the man who speaks all tongues — climb onto the salmon's two shoulders. The salmon is large enough to carry them both without strain. No word is exchanged. It does not speak to them; it simply turns.

It swims against the current. This is what it always does: it goes upriver, against the water's direction, following a pull that is older than memory. It has been making this journey since before the valley around it had the trees it has now. It does not explain itself to Kai and Gwrhyr. There is nothing to explain. The wrong at the end of the journey is simply the thing it keeps returning to, the way a tongue keeps finding a missing tooth.

The walls of Gloucester appear. The city's stone sits heavy on both banks; the river narrows here and smells of the sea even this far inland. This is where the salmon always comes. It does not dock or stop; it circles near the wall, as it circles each tide, as close as the current allows. The wrong it cannot stop returning to is contained inside those stones.

Then they hear it. From below the wall — from beneath, from inside the earth itself — a voice lamenting. Gwrhyr leans from the salmon's back and calls through the stone:

*"Who is it that laments in this house of stone?"*

A pause. Then:

*"Alas, there is reason enough for whoever is here to lament. It is Mabon the son of Modron who is here imprisoned; and no imprisonment was ever so grievous as mine, neither that of Lludd Llaw Ereint, nor that of Greid the son of Eri."*

The prisoner names his suffering against the scale of the world's grief. He has been there long enough to have learned what the greatest imprisonments in legend were — and to judge his own worse. He has been measuring himself against mythological suffering for an unreckonable time.

*"Hast thou hope of being released — for gold, or for silver, or for any gifts of wealth, or through battle and fighting?"*

*"By fighting will whatever I may gain be obtained."*

He does not ask who they are. He does not ask how they found him. He only names the terms of his release, as if he has been asked before, across centuries, by creatures who came and could not act on the answer.

Kai and Gwrhyr return to Arthur. Arthur raises the warriors of all the islands. They march to Gloucester. While the whole army storms the castle walls on every side, Kai and Bedwyr ride again on the salmon's shoulders — carried silently through the water to the base of the prison wall. Kai breaks through the stone. He lifts Mabon from the dungeon floor onto his back and carries him out into the running water while the battle rages above them on every side.

The salmon is still there. It holds its place against the current. It waits, as it has always waited. When the prisoner passes over it — carried on Kai's back, no longer inside the stone — the wrong it has been returning to for longer than the trees can count is, for the first time, not there.

The text does not say what the salmon does then. The story moves on to Arthur at Gelli Wic. But the salmon of Llyn Llyw was the last link in a chain that began with a tiny bird filing its beak on an iron anvil in an otherwise empty valley — and the chain held. Each creature was only old enough to send the question on. None of them had the answer. The answer was at the bottom, inside the stone, and it had to be reached in the right order, through every creature that had ever been close enough to the wrong to feel it without being able to fix it.

The Asking was the action. The chain of witnesses was the key.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

**1. The Relay of Ancient Witnesses**
*(Kilhwch and Olwen — Mabon chain)*
A prisoner has been held somewhere since before living memory, taken three nights after birth. No one alive knows which city. A chain of the oldest creatures — ousel, stag, owl, eagle, salmon — each one directs to the next, each one older. The Fighter carries the question from creature to creature. At the end: a prison beneath a city with Roman walls, and a voice inside the stone. The oldest creature carries the Fighter there on its back.

**2. The Badger in the Bag**
*(Pwyll / Manawyddan)*
A rival has been promised something by accident. An enchanted bag is the only leverage available. The Fighter enters a feast in rags, asks for a small thing, watches a trap spring. The rival is inside the bag. What conditions do you name before releasing him? This works as a pure negotiation quest: the Fighter has all the leverage, but must identify every condition or the enchanter finds a loophole.

**3. The Enchanted Province**
*(Manawyddan — Third Branch)*
The Fighter arrives in a region where everything is gone: no people, no animals, no crops, no smoke. The province has been emptied by an enchanter pursuing an old grievance — probably a grievance that has nothing to do with the Fighter. A sluggish creature is the only thing catchable. The quest: negotiate the restoration of the land condition by condition, without relinquishing the one thing that gives you leverage, until every person is returned.

**4. Carrying the Head**
*(Branwen — Second Branch)*
Seven survivors carry a dead king's head from one island to another. They are forbidden to open a particular door for fourscore years. The enchanted hall keeps all grief at bay; they are company to each other and to the head; time does not pass. One of them opens the door. Everything floods back. The quest: finish the journey to London with the head before the grief dissolves the group entirely.

**5. The Maker of Wives from Flowers**
*(Math — Fourth Branch)*
A man is forbidden any wife of the race of the earth. Two magicians make him a woman from oak blossom, broom, and meadow-sweet. She falls in love with someone else the first night a stranger passes by her door. She learns her husband's paradoxical death-conditions. The Fighter is caught between the conspiracy and the husband's household — and must decide which loyalty holds.

**6. The Animal Punishment Years**
*(Math — Fourth Branch)*
Two men who did a great wrong are transformed into animal pairs and forced to live as the animals whose form they bear, producing a child each year who is raised as human by the man who punished them. The quest takes place in the punishment years: the Fighter (or a witness to the Fighter) must carry a message that only the transformed men know, to someone who does not know who they really are. The token is the secret: proof of identity that cannot be declared openly.

**7. The Blerwm Silencing**
*(Taliesin)*
A young bard enters a tyrant king's court and makes faces at the official bards as they pass — each one immediately begins babbling nonsense before the king. The Fighter must smuggle the bard in past the court guards, hold the position long enough for the song to work, and get the imprisoned patron freed before the king understands what is happening. The bard can sing anything and survive anything — but only if he is not taken before the song ends.

## 5-Act Vignette Play — "The River That Knows Its Wrong"

### The Token: The Asking-Rod of Gwrhyr

A short rod of carved whalebone, marked at one end with an ogham inscription: *"I bring the question of the one who cannot be forgotten."* The other end is blank at the start. Each ancient creature that answers marks it — a bill-groove, a claw-score, an antler-scratch, a scale-press. The rod is both credential and record. Without the marks, the question carries no authority. With all marks present, the door at the bottom of the chain opens — not by force, but because the right question was asked in the right order, with the right witnesses behind it.

**TOKEN DOCTRINE:**
- **ACCEPT** — Act 1: Gwrhyr gives the Fighter the rod
- **CARRY** — Acts 2, 3, 4: each creature marks it and directs onward
- **DELIVER** — Act 5: the rod is presented at the prison wall; the door yields to the complete chain
- **RECORD** — the rod is returned to Gwrhyr; each witness-mark on the rod becomes a letter of the prisoner's name in the language of the creature that answered

---

### Act 1 — The Man Who Speaks All Tongues

**Location: CWT (Cwm Wyllt — a Welsh river valley; shale and oak; mist in the mornings; one old stone bridge; no people)**

Gwrhyr Gwalstawt Ieithoedd lies on a pallet in a stone shepherd's shelter, his thigh wrapped in linen that needs changing again. The wound is from the Twrch Trwyth hunt — a tusk-strike that healed wrong and has never fully closed. He has the languages: bird, fish, beast, the old tongues before Latin. He cannot walk the chain himself anymore.

There is another prisoner. Not Mabon — Mabon was found and freed. This one was taken even younger, from a different place, in a different age. The chain of ancient witnesses must be followed again in sequence. Without the sequence the creatures will not answer; they have been asked too many wrong questions by too many wrong people.

He gives the Fighter the rod. "Present it to each creature. Ask only two things: *What wrong have you witnessed that you cannot stop returning to?* And then: *Who is older than you?* Do not offer the rod to any creature that does not first reach toward it — only those old enough to carry witness will recognize it."

*Mission bit granted: `rod_gwrhyr_asking`*

**onPass:** Fighter accepts. Gwrhyr gives the rod.
**onFail:** Fighter declines. "I will find someone else," says Gwrhyr. Quest closes.

---

### Act 2 — The Ousel's Rust

**Location: CWT — granite ridge above the valley; a small cold stream; a single iron anvil half-submerged in the shallows**

The Ousel of Cilgwri perches on the anvil. When Gwrhyr first came, this anvil was the size of a man's thigh; the ousel's bill has been working it down for longer than the valley has had oak trees. It is now the size of a nut.

The Fighter presents the rod. The ousel pauses, then touches it with its bill — leaving a small groove, as if it has been filing.

"I have seen many things and witnessed many wrongs. But the wrong I cannot stop returning to is this: that a child was taken before they had a name, from a mother who had held them three nights only, and no one came to ask after them for longer than we birds reckon time. I am not old enough to know where this child is. Go to the Stag of Redynvre. He is older than I am."

*Mission bit check: `rod_gwrhyr_asking`*

**onPass:** Ousel marks the rod with a bill-groove. Directs to the Stag.
**onFail (rod not presented):** Ousel is silent. Flies away without a word.

---

### Act 3 — The Stag at the Dead Oak

**Location: RDV (Redynvre — a hillside where one great oak stands among stumps; the forest that was here has grown, aged, and fallen twice over; the stag stands in the old clearing)**

The Stag of Redynvre has watched this hillside through two full cycles of forest — from bare stone to oak to decay to oak again. The stumps around him are from the first forest; he was here before they grew.

The Fighter presents the rod. The stag lowers its head slowly and presses one antler-tip against it, leaving a pale scratch along the grain.

"I know of the wrong the ousel named. I have been near it longer than she has. The child was carried west along a river, not south. But I am not old enough to know which river, or which city. Go to the Owl of Cwm Cawlwd. She is older."

*Mission bit check: `rod_gwrhyr_asking`*

**onPass:** Stag marks the rod with an antler-scratch. Confirms western direction. Directs to the Owl.
**onFail:** Stag watches. Does not speak. Does not move.

---

### Act 4 — The Owl in Three Forests

**Location: CWL (Cwm Cawlwd — a steep-sided valley; three generations of forest visible in the geology; the owl perches on a dead elm at the valley's crook)**

The Owl of Cwm Cawlwd has watched three separate forests grow and collapse in this valley. She was here when it was bare rock. She was here when the first pines came. She was here for the oak that replaced them, and for the bare rock again after the clearing, and for the oak that came back after that. She does not count years; she counts forests.

The Fighter presents the rod. The owl grips it briefly with one talon, pressing hard enough to leave a claw-score across two of the existing marks.

"I know the river. It is a tidal river — it moves like the sea even far inland, and the city above it was built with Roman stone. There is a prison beneath that city. I have heard the salmon speak of the wrong there. The salmon is older than I am. Go to the Eagle of Gwern Abwy first — the eagle once struck that salmon and made peace with it. The eagle will call the salmon up."

She turns her face away then, which is how owls indicate a conversation is finished.

*Mission bit check: `rod_gwrhyr_asking`*

**onPass:** Owl marks the rod with a claw-score. Names the tidal river-city. Directs to Eagle then Salmon.
**onFail (rod has fewer than two marks):** Owl turns away immediately. "The rod has not enough witness. Return when the stag has spoken."

---

### Act 5 — The Salmon Carries You There

**Location: TWY (Tidal river approach — Roman-walled city; the river smells of the sea even in winter; the stones of the wall go down below the waterline)**

The Eagle of Gwern Abwy is on its crag above the river bend. It sees the rod; it calls once, low, down toward the water. A disturbance in the current below: the salmon of Llyn Llyw surfaces.

The salmon is enormous — longer than a currach, grey-silver, ancient. It does not speak. It circles once near the bank. The Fighter holds out the rod over the water. The salmon comes close, barely touching its snout to the wood — a faint silver scale-press, the lightest of the four marks, barely visible unless the light is right. Then it turns upriver.

The Fighter rides on its back, or on a coracle it tows, in silence. It moves against the current with no visible effort. It has done this before; it will do this again. The city walls appear on both banks; the water narrows; the stone smells of rot and old iron.

They hear the voice before they see the wall.

The Fighter holds the rod up to the stone and asks the question in full — Gwrhyr's question, carried through the whole chain: *"What wrong have you witnessed that you cannot stop returning to?"*

From inside the stone: *"That I was taken before I had a name, and that no one came until now."*

The rod is pressed against the wall. The inscription — *I bring the question of the one who cannot be forgotten* — warms in the Fighter's hand. The four marks on the rod glow faint in sequence: bill-groove, antler-scratch, claw-score, scale-press. The door in the wall opens.

Not by force. By sequence. By the chain having held.

The prisoner emerges. They have no name yet; they have been there since before names were given to them. The rod is returned to Gwrhyr. On the blank end, Gwrhyr reads the four marks together as letters in four different animal-languages; he writes out the prisoner's name for the first time. The rod is placed in the shepherd's shelter where Gwrhyr sleeps, set beside a stone, and left as a record.

*Mission bit taken: `rod_gwrhyr_asking`*
*Mission bit granted: `prisoner_named`*

**onPass:** Prisoner freed; rod returned; name written for the first time.
**onFail (fewer than 4 marks on rod):** The door does not open. The inscription does not warm. The voice in the stone says: "The chain is not complete. Someone in the relay did not answer."

## Cycle 2 — Angle: Carrying the Head (Branwen)

**What this angle reveals that Cycle 1 missed:** Cycle 1 (The River That Knows Its Wrong) showed the Mabinogion as a world of ancient chain-witnesses — the relay of the oldest creatures, the credential-rod that accumulates marks, the question that requires the right order. Cycle 2 shows the book's darkest corner: the Second Branch and its irreversible destruction. Cycle 1 missed entirely the war with Ireland, Branwen's death, the seven survivors, the living head, the enchanted hall at Gwales, and the structural paradox that deferred grief is not healed grief. The flood of feeling when Heilyn opens the forbidden door is the Mabinogion's most honest moment: eighty years of protected peace ending in a single second because a man looked at the door that faces Cornwall and could not help but want to know.

---

### Pass 1 — Full Telling (Branwen / Carrying the Head Angle)

Bendigeidfran — Bran the Blessed — is the giant king of Britain whose head becomes, in death, more powerful than he ever was in life. He is too large to enter any house; he must camp outside. He is the host who gives everyone too much: the magical cauldron of rebirth to Matholwch, his sister Branwen to the Irish king as wife, the peace he tries to hold together after Efnisien's mutilation of the horses nearly breaks it. He is generous and he is too late. Everything he gives is used to make the catastrophe worse.

Branwen's story is the Second Branch's wound. Married to Matholwch of Ireland with pomp and alliance, she is first honored and then punished for the insult of Efnisien's act — forced into kitchen servitude, struck daily, denied any communication with Britain. She trains a starling to carry a letter to Bran. The letter reaches him. He raises Britain's army and wades through the Irish Sea, his ships sailing in his wake, because he is large enough to do this. The war that follows destroys both islands.

The Irish cauldron of rebirth — Bran's own gift to Matholwch — reanimates Irish dead as silent warriors; they cannot be killed conventionally and they cannot speak. Efnisien, who caused the war with his cruelty, destroys the cauldron by hiding among the Irish dead and bursting it from inside. He dies killing it. This is the Second Branch's structural center: the man who made the catastrophe undoes it at the cost of his life, which is not redemption but it is completion.

Seven Britons survive. Bran is mortally wounded by a poisoned spear in the foot — the wound that cannot heal but will not kill quickly. He commands his seven survivors to cut off his head. His head remains living — wise, joyful, the same Bran in all ways except with no body. He commands them to take the head to London and bury it on the White Hill (Gwynvryn) facing France, as a protective talisman for Britain.

The seven survivors travel with the head. At Harlech they feast for seven years while Rhiannon's birds sing over the sea — birds whose song can wake the dead from sleep and send the living into the deepest rest; the seven feast in their magic, in their grief held at bay. Then to Gwales in Penfro — Grassholm Island, off the Pembrokeshire coast — where they enter an enchanted hall. Three doors. Two may be opened. The third faces Cornwall and must never be opened, on pain of losing the enchantment.

Fourscore years. Eighty years in the hall with Bran's speaking, feasting head. They do not age. They do not grieve. They do not remember Branwen, or the war, or Ireland, or any of the destruction. The hall holds them in a kind of mercy that is also a kind of theft: it steals their grief from them so they cannot carry it, and in doing so it steals their ability to finish.

Heilyn son of Gwyn opens the third door.

The grief comes back all at once. Every loss, every dead face, every moment of the war, every year of their enchanted peace shown suddenly as what it was — time taken. Branwen dead on an Irish riverbank, grieving that two islands were destroyed for her sake. The weight of everything floods back and the seven survivors stand in the hall with the grief of eighty years falling on them in a single moment.

They weep. Then they pick up the head and finish the journey. They bury it at London. Bran's head faces France. The journey is done.

The Second Branch is about this: the completion that follows grief, not the completion that avoids it. The hall at Gwales was mercy but it was also delay. Opening the door was the only way to finish.

### Chapter Notes

**Opening (Matholwch's ships):** Matholwch arrives seeking Branwen's hand. The alliance is celebrated. Efnisien, Bran's half-brother, mutilates the horses in unprovoked fury — the wound from which everything grows. Bran compensates with the cauldron.

**Branwen's Abasement:** Branwen in kitchen servitude, struck daily, her status stripped. She trains the starling. The letter reaches Bran.

**The Crossing:** Bran and his army cross to Ireland. Bran wades the Irish Sea. Mathowlch flees across the Shannon, breaks the bridge, builds a house large enough for Bran to enter — this is the first time he has been housed. The truce, fragile.

**The War:** Efnisien murders Gwern, the Irish-British prince, in the feast. War begins. The cauldron of rebirth in Irish hands: the dead return as silent fighters. Efnisien hides among the Irish dead and bursts the cauldron from inside, dying. Bran wounded. Only seven Britons survive.

**Branwen's Death:** Branwen on the Irish shore, watching the destruction, says: "Woe is me that I was ever born; two islands have been destroyed because of me." She dies of grief there. One of the series' most formally devastating moments: the innocent person who carries the guilt anyway.

**The Head's Journey:** Seven survivors, living head, Harlech birds. The enchanted hall at Gwales. Fourscore years. The forbidden door. Heilyn opens it. Grief floods back. They complete the journey to London. The burial on the White Hill. The talisman in place.

### Pass 2 — Slower

**Bran the Blessed:** The novel's (myth's) elemental host — a man too large for any building, whose generosity never stops and never saves. His head retains his personality; it is good company in the enchanted hall. He is the best version of himself after death. This is the Second Branch's gentle dark joke: the living Bran was too large, too generous, too slow to stop Efnisien; the dead Bran is the ideal king, speaking wisdom and goodwill from a box, asking only to be carried to the right destination.

**Branwen:** The myth's center and its most powerless figure. She did nothing except exist as the object of a political alliance. She was punished for Efnisien's act. She trained the starling. She caused, through no fault of her own, the deaths of the people who loved her. She dies of grief for this. The myth gives her the dignity of her grief — she does not minimize it, she names it accurately. Two islands were destroyed because of her. She could not prevent it. She acknowledges both things.

**Heilyn son of Gwyn:** The man who opens the forbidden door gets no defense in the text. He is simply the man who could not not open it. After eighty years in the hall, the grief held at bay and the door facing Cornwall — he looked at the door and opened it. The myth does not judge him. It gives him no motivation except that the door was there and the enchantment was eventually incomplete against human curiosity.

**The Enchanted Hall as a Moral Structure:** Gwales is not evil. The hall protected the survivors. The eighty years were genuinely peaceful — not illusion, not malicious. But the protection came at the cost of the grief being real: somewhere, Branwen was still dead on the Irish shore, and the survivors were not allowing themselves to know it. Opening the door is the only way to finish. The door had to be opened. Heilyn was the one who opened it.

### Pass 3 — The Scene: After the Door

The door faces Cornwall. The light through it is ordinary light — the same Pembrokeshire morning light that would have been there yesterday and the day before and for eighty years of yesterday and before, held off by the enchantment and now simply the sky.

Heilyn has stepped back from the door. He is not defending himself. He is standing in the ordinary light with his hands at his sides and the look on his face of a man who has done something that cannot be undone and knows it. He is not a villain. He opened a door. He did not know — or he did know, and the knowing did not stop him, which is a different kind of knowing.

The grief comes as a physical fact. This is how the text describes it and it is correct: grief received all at once after fourscore years of being held away is not a feeling but a weight change. The seven survivors stand in the hall and the weight of everything they have been not-carrying lands on them simultaneously. Branwen dead on the Irish shore. The war. The cauldron used against them by their own gift. Efnisien dead inside the thing he broke. Bran's wound, the poisoned spear, the long death that they carried across the sea. All of it, all at once.

The Fighter is not one of the seven. The Fighter is the eighth person — the escort, the guard, the one who has been carrying something on behalf of the group. The Fighter does not have eighty years of held grief to receive. What the Fighter has is the six remaining survivors weeping in an ordinary room, and the head on the table before them, and the journey still unfinished.

Bran's head is watching them.

**Bran's Head:** *(after a long time)* "The door is open. This was always what the hall was for — not to keep the grief away forever, but to hold it until the journey was ready. We are ready now."

One of the survivors looks up. His face is wet.

**Bran's Head:** "We are ready now. Pick me up. It is time to go to London."

They pick him up. They go out through the door that faces Cornwall, into the ordinary Pembrokeshire light. The hall is empty behind them. The journey continues.

*The Fighter holds the cord — Branwen's cord, the silk loop she used to tie the letter to the starling's leg — and follows the survivors into the light.*

### Seven Storyline Ideas — Cycle 2

1. **Carrying the Head (Branwen)** ← *CHOSEN* — Seven survivors, the living head, Gwales and the enchanted hall, the forbidden door, the grief-flood, the burial at London.

4. **The Maker of Wives from Flowers** — A man forbidden any wife of the earth's race; two magicians make him a woman from blossom; she falls in love with a stranger the first night; the Fighter is caught between the conspiracy and the husband's household.

7. **The Seventh Survivor** — After the burial at London, one survivor must carry a sealed account of what happened in the hall at Gwales to a caer whose keeper has been waiting for the news since before the war began; the account names Heilyn and the door and the fourscore years; it is the only document that names both the grief and the mercy that held it.

### Theme Check

**Existing active themes (selection):** ancient witnesses and the relay of questions (KIR-01) · the carrier who arrives with a reason to live (GCI-02) · grief carried in an object (INV/BHD/GDN) · loyalty as patience (LHR/STN) · the oath remembered when everyone else forgets (LHR-02).

**Themes being avoided:** KIR-01 already claimed "the relay of questions." The grief-in-an-object theme (INV/BHD/GDN) is about objects that hold specific individual grief. This is different: the grief is collective, held at bay by enchantment, received all at once — not an object's weight but a flood.

**What this quest introduces:** *Grief deferred by enchantment is grief doubled when received — and the only path through it is the unfinished journey; completion is the specific form of grief that does not break you.* No existing quest in the map uses deferred grief as its mechanism — the grief that was protected against, that floods back when the protection fails, and that can only be processed by finishing the thing the protection was delaying.

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *Carrying the Head*

**THE TOKEN: BRANWEN'S CORD** — A loop of silk cord, strong enough to have held a letter to a starling's leg through an Irish crossing, now loose and soft in the Fighter's hands. It was found on the Irish shore where Branwen died — one of the seven survivors folded it and held it all the way through Harlech and Gwales without knowing why, and at the hall's far side he pressed it into the Fighter's keeping: "She gave him the letter. He heard it. Carry this with us." The cord is the only surviving object Branwen touched.

---

**ACT I — The Shore**
*Branwen's cord: found on the shore, folded, placed in the Fighter's keeping. Location: the Irish shoreline after the battle — new node BRC.*

Seven men stand on a shore of the dead. Their dead outnumber them beyond counting. The Irish dead are no different — the cauldron has been broken but the work was done, and the field is field-colored. Bran's head is in Pryderi's arms, still alive, still talking softly to itself.

Manawydan kneels on the shore. He lifts something from the mud — a small loop of silk cord, the kind used to bind letters to birds. He holds it a moment. Then he walks to the Fighter.

**Manawydan:** "She tied the letter to the starling with this. He heard it and came." He places it in the Fighter's hands. "Carry this with the head. She should go home."

*The Fighter closes their hand around it. It is lighter than loss. It is not lighter than loss.*

**Bran's Head:** *(from Pryderi's arms, calmly)* "We go to Harlech first. The birds will meet us there."

---

**ACT II — Harlech**
*Branwen's cord: in the Fighter's pack, kept dry. Location: Harlech coastal promontory — new node HLG.*

Rhiannon's birds sing over the sea at Harlech. They are the three birds whose song can wake the dead and send the living into the deepest rest — their music is not a sound exactly but a movement in the air that makes the body want to stop. The seven survivors sit down to feast. They have been walking since Ireland. They deserve the rest.

The feast is enchanted. Time does not quite pass at Harlech the way it passes elsewhere. The singer-birds are in the trees across the water, visible, their mouths open.

*The Fighter understands: stay awake. Someone must.*

They walk the perimeter of the feast with Bran's head in its covered frame. The birds sing. Two of the survivors are already asleep at the table. The head speaks quietly to Pryderi, who is not asleep yet but is close.

The birds are very beautiful. Their song is very old. It goes into the chest and settles there and asks only that you close your eyes.

*The Fighter counts their own steps: one, two, three, four. One, two, three, four.*

The seven years at Harlech end when the seventh dawn comes and the birds stop singing. The survivors wake, stretch, and look at each other with the expression of people who have been somewhere and are not entirely sure where.

---

**ACT III — Gwales**
*Branwen's cord: in the Fighter's coat, against the chest. Location: Gwales, the enchanted hall — new node GWL.*

The hall has three doors. Two face east and north, open as invited. One faces Cornwall. At the Cornwall door there is a quality of air that the Fighter does not understand except that it is the quality of something held.

The seven survivors have been in the hall for a long time. The grief is not present here. This is its gift and its cost. Bran's head is good company — he tells stories, he asks questions, he remembers things that happened before any of them were born. The corn in his beard is always fresh. The ale is always cool.

But Heilyn son of Gwyn is looking at the door.

He has been looking at the door for years. Not always, not obsessively — but the Fighter has seen him look. He looks at it the way a person looks at a thing they have decided they will not do and are still deciding.

*The Fighter thinks: he is going to open it. Not today. But today is the day they will not be watching.*

*Read him. Find the moment.*

---

**ACT IV — The Opening**
*Branwen's cord: in the Fighter's fist. Location: Gwales, the hall, the Cornwall door.*

Heilyn opens the door.

The light through it is ordinary Pembrokeshire light — the same light that was there yesterday and every day for fourscore years, just outside, held off by the enchantment. He has both hands on the door and his face is set with the look of a man who has done something that cannot be undone.

The grief arrives. It does not approach — it is simply there, all of it, the whole weight that the hall was holding: Branwen on the Irish shore, the field, the cauldron used against them, Efnisien dead inside it. The seven survivors stand in the hall with eighty years of grief falling on them simultaneously.

They weep. This is correct. The weeping is what needed to happen.

*The Fighter holds the cord and does not weep, because the grief in the cord has been there since the Irish shore and the Fighter has been carrying it all along; theirs is not the same deferred grief; theirs is the grief of having watched.*

After a long time, Bran's head speaks.

**Bran's Head:** "The door is open. This was always what the hall was for — to hold the grief until the journey was ready. We are ready now. Pick me up."

They pick him up. They go out through the open door.

---

**ACT V — The White Hill**
*Branwen's cord: placed in the burial chamber with the head. Location: Gwynvryn (the White Hill, London) — new node LDN.*

The burial ground on the White Hill looks out over the Thames and, on clear days, toward the east where France lies. The head faces outward. The seven survivors have carried it from Ireland through Wales through the birds and the hall and the grief to this hill, and this is the place it was always going.

The stone is placed. The chamber is covered.

The Fighter holds the cord — Branwen's silk loop, Branwen's last connection to the letter that started the war that killed her. The Fighter sets it in the chamber before the stone is rolled.

**Pryderi:** *(quietly, watching)* "She should be here."

**Fighter:** *(one word)* "She is."

The stone rolls. The head faces France. Britain is protected for as long as the head remains unexhumed — though the myth notes that Arthur eventually digs it up, because he will not have any protection of Britain except his own. That is another story, for another time.

*The Fighter stands on the White Hill and watches the Thames below. The cord is in the stone. The journey is done.*
