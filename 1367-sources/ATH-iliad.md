# ATH — The Iliad
**Author:** Homer
**Source file:** ATH-iliad.txt (907 KB — over 400 KB threshold, drawn from memory)
**Period:** Composed c. 8th century BCE; drawing on oral traditions of the Bronze Age Mycenaean world
**Translation note:** Multiple English translations circulate (Chapman 1598–1616, Pope 1715–1720, Lattimore 1951, Fagles 1990); this summary draws on the poem's substance without committing to a single translation's diction
**Category:** Ancient Greek Epic, 24 Books in dactylic hexameter

---

## Pass 1 — Full Telling

The Iliad's first word is *mênis* — wrath — and everything that follows is the shape that word makes when dropped into a world. The poem covers less than two months of the tenth year of the Trojan War, but it carries the weight of ten years in every line, and the weight of what comes after — Troy's fall, Achilles's death, the long voyage home — in the silences between them. It is a poem about what anger costs, but also about what war asks of the people who are not warriors, and about the strange mercy that breaks open at the point where grief becomes universal.

The immediate cause is small: Agamemnon, commander of the Greek forces, insults Achilles by taking his war-prize, the captive woman Briseis. Achilles responds not with violence but with withdrawal — he removes himself from the war entirely and asks his divine mother Thetis to petition Zeus that the Greeks suffer without him. Zeus agrees. The Greeks begin losing catastrophically. The poem then traces the arc of that catastrophe until Patroclus's death forces Achilles back, and then the arc of Achilles's grief and rage until Priam's embassy brings it to its end.

**The Historical Moment**: The poem was composed in an era looking back to a world that had already ended — the Mycenaean palace cultures had collapsed three or four centuries before Homer. The Bronze Age heroes are presented with an awareness that they belong to a grander, heavier world than the present one: they throw rocks that two ordinary men couldn't lift; they shout across battlefields; they are touched by gods. The poem preserves something about a human world before the categories of history and philosophy had been fully developed — and it is savage and beautiful in the way things are that have not been organized yet.

**The Embassy (Book 9)**: When the Greeks are desperate, Agamemnon sends Phoenix (Achilles's old tutor), Ajax (the strongest of the Greeks after Achilles), and Odysseus (the cleverest) to offer magnificent gifts and restoration of Briseis if Achilles will return. Each man makes his argument differently. Odysseus makes the practical case: you will get everything you were promised, the Greeks need you. Phoenix makes the emotional case: remember I raised you; remember the story of Meleager who held out too long and lost everything. Ajax makes the direct case: a man accepts blood-money for a murdered kinsman; why not you? Achilles says Ajax touched his heart most of all, and still refuses. He offers no argument — he simply cannot. His injury was not to his interests or his honor in the way those categories normally operate; it was something more absolute. He tells them he is going home. He doesn't go home.

**Hector's Farewell (Book 6)**: In the middle of battle, Hector goes back to Troy to arrange ritual supplication of Athena. He passes through the city — a city of women and old men while the men are at the wall — and finds Andromache and their infant son Astyanax at the Scaean Gate. They speak. Andromache tells him he is the city; if he dies, she is a slave, and their son is an orphan at someone else's table. Hector takes off his helmet because Astyanax is terrified of the plume, and father and son laugh together for a moment. Hector puts the helmet back on. He says he knows Troy will fall, and that the worst of it will be Andromache's enslavement. He leaves. He does not say he can stop it. He says: "let me not die without a struggle, but gloriously, having done something great for generations to come." Then he walks back to the killing ground.

**Patroclus (Books 11–16)**: Patroclus has been watching the Greeks die throughout the poem, growing increasingly distressed. He comes to Achilles in tears — the great warrior sitting by his ships playing the lyre, singing of famous men — and pleads: if you won't return, let me go in your armor; let me look like you; the Trojans will think Achilles has returned and it will break their momentum. Achilles agrees, with conditions: go to the ships, save them, but do not push too far, do not take glory from me. Patroclus agrees and goes. He is magnificent. He drives the Trojans back, fights like a god, forgets the conditions entirely, pushes to the walls of Troy, is stunned by Apollo, stripped of his helmet, and killed by Hector. Hector takes Achilles's armor from the body.

**Achilles's Return (Books 18–22)**: Achilles hears of Patroclus's death from Antilochus. He does not speak. He collapses into the dirt and pours ash on his head and tears his hair. His grief is total and specific and animal. Thetis comes up from the sea to hold his head and weep with him. She has known since his birth that he would die young and gloriously or live long and ignobly; she knows which choice the death of Patroclus has sealed. She brings him new armor from Hephaestus — the shield of Achilles, the most famous ekphrasis in ancient literature, on which the god engraved the entire world: a city at war, a city at peace, a wedding, a harvest, a vintage, dancers, the ocean encircling everything. The world in miniature, worn by the man who is going to end someone's portion of it.

Achilles reconciles with Agamemnon, dons the new armor, and returns to battle. He is not seeking victory anymore; he is seeking Hector. He kills at a scale that makes the river Scamander choke on bodies and rise against him. He is stopped only when the river itself floods him out; the gods on both sides fight each other directly; the whole world is in disorder. Then he finds Hector.

Hector waits outside the Scaean Gate. Priam and Hecuba beg him from the walls to come inside. He stands there, and when Achilles approaches he runs — circles the walls of Troy three times with Achilles behind him, both men running at divine speed while the gods watch — and then turns to fight. Athena deceives him into standing. They fight. Achilles kills him. He ties the body to his chariot and drags it around the walls before Troy's watching eyes.

**Book 24 — Priam and Achilles**: For twelve days Achilles drags Hector's body around Patroclus's grave each morning. The gods hold a council and decree that the ransom must happen: Priam must go, with mule cart and treasure, to Achilles's tent and ransom his son's body. Priam loads the cart himself, weeping, dismissing his surviving sons who try to help. He drives through the night with only Idaeus his old herald. Hermes meets them disguised as a young man and guides them through the sleeping Greek camp. Hermes leaves them at Achilles's door.

Priam enters alone. He finds Achilles at table. He clasps Achilles's knees — the gesture of supplication — and says: *"Think of your own father, Achilles. He is old, as I am, and has only the hope that you are alive. I have endured what no man on earth has endured: I have kissed the hands of the man who killed my sons."*

Achilles begins to weep. He thinks of his own father, Peleus, whom he will never see again. He takes Priam's hands and raises him up and they weep together: Achilles for Patroclus and for his father; Priam for Hector and for all his dead sons. Then Achilles says: I will do it. He has Hector's body bathed and wrapped and placed on the mule cart. He promises Priam a truce of eleven days for the burial. He feeds Priam. Priam sleeps in the shelter. At dawn Hermes wakes him and sends him home before the camp wakes and sees a Trojan king among them.

The poem ends eleven days later, with Hector's funeral. "Such were the rites of horse-taming Hector."

The Iliad contains nothing after this. Troy does not fall in the poem. Achilles does not die in the poem. The burning and the endings happen in the silence that follows the last line. What the poem shows is this: that the greatest warrior who ever lived was also a grieving son and a man who could be reached by another man's grief; that the most human moment of the entire ten-year war was a night in a tent when two enemies wept together and then shared a meal; and that this moment required a formal structure — the cart, the treasure, the act of supplication — because without form, the human act cannot happen in a world at war. Priam could not simply walk in. He had to bring the ransom. Not because Achilles required it, but because the transaction needed a grammar.

## Pass 2 — Character Deep-Dives

**Achilles**: The poem's most contradictory figure. He is the fastest, the most beautiful, the most dangerous, the most doomed. He knows his destiny — early glorious death or long obscure life — and he has chosen early death without being able to fully metabolize what that choice means. His wrath in Book 1 is disproportionate in a way that makes sense only if you understand that he is not really angry about Briseis: he is angry about the nature of a world that asks him to bleed for men who will never fully honor him. Patroclus's death transforms this into grief, which is wrath turned inward and then outward at a specific target. When he meets Priam, something in him breaks open: the grief is the same on both sides, and he recognizes it.

**Hector**: The poem's other emotional center and its truest hero in the conventional sense — loyal, brave, loving, responsible. He fights knowing his city will fall and his wife will be enslaved. He keeps fighting not because he thinks he can prevent it but because this is what he is: Hector-under-arms. His farewell to Astyanax is the poem's most devastating small scene. The baby screams at the plumed helmet. Hector takes it off and holds his son. Then he puts it back on.

**Patroclus**: The most lovable character in the poem. He appears first weeping at Greek suffering; he is gentle; he cares for wounded men; he is the only person who can reach Achilles. His death is the poem's hinge. Everything before it is the buildup; everything after is the consequence. He borrows the armor to look like Achilles and is destroyed at the point where he forgot the difference.

**Priam**: Old, diminished, watching his sons die from the walls of his city. His night journey to Achilles is the single bravest act in the poem — not the bravery of the battlefield but the bravery of a father who has lost everything except one last thing he can do. He crosses the enemy camp guided only by a disguised god. He clasps his enemy's knees. He says the truest thing anyone says in the poem.

**Andromache**: She knows Hector will die. She says it clearly when she sees him at the Scaean Gate. She is right. Her grief after his death — she hears the wailing and runs to the wall and faints when she sees his body dragged by the chariot — is the poem's fullest account of what war does to the people it doesn't kill.

**Idaeus**: The old herald who drives Priam's cart. He waits with the horses and mules while Priam enters Achilles's tent. He is a minor character, but he is the witness — the only Trojan who sees both sides: the cart loaded, the body returned, the road home in the dark. He carries the formal commission tablet that authorizes the embassy. He carries it back to Troy. He is the man who holds the last diplomatic document of the city that is about to burn.

**Thetis**: Achilles's divine mother, who knows he is going to die and cannot prevent it. She holds his head when he collapses into the dust at Patroclus's death. She goes to Hephaestus and asks for armor. She has been mourning her son since before he was born.

**Odysseus**: Clever, pragmatic, useful. His argument to Achilles in Book 9 is the most politically sophisticated and the least effective. He understands the world too well to understand Achilles.

**Ajax**: Big, honest, limited. His argument to Achilles is the most human: you accept blood-money for a dead kinsman, why not this? Achilles says he was most moved by Ajax. He still refuses. Ajax walks out disgusted. There is something very real about a man who argues well and is still refused.

## Pass 3 — Pivotal Scene: Book 24, Stanzas 477–551 (Lattimore numbering)

*Priam enters Achilles's shelter. He moves unseen to Achilles's feet.*

He clasps his knees. He kisses the terrible hands — "the manslaughtering hands that had killed so many of his sons." He speaks: *"Think of your father, Achilles like the gods. He is old as I am, at the door-sill of sorrowful old age, and the men who live around him wear him down, and there is no one to defend him from ruin. Yet he, hearing that you are still alive, is gladdened in his heart and hopes each day that he will see his beloved son return home from Troy. But I, my fate is entire wretchedness, for I have had the finest sons in all of wide Troy, and not one, I say, is left to me..."*

Achilles begins to weep. He takes Priam's hands and gently pushes him back. Both men weep. The shelter is loud with grief. Then Achilles rises: *"Come then, sit beside me on the chair, and we will let our sorrows lie still in the heart for all our grieving. There is not any advantage to be won from grim lamentation."*

He goes out and prepares Hector's body himself — he does not want Priam to see it in the state Achilles has kept it in; he is protecting an old man from more grief than he needs. The body is washed, anointed, wrapped in fine clothing. It is placed on the bier. Only then does Achilles let Priam look.

*"Friend, we have done what you asked. Let no word of my answer anger you."* They eat together — mutton and bread and wine. Priam stares at Achilles, wondering at his size and beauty. Achilles stares at Priam, wondering at the old man's dignity. The poem's word for their mutual looking is one of the most poignant in Greek: they are both *thaumazô* — astonished, filled with wonder. Enemies at dinner. The war outside continuing.

Then: "Priam, son of Dardanos, spent the night there and slept in the hall of assembly." And before dawn, Hermes wakes him. "Old sir, you seem to have no thought of danger. Achilles sleeps, but what if his companions see you?" The old man rises and loads his son's body and drives home through the sleeping camp, guided by a god, in a night that the poem holds very carefully, as if it knows this is the last good thing it will be given to describe.

## Seven Storylines

1. **The Herald's Tablet** — Idaeus the old herald has carried the formal commission document from Priam's embassy — the last diplomatic record of Troy before the city burned — and needs it delivered to an archive before the Greeks claim everything as war-spoil. *(Selected)*

2. **The Embassy's Offer** — The formal list of gifts Agamemnon offered Achilles in Book 9 was composed by Odysseus and carried by Phoenix; Achilles rejected it; the document exists now as evidence of what was offered and refused; after the war, it has bearing on questions of inheritance and honor-debt among Agamemnon's successors.

3. **Patroclus's Commission** — Before he went out in Achilles's armor, Patroclus asked Achilles in specific terms not to press too far; the witnesses to that conversation include Nestor and several squires; after the war, Achilles's heirs want a formal record of whether Achilles instructed Patroclus to advance or to hold.

4. **Andromache's Loom** — Andromache was at her loom weaving a purple cloth figured with flowers when she heard the wailing from the walls; she dropped the shuttle and ran; the cloth was never finished; someone has preserved it — half a record of the last morning of a normal life.

5. **Helen at the Funeral** — Helen wept at Hector's funeral, the only non-family member to do so, because he had been kind to her; she said "twenty years I have been here and never heard a harsh word from you"; her words need to be recorded before the Greek version of her story becomes the only one.

6. **The Shield's Description** — Hephaestus engraved the entire world on Achilles's shield before the battle that would destroy most of it; one of his divine attendants preserved a description of the images; after Achilles's death the shield was taken by Ajax and eventually disputed; the description is the only record of what was on it before the battles scratched it.

7. **Hecuba's Supplication** — Hecuba, queen of Troy, went to Athena's temple on the citadel with the other Trojan women to beg for the goddess's protection; she offered Athena's priestess the most beautiful robe in her treasury; Athena refused; the robe was left on the altar; someone needs to return it to Troy's archive — the official record of a supplication that failed.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: "The Herald's Tablet" (ATH_001)

*French noir register. The Fighter is a carrier. The TOKEN is the last grammar of a city.*

---

**TOKEN**: The Herald's Tablet

A clay tablet, palm-sized, fired rather than just sun-dried — someone anticipated it needed to last. On the face: the seal impression of Priam's royal device, a double horse, pressed into the clay before firing. Below it, incised in Mycenaean script: the formal commission authorizing the herald Idaeus to conduct the person and property of the king of Troy to the tent of the Achaean champion and to receive in exchange the body of the prince Hector, son of Priam.

The tablet is dull grey. It has been carried in Idaeus's pack since the night he drove the mule cart through the sleeping Greek camp. It weighs almost nothing. It is the last document the Trojan royal household ever issued, and it is in the correct form: witnessed, sealed, dated, authorized.

Without it, the embassy to Achilles is a story — a father who crawled to his son's killer. With it, the embassy is a diplomatic record: an authorized transaction conducted under the protocols of guest-friendship and warrior honor, the response to which was also honorable. Two different historical facts.

**TOKEN DOCTRINE**: TOKEN granted Act 1 (Idaeus's hands, in the refugee camp). TOKEN checked Acts 2–4. TOKEN delivered Act 5 (Weimar archive vault — the tablet enters the permanent record; the Fighter keeps nothing except the memory of what it weighed).

---

### Act 1 — Idaeus's Shelter (IDC)
*Node: IDC (new). Skill: Insight DC 12. TOKEN GRANTED.*

The refugee camp outside the ruins smells of ash and salt and the specific sour quality of too many people without enough water. Idaeus is in a lean-to at the camp's edge — he is old enough that the other survivors have given him space without discussing it. He has been trying to explain the tablet's significance to anyone who would listen. No one wants to hear about diplomatic commissions. Everyone is alive and that is what matters.

He holds the tablet out to the Fighter. He explains what it is in plain terms: the formal commission document from Priam's last embassy. The only one who cares is him. He is going to die soon. Someone should carry it somewhere that receives such things.

Insight DC 12: The Fighter understands that Idaeus is not asking for the document to be preserved as a trophy or curiosity. He is asking for it to be preserved as an administrative record — specifically as the evidence that what happened in Achilles's tent happened in the correct form; that Priam went as a king exercising diplomatic authority, not as a broken old man begging; that Achilles received him under guest-friendship, not as an act of private mercy. The difference is enormous. One enters the record as law; the other enters it as anecdote.

*Success*: The Fighter takes the tablet with both hands. Idaeus nods.
*Failure*: The Fighter reaches for it while asking questions about the events themselves. Idaeus pulls it back — not because he won't give it, but because the question misses the point. After a moment's silence, he pushes it forward again with a look that says: the story does not matter. This matters.

**TOKEN GRANTED.**

---

### Act 2 — Camp Perimeter Road (IDC outskirts)
*Node: IDC road. Skill: Persuasion DC 13. TOKEN CHECKED.*

A Greek officer is operating a checkpoint controlling what Trojan survivors carry out of the camp. He has authority over all material property. He sees the clay tablet, notes the seal, and wants it catalogued as war-spoil: Priam's seal belongs to the Greek general who claims the royal treasury.

*The argument*: A diplomatic commission document is not royal treasury. It is a legal record created in the course of an authorized transaction. The transaction it documents was completed under the personal authority of Achilles himself — the Greek commander authorized to grant and receive such embassies. Confiscating it as war-spoil invalidates the protections under which it was issued, retroactively dishonoring the treaty Achilles made with Priam. The officer almost certainly does not want to be the man who is recorded as having dishonored Achilles's last act of warrior's mercy.

Persuasion DC 13.
*Success*: The officer notes the tablet in his log as "diplomatic record, released under Achilles protocol" and stamps it through.
*Failure*: He holds it for review. A delay of half a day while the officer consults a superior who agrees with the argument the Fighter already made. The tablet is released.

---

### Act 3 — Ruins of the Scaean Gate Road (SKN)
*Node: SKN (new). Skill: Persuasion DC 14 + possible combat. TOKEN CHECKED.*

The road through the ruins passes the Scaean Gate — or what remains of it. Two armed men are waiting here. Their lord is a Greek captain who claims to have been present at the original insult to Achilles in Book 1; he has built a legal argument that Agamemnon's offer of gifts in Book 9 creates a precedent in honor-debt that should pass to him as an heir to Achilles's honor-claims. He wants the commission tablet as evidence that the embassy in Book 24 superseded or resolved the honor-debt created in Book 1, which would collapse his own claim.

He is using the tablet as a legal instrument.

*The argument*: The commission document records a transaction between Priam and Achilles — not between Agamemnon and Achilles, and not between any third party and anyone. Using it as evidence in a honor-debt claim between Greeks would require reading Priam's act of supplication as a resolution of a Greek-internal dispute, which it was not. The tablet is evidence of what it says it is: an authorized ransom-embassy. If it is cited in a different legal context, its meaning in its original context is destroyed.

Persuasion DC 14.
*Success*: The men step aside.
*Failure*: Combat: 2× soldiers, AC 13, HP 16 each. The tablet is not damaged — the soldiers were not authorized to destroy it, only to acquire it.

### Act 4 — River Ford (road between SKN and WM)
*Node: road ford. Skill: Athletics DC 13. TOKEN CHECKED.*

A river in early flood has washed out the bridge. The ford is waist-deep. The clay tablet is fired and durable but the seal impression on its face can be softened if the tablet soaks; the incised text would survive immersion but the royal seal would blur.

Athletics DC 13: the Fighter must cross without allowing the tablet to be submerged. It can get wet; it cannot soak. There is no persuasion option — the river is not an argument. The ford must be taken.

*Success*: The tablet arrives damp on its edges but the seal is intact.
*Failure*: The ford is taken the hard way; the tablet is submerged for a moment. The seal impression is softened. The text is intact. At Act 5, the Archivus will note this — not as a failure but as an additional record: "seal softened in transit, text fully legible, commission confirmed authentic by content." The tablet is still received and filed.

### Act 5 — The Weimar Archive (WM)
*Node: WM. Skill: Insight DC 12. TOKEN DELIVERED (TAKEN).*

The Archivus Sweelinck receives documents at a long desk. He takes the tablet, reads the commission text, notes the seal (softened or intact), and consults his register of known diplomatic formats. He finds the format consistent with known Mycenaean royal commission structure.

He sets the tablet down and looks at it for a moment.

"There is a separate entry for the burning of Troy," he says. "Under destruction events. This document does not belong there." He makes the notation in a different register — under Diplomatic Records, Trojan Cycle, and under an entry labeled *Priam of Troy: last known official act.* He carries the tablet himself to the permanent vault.

Before he returns, he says: "This was the last night Troy was Troy."

Insight DC 12: The Fighter understands that this phrase describes a specific thing — not Troy's last night before burning (which came eleven days later), but the last night on which Troy was, functionally, a city: governed by a king exercising lawful diplomatic authority, producing documents in correct form, performing acts that would be honored by the enemy's own champion. After that night, Troy was already a city-in-the-past, waiting for the fire to make it official. The tablet is the boundary marker.

TOKEN TAKEN. The Archivus does not say what he keeps. He keeps the tablet. He returns to his desk. The Fighter leaves.

*What the Fighter carries out*: Nothing material. The knowledge that the record exists. The last line of what was spoken: **"This was the last night Troy was Troy."** — the archive's entry for a city that could still produce a just act on its final night, at its lowest point, under the worst conditions, with only an old king and an old herald and a mule cart and a god in disguise.

## Cycle 2 · Helen at the Funeral

**New angle:** Cycle 1 was about form — the diplomatic document that transforms an act of grief into a legal record, the commission tablet that makes the night in Achilles's tent an authorized transaction rather than a private moment. Cycle 2 is about voice — specifically the one account in the entire poem that records what the last twenty years felt like from inside the city, by the person whose name the war was fought over. When Helen wept at Hector's funeral, she said she had been in Troy twenty years and had never heard an ill or hard word from Hector. That is the only record she left. What cycle 1 missed: the account from the cause, delivered at the burial of the man who showed her kindness she had not earned; and the institutional problem that her voice at that funeral will be absorbed into the Greek version unless someone carries it out.

---

### Token Doctrine

**TOKEN: Helen's Funeral Account**
A sealed folio in Helen's own hand — composed the night of the funeral while the city burned around her on its last night. Not a self-defense; a record: what she said at the bier, and why she said it, and what twenty years in Troy had been. She gave it to her Trojan attendant Aethra in case she did not survive the sack. Aethra survived. She has no possessions now. The folio must go to an archive where it will be filed under Helen's name as her primary record.

### 5-Act Vignette Play: "Helen at the Funeral"

*A Fighter has been retained by a Trojan woman at the harbor to carry a sealed folio out of the ruins of Troy before she loses all possessions. The TOKEN ITEM is Helen's Funeral Account: Helen's own record of what she said at Hector's funeral and what she meant by it. The Fighter's job is to get it out of the Greek camp and into an archive that will file it under Helen's name.*

*The Thing · Helen's Funeral Account · sealed folio in a cedar sleeve · Aethra → Fighter*

---

**ACT I — Troy Harbor**

*The loading dock. Greek ships being provisioned. Trojan women in lines at the harbor registry. Aethra — Helen's elderly Athenian attendant, here since before the war — at the edge of the line with a cedar sleeve under her arm.*

*The Thing · Helen's Funeral Account · sealed · not yet in the Fighter's hands*

*The Fighter observes: the cedar sleeve is old and well-made. Aethra has been protecting it for a while.*

Aethra explains quickly and without sentiment: Helen gave this to her the night of the funeral, before the Greeks entered the city. She does not know what happens to her now. She is old and has no family and will be given to someone as a slave. She cannot keep anything. But this should not disappear. Helen wrote it herself. She was at the bier. She said the things written in here. Nobody else wrote them down.

She presses the sleeve into the Fighter's hands and turns back toward the registry line without looking back.

---

**ACT II — The Camp Perimeter**

*The Greek camp's exit checkpoint. A records officer checking departing documents.*

*The Thing · Helen's Funeral Account · sealed cedar sleeve · Fighter's pack*

*The Fighter observes: the officer has a list of items belonging to Helen that have been designated as Menelaus's property pending his return to Sparta.*

He sees the cedar sleeve. He asks to note it in the property log. Helen's written materials, he says, are technically her husband's property under Greek law. To leave with such a thing requires authorization from Menelaus's household steward, who is three ships down the harbor.

The argument: this is a record of a public speech delivered at a public funeral before thousands of people. A public speech is not private property. The document records an event; it is not Helen's possession in the relevant sense; it is a record of something that happened.

---

**ACT III — The Harbor Road**

*An hour past the checkpoint. A man in Menelaus's livery comes down the road on a fast horse.*

*The Thing · Helen's Funeral Account · sealed cedar sleeve · Fighter's pack · subject of the review*

*The Fighter observes: the messenger has a written instruction from Menelaus's household steward, not from Menelaus himself. The steward is managing the paperwork of reassembly. The instruction gives him review rights over materials attributed to Helen.*

He is not hostile. He explains: Menelaus's household needs to review all recorded statements attributed to Helen before they enter any archive, to ensure the record is accurate. He is prepared to be reasonable. He only needs to read it before it is filed.

The problem with this is precise: if the steward's household reads it first and decides what "accurate" means in the context of Menelaus's return to Sparta, the account will be filtered before it reaches any archive. Even if nothing is changed, the filter itself is a modification of the record. Helen's voice only arrives clean if it arrives directly.

---

**ACT IV — The Mountain Road**

*The road from the harbor toward the archive city. A stand of pine above the road.*

*The Thing · Helen's Funeral Account · sealed cedar sleeve · targeted*

*The Fighter observes: three men. No livery. The kind of men hired for results, not for questions.*

The hard faction doesn't want a review. Menelaus's household is in two minds about Helen's return; the harder element — the one that wanted her killed on the beach and was talked out of it — doesn't want any record that humanizes her circulating before the Greek account is fixed. If the folio disappears, it disappears. Nobody knows what was in it.

Combat: 2× hired men (AC 12, HP 20, short swords and leather)

---

**ACT V — The Archive**

*The archive receiving room. A careful archivist, Marta, who specializes in classical-era materials.*

*The Thing · Helen's Funeral Account · sealed cedar sleeve · Fighter's hands → archive*

*The Fighter observes: Marta has received materials from the Trojan campaign before. She knows the classification problem.*

She opens the sleeve, reads the folio, sets it down. She says: "I can file this as 'attributed statement, Helen of Sparta, funeral of Hector.' That puts it in the secondary accounts section alongside Trojan propaganda." She looks at the Fighter. "Or I can file it as a primary record — her voice, under her name, in the primary accounts section. For that I need to establish it was composed by her, in her hand, at the time stated." She pauses. "Can you establish the chain of custody from her hand to yours without a gap?"
