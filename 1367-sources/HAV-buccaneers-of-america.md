## Pass 1 — Overview

Buccaneers of America (also published as *The Pirates of Panama*) is the primary first-hand account of Caribbean piracy in the 1660s–1670s. Exquemelin arrived in Tortuga in 1666 as a servant of the French West India Company; when the company collapsed he was sold twice — first to a cruel master, then to a kind surgeon-master who freed him after a year of service. He then joined the pirates as a barber-surgeon and remained with them until 1672, witnessing every major operation he describes. He was present: he treated the wounds; he watched the interrogations; he counted the shares.

The book's value for the pipeline is threefold:
- **The covenant structure of piracy**: Before any expedition, the crew signed articles specifying every member's share, the surgeon's fee, and exact compensation for every wound (right arm: 600 pieces of eight; left leg: 400; an eye: 100). "No prey, no pay." This is often more rigorous than any official legal document of the period.
- **The commitment structure**: Pierre le Grand had the surgeon bore a hole in the boat before boarding the Spanish vice-admiral. The committed force that cannot retreat. The fire-ship used against the Spanish squadron. Irreversibility as tactical doctrine.
- **The eyewitness in the middle**: Exquemelin is neither captain nor common mariner — he is the barber-surgeon, the one who saw what had to be treated, who counted the dead, who watched the rack. His account is valuable precisely because he has no glory to claim.

Unique features as quest material:
- The pirate articles are the clearest example in world literature of a democratic outlaw covenant — more scrupulously drafted and honored than most official agreements
- The question of legitimacy turns entirely on one document: the letter of marque (privateering commission). With it: a corsair. Without it: a pirate. The same acts; two different legal identities.
- The ransom letter / diplomatic exchange: Morgan writes to the Spanish admiral; the admiral replies with formal ultimatum; Morgan responds; the whole exchange is conducted in the language of treaty between parties at war
- The eyewitness physician: Exquemelin's position gives him full access but partial responsibility — his account is evidence without agency

## Pass 2 — Structure

### Part 1 Content (Chapters I–VIII)

**Introduction (Williams, 1914):** Context: book originally Dutch (1678), immediately translated into Spanish (two impressions in one year), noted by the Paris Academy. The 1684 English translator's preface argues these men showed courage to rival Alexander or Caesar.

**Exquemelin's Background (Chapter II):** Arrived Tortuga 1666. Sold first to a "most cruel and perfidious man" who starved and mistreated him; then sold to a surgeon for 70 pieces of eight; freed after one year. Joined the pirates. Served until 1672.

**Tortuga (Chapter II):** The island base: a single good port, a fort on a high rock accessible only by ladder, French-Spanish conflict for control, the four settlements. The governor eventually holds the island for the French West India Company.

**Hispaniola and the Buccaneers (Chapter III):** Three professions: hunter, planter, pirate. Hunters and planters are violent and degenerate; after every expedition or hunt, they spend everything on drink. The comrade/partner system: every man finds a partner and they sign articles pledging their fortunes together, with inheritance provisions. Servants are treated more cruelly than slaves because they only serve three years — masters care nothing for their survival.

**Pierre le Grand (Chapter IV):** First pirate of Tortuga. With twenty-eight men in a single boat, took a Spanish vice-admiral of the flota. Before boarding: had the surgeon bore a hole in the boat so it would sink — no retreat possible. Took the ship in the dark with pistol and sword, captured the captain playing cards in the great cabin. Sent ship to France. Never returned to piracy.

**Pierre François (Chapter V):** Twenty-six men attacked the pearl fleet. Took the vice-admiral; tried to take the man-of-war; mast carried away; caught. Surrendered with 100,000 pieces of eight in pearls aboard.

**Bartholomew Portugues (Chapter V):** Took a ship of 20 guns with 30 men, then was retaken by a Spanish squadron. Condemned to hang at Campechy. Escaped the night before his execution using two earthen wine jars as floats — he could not swim. Reached shore, made knives from nails whetted on stones, built a raft from branches, survived on shellfish, walked 40 leagues. Returned fourteen days later with 20 men and retook the same ship that had held him prisoner.

**Brasiliano (Chapter V):** Dutch/Flemish, based in Jamaica. Personal cowardice in death: brave in battle, foolish drunk. Peculiar cruelty to Spaniards — roasted some alive on spits for not revealing hog-yards. Escaped Spanish execution by writing a fake letter in the names of "pirates abroad at sea" threatening reprisals if his men were harmed. The governor, fearing the pirates at sea more than the men in his dungeon, released them.

**The Articles (Chapter V):** Before every voyage, articles signed by all:
- Share: captain 5–6 portions; master's mate 2; ordinary mariner 1; boys ½
- Surgeon's chest: 200–250 pieces of eight from common stock
- Carpenter's fee: 100–150 pieces of eight
- Provisions: ~200 pieces of eight
- Wound compensation table: right arm 600 pieces or 6 slaves; left arm 500/5; right leg 500/5; left leg 400/4; eye 100/1; finger 100/1
- Rule: all prizes equally divided; solemn oath not to conceal anything taken; violators expelled
- Among themselves: very generous; those who lose everything freely given from what others have

**Lewis Scot, Mansvelt, John Davis (Chapter V):** Land invasions begin when sea pickings thin. Scot sacked Campechy. Mansvelt penetrated to the South Sea. John Davis raided Nicaragua churches at night, took 50,000 pieces of eight in silver plate; later raided St. Augustine, Florida.

**Lolonois (Chapters VI–VIII):** French buccaneer from Les Sables d'Olone. Received a ship from the governor of Tortuga after early success. Lost it at Campechy. Survived the Spanish attack by covering himself with blood and sand and lying among the dead. Escaped, stole a canoe with slaves, returned to Tortuga. Got another ship. Faked his death so convincingly the Spanish lit bonfires. Led fleet of 660 men to take Maracaibo and Gibraltar: extreme cruelty throughout — prisoners racked, stretched, burned with palm leaves, eyes burst with cords. Took enormous ransom. His end: the text mentions he was eaten by indigenous people after being stranded in Darien — a fitting end that Exquemelin does not sentimentalize.

### Part 2 Content (Chapters IX–XVIII)

**Morgan at Puerto Bello (Chapters IX–XI):** Morgan (Welsh, born into minor gentry, came to Caribbean as indentured servant, rose through privateering) took Puerto Bello with 400 men. Forcing religious prisoners — priests and nuns — to carry scaling ladders against the castle walls while the defenders fired on them. The governor refused to surrender even when his wife and daughter begged him, and was killed fighting. Ransom: 100,000 pieces of eight. Morgan sent the Spanish president a pistol as a "sample of his arms," inviting him to come to Panama to retrieve it. The president returned the pistol, saying Morgan would not fare as well in Panama.

**Morgan at Maracaibo (Chapter XII):** Second raid on Maracaibo. Spanish had rebuilt the fort. Morgan used a fire-ship — rigged a captured vessel to look manned, filled it with combustibles, steered it into the Spanish flagship — and sank all three Spanish warships. Then was trapped in the lake with the fort blocking his exit. His solution: ostentatiously landed and re-embarked his men all day (to make the Spanish think he was landing for a direct assault on the fort); at night, lying flat in the canoes, they slipped out past the fort in the dark. The fort prepared for a land attack that never came. Morgan escaped with all his ships.

**The Admiral's Letter:** The Spanish admiral Don Alonso del Campo y Espinosa wrote Morgan a formal ultimatum: surrender all prizes, slaves, and prisoners and depart freely; resist and he would destroy them all. Morgan replied that he would burn Maracaibo if not paid ransom, and that he would come to Panama if invited. The epistolary exchange is conducted in full diplomatic form by parties who are technically at war.

**Morgan's Panama Expedition (Chapters XIII–XVIII):** 1,200 men across the isthmus. Starved for days, eating leather bags and leather pouches boiled and beaten on rocks. Took the river and reached Panama. The Spanish burned their own city to deny it to the pirates. Morgan looted for weeks, then returned to Chagre, divided the shares, and returned to Jamaica — to be arrested and sent to England. He was eventually knighted and made Deputy Governor of Jamaica.

## HAV-01 Full Vignette Spec: "The Articles"

### Source Hook

Chapter V of the book contains Exquemelin's transcription of the pirates' articles of association. Before every voyage, all members of the company signed a document specifying:
- Captain's share: 5–6 portions
- Ordinary mariner: 1 portion
- Surgeon's chest: 200–250 pieces of eight from common stock
- Wound compensation: right arm 600 pieces of eight, left arm 500, right leg 500, left leg 400, eye 100, finger 100
- Rule of division: all prizes equally split; oath not to conceal anything
- Enforcement: violators expelled

This document governed an outlaw society with more precision and more faithful compliance than most official commercial agreements of the period. The question for any court adjudicating their actions was whether they had a commission — a letter of marque from a recognized authority. With the commission plus the articles, they were a corsair company. Without the commission, the articles were evidence of organized piracy. The same document; two different legal statuses; determined entirely by one other document.

### 1367 Setting

The Knights of St. John at Rhodes are adjudicating a prize dispute. A Greek corsair company operating in the Aegean — based at Chios, operating under an alleged commission from the Genoese commune of Chios — seized a Venetian merchant vessel and held its cargo. The Venetian merchant has filed complaint. The Genoese commissioner of Chios (whose authorization was claimed) says the commission is legitimate. The Venetian consul says it was piracy.

The corsairs' articles of association — signed by all fifteen members of the company, witnessed by a Chios notary named Georgios Palaiologos — are the key evidentiary document. They establish: the company was formally constituted; the seizure was conducted under organized authority, not mob violence; every member had a named role and a specified share. If the articles reach the commandery session in time, the Prior will classify them as a corsair operation (authorized private war); without the articles, the Venetians' framing of piracy wins by default.

The corsairs' legal representative at Rhodes — a Genoese lawyer named Bartolomeo Grimaldi — has sent a carrier to Chios to retrieve the original notarized articles and bring them back to Rhodes before the session closes. The session opens the day after tomorrow.

**TOKEN:** A folded and notarized parchment — the articles of association of a Greek corsair company. All fifteen signatures are visible on the back; the notary's seal (Georgios Palaiologos, Chios) is stamped in red wax. The document smells faintly of salt air and old leather. The wound compensation table occupies the bottom third of the front face.

**TOKEN granted:** Act 1 (the notary releases the original into the carrier's hands)
**TOKEN checked:** Acts 2, 3, 4
**TOKEN taken:** Act 5 (the Prior's secretary logs the document into the session record)

**Commission city:** CHI — Chios, Genoese-administered Aegean island, 1367 under the Maona di Chios (Genoese merchant consortium)
**Delivery city:** RHD — Rhodes, headquarters of the Knights of St. John (Hospitallers), 1367 under Grand Master Raymond Bérenger

### Act 1 — Chios (Notary's Office, Harbor District)
**DC 12 | Persuasion or Deception**

Georgios Palaiologos, the Chios notary who witnessed and sealed the articles, has the original in his register box and a certified copy in his file. He is reluctant to release the original. His reasons: if he releases the original and something happens to it in transit, his professional liability as the authenticating notary is exposed; he would prefer to send a certified copy, which in his view is legally equivalent. But Bartolomeo Grimaldi's instructions were specific: the original, bearing all fifteen signatures plus the notary seal, not a copy.

The underlying issue: Georgios actually witnesses a great deal of corsair work and is worried about his professional standing with the Genoese commissioner, who has asked him not to appear too eagerly involved in this case. He is not dishonest. He is cautious.

**Skill check purpose:** Get the original document released.

- **Success (Persuasion DC 12):** The carrier persuades Georgios that a certified copy retained in his register provides all the professional protection he needs, while the original is required for the evidentiary proceeding. Georgios accepts this, stamps the carrier's receipt, and releases the document with a brief note about chain of custody.
- **Success (Deception DC 12):** The carrier claims that Grimaldi has arranged for a second carrier to meet at Rhodes with the copy, so the original and the copy will be in different hands — double protection. Georgios accepts this with visible relief and releases the original.
- **Failure:** Georgios releases a certified copy only. The copy is legally valid but the Prior's session may weight the original more heavily; at Act 5, the secretary who receives it flags it as a copy rather than the original. This doesn't fail the quest but changes Act 5's DC to 14.

**Flavor:** Georgios's office is in the harbor district, two floors above a chandler's shop. The smell of tar and salt comes through the window. He has a ledger open on his desk that he keeps glancing at, as if expecting to find a reason in it to refuse. When the carrier finally gets the document from him, he says: "I signed that. The wound compensation table is in my hand — I copied it from dictation. I remember thinking: these men have considered every loss in advance. Most merchants have not."

### Act 2 — Aegean Sea Crossing (Chios to Rhodes, Day One)
**DC 12 | Athletics or Persuasion**

A Venetian merchant galley is in the same shipping lane — identifiable by its flags. As the carrier's vessel draws alongside at a water-resupply stop at a small island, a Venetian official (a factor named Marco Loredan, recognizable by the Venetian house blazon on his luggage) sees the carrier boarding and asks in a friendly manner what business brings someone from Chios in such obvious haste. He seems amiable. He is the representative of the Venetian merchant who filed the complaint. He knows the session dates.

**Two paths:**
- **Persuasion DC 12:** Pass through without revealing the destination or purpose. The carrier can claim personal business, family matter, anything except the actual errand. Loredan is not hostile but he is watchful. Success: he accepts the misdirection and they depart separately. Failure: he is not fooled; he rides the same sea-lane and arrives at Rhodes the same morning, giving the Venetian consul advance notice that the articles are coming.
- **Athletics DC 12:** Get back to the carrier's vessel before Loredan can organize his men to search the carrier's bag. (He has four men with him and is visibly calculating whether he can get away with a search at a small island with no witnesses.) Success: the carrier is aboard and the vessel underway before he decides to act. Failure: the bag is searched; the articles are found; Loredan copies the wound compensation table for the Venetian consul. The document is not taken, but the Venetians now know exactly what is coming.

**Failure consequence:** At Act 5, the Venetian consul has sent a message to the Prior's secretary challenging the admissibility of the articles on a technicality — they were notarized by a Chios notary under Genoese authority, and the Genoese are a party to the dispute. This adds +2 to Act 5's DC.

**Flavor:** The island is barely an island — a rock with a cistern and two old olive trees. The water is cold and clear and tastes of limestone. Loredan has good boots and watches the carrier with the specific attentiveness of a man who already knows what he doesn't know yet.

### Act 3 — Rhodes Harbor (Harbor Master's Inspection Post)
**DC 12 | Persuasion or History**

The harbor master's post at Rhodes requires all arriving documents and sealed parcels from Aegean ports to be logged by category. The harbor master himself (a Hospitallers brother named Fra Thibault de Montauban) knows the session is in progress. He sees the Chios notary seal on the articles. He is not going to look the other way, but he is also not hostile to the corsairs' case — the Knights themselves operate in the gray area between corsairing and piracy regularly.

His concern: if he logs the articles as "legal correspondence, notary documents," they pass. If he logs them as "piracy-related materials, Venetian complaint pending," they get flagged to the Venetian consul before they reach the Prior.

**Skill check purpose:** Get the articles logged under the category that does not trigger the Venetian consul notification.

- **Success (Persuasion DC 12):** The carrier makes the argument that the articles are a commercial agreement — a company charter, essentially, governing shares and compensation — and should be classified under commercial correspondence. Fra Thibault considers this, checks his ledger, notes that company articles do fall under commercial correspondence per Hospitallers harbor protocol, and logs them accordingly. He gives the carrier a harbor log receipt and says nothing more.
- **Success (History DC 12):** The carrier knows that the Hospitaller regulations distinguish between "evidence in a pending case" (flagged) and "documentary submission for an open session" (logged directly). The articles are being submitted to an open session, not filed as evidence for an adversarial case. This distinction exists in the regulations. Fra Thibault checks the relevant paragraph, nods, and logs them as a session submission.
- **Failure:** Flagged as piracy-related materials. The Venetian consul receives notification within two hours. At Act 4, he has stationed a man at the commandery gate with instructions to challenge the carrier.

**Flavor:** Fra Thibault's log is extraordinary — in forty years of harbor duty he has classified ships' cargoes, prisoners' ransom notes, relics, stolen icons, a sealed letter from the Pope, and at least three documents someone claimed were treaties but were not. He opens the articles, reads the wound compensation table, and reads it again. He says: "These are more carefully drafted than our own regulations for injured brothers." He closes the document and begins writing.

### Act 4 — Rhodes Town (Commandery Gate Approach)
**DC 12 | Persuasion or Stealth**

The session started an hour ago. Grimaldi is inside. The commandery gate is guarded by a Hospitallers brother on duty. At the gate, a Venetian agent (name: Pietro Soranzo, identified by the Venetian coat he wears) is waiting and watching. He has not been told to block the carrier physically — he has been told to delay, distract, and create any pretense for a jurisdictional challenge.

If the harbor log is flagged (Act 3 failure): Soranzo has a written challenge from the consul. He presents it to the gate brother, saying the articles are improperly filed under commercial correspondence and should be held by the harbor master pending the consul's formal challenge. The gate brother is confused.

If not flagged: Soranzo approaches the carrier claiming to be a legal representative of the session and demanding to inspect the document before it is submitted.

**Skill check purpose:** Get through the gate without surrendering the document or accepting a delay.

- **Success (Persuasion DC 12):** The carrier addresses the gate brother directly (not Soranzo), presents the harbor log receipt showing the articles were properly categorized and accepted for session submission, and requests immediate entry for a session document. The brother recognizes this is his jurisdiction, not Soranzo's, and opens the gate. Soranzo shouts a protest; the gate closes.
- **Success (Stealth DC 12):** The commandery has a secondary entrance used by brothers on night patrol — one of the maintenance doors. A Chios dockworker pointed it out last night. The carrier uses it; the gate brother on the main entrance remains occupied with Soranzo.
- **Failure:** Soranzo buys forty minutes of procedural delay — the gate brother is genuinely uncertain about his authority to admit session documents without senior Hospitallers approval. This means the carrier arrives at the session forty minutes after the preliminary ruling has been made on admissibility.

**Flavor:** Soranzo is pleasant. He says: "I only want to verify the document is the one registered. It will take five minutes." He says this while standing between the carrier and the gate. His hand is on the document case.

### Act 5 — The Prior's Session Hall
**DC 12 | Persuasion or Deception**

The session is in progress. Grand Prior Raymond Bérenger is at the head of the table; Grimaldi is presenting the corsairs' defense; the Venetian consul is presenting the complaint. The Prior's secretary — a tightly organized Hospitaller clerk named Fra Denis — controls access to the session record.

The Prior's attention is on Grimaldi. The corsairs' defense has been proceeding without the articles. Grimaldi has been arguing from memory. He needs the document.

The secretary is at a writing desk outside the session door, logging submissions. He needs to examine and log any new submission before it goes to the Prior. The session rules give him this authority. He is not obstructionist; he is procedural.

- **If Act 1 succeeded (original document):** Persuasion DC 12. Fra Denis examines the document — all fifteen signatures, the notary seal, the wound compensation table in the notary's hand — and logs it immediately as an original certified submission. He opens the session door and passes it to an assistant who carries it to Grimaldi's table. The Prior pauses to read.
- **If Act 1 failed (certified copy only):** Persuasion DC 14. Fra Denis notes it is a copy and flags it for a verification step. Grimaldi must argue (with the copy in his hands) that it is equivalent. Possible but harder.
- **If Act 3 failed (harbor log flagged) and Act 2 failed (Loredan copied the table):** DC 16. The Venetian consul has a prepared objection to admissibility. The carrier must argue the chain of custody and proper harbor classification before Fra Denis will log it.
- **If Act 4 failed (forty-minute delay):** The Prior has already issued a preliminary finding of "piracy, no evidence of organized commission presented." The articles, when they arrive, reopen the finding — but the preliminary stands in the record.

**onPass flavor trigger:** The Prior reads the wound compensation table. He says, more to the session than to anyone: "Six hundred pieces of eight for the right arm. These men agreed this in advance, in writing, with witnesses. They bound themselves." He sets the document on the table. "The Venetians have not submitted evidence that the seizure was conducted without a commission. The articles demonstrate a constituted company. The commission question remains open. But this is not a mob." He looks at the Venetian consul. "You will need more than an absence."

**onFail flavor trigger:** The articles arrive during the second day. Fra Denis logs them under "Late submissions, session record." The preliminary finding of piracy is on record. The articles reopen the case; the corsair captain is released from detention; the full hearing proceeds — but the preliminary finding is still visible in the register when the Venetian consul's lawyer looks it up six months later.

Grimaldi writes to the carrier's employer: "The wound table did it. The Prior read it twice." Outside the commandery, Soranzo folds his letter of challenge and walks back to the Venetian consulate.

### onFail

The articles arrive late. The preliminary finding is in the record. The corsair captain is eventually freed — the full hearing, conducted a month later with all evidence present, resolves in his favor — but the preliminary finding was seen by the Venetian house's archive. They flag it in their records as "piracy, preliminary finding, reversed on late evidence." The next time a ship from that company requests harbor access in a Venetian-controlled port, the harbor master pulls the record.

## Seeds HAV-02 through HAV-06

### HAV-02 — "The Sunk Boat"
**Source hook:** Pierre le Grand, before boarding the Spanish vice-admiral, ordered the surgeon to bore a hole in their own vessel. It would sink in minutes. No retreat. The attack succeeded; Pierre kept the ship and sailed to France, never returning to piracy.

**1367 commission:** A Genoese corsair captain operating out of Caffa (Crimea) has used his letter of marque against a Mongol-tributary merchant ship; the commission document, once presented at a seizure, becomes the irreversible claim — it cannot be withdrawn, because the act of using it has been witnessed. His representative in Caffa is sending the commission's usage record (the formal attestation that the letter was invoked) to the Trebizond Genoese factor for registration. Once registered, the corsair cannot be prosecuted by the Genoese commune for the seizure — but if the registration is blocked, the commission is voided retroactively.

**Route:** CAF → TRB (Caffa → Trebizond)
**TOKEN:** A sealed wax-covered cylinder containing the commission usage attestation — a single sheet in Genoese chancery hand, stamped with the captain's mark. Heavy. Cold from being carried in a boat overnight.
**Theme:** The decision that cannot be walked back creates a different quality of action than any decision that leaves retreat open; the document that makes retreat impossible is the most honest commission, because both parties know exactly what has been agreed and neither can pretend otherwise

### HAV-03 — "The Surgeon's Log"
**Source hook:** Exquemelin served as barber-surgeon and eyewitness. His wound register is the clearest accounting of any expedition's real cost — not the glory, but exactly what was paid in blood, tabulated against the compensation schedule in the articles.

**1367 commission:** A Byzantine physician (Nikephoros Melissenos) served on a Genoese corsair vessel out of Chios for two seasons as ship's surgeon. He kept a meticulous wound log — date, wound type, treatment, outcome, compensation claimed under the articles. He has returned to Rhodes and is sending the log to a medical school in Constantinople as evidence of wound types and treatment methods under maritime conditions. It is not secret; it is simply a medical record. But one of the wounded men listed in it is now a Knight of St. John, and he has not told his order about his corsair service.

**Route:** RHD → CON (Rhodes → Constantinople)
**TOKEN:** A small vellum-bound log, physician's hand throughout, compact and water-stained at the corners. Each entry: date, wound description, treatment, prognosis. Names are initials only. The last entry is the physician's own: a rope-burn across both palms from a boarding operation.
**Theme:** The healer in the company of killers holds the most complete record of the expedition's actual cost; the wound register accounts what no victory narrative includes; all the real arithmetic is in it, and none of the names are glorious

### HAV-04 — "The Dead Man's Bluff"
**Source hook:** Lolonois survived the Spanish attack at Campechy by smearing himself with sand and the blood of his wounds and lying among the dead until the Spanish search parties moved on. Then he walked away. The appearance of death is the most effective passport in a searched field.

**1367 commission:** A Venetian factor in Famagusta (Cyprus) holds a commission document from a defunct privateering company — the company was formally dissolved five years ago, all its letters of marque supposedly canceled. But the dissolution decree has a legal flaw: it cancels the company's future operations but does not formally void the specific acts already performed under the old commissions. One act — a seizure from 1361 — is now the subject of a new legal claim by the seized party's heirs. The factor is carrying the old commission document (formally "dead," but never properly nullified for past acts) to a Venetian jurist in Venice who specializes in exactly this question. The document that appears dead is still the key evidence.

**Route:** FAM → VEN (Famagusta → Venice)
**TOKEN:** An old parchment commission, formerly sealed (seal broken, wax fragments still attached at the fold), carrying the dissolved company's mark. Age-browned. The dissolution stamp is marked across the face in red ink, but the original text and the company's authorized signature are fully legible.
**Theme:** The appearance of cancellation is not the same as actual nullification; the document that has been declared void still carries its original authority for acts already performed under it; the dead letter lives for every act it once authorized

### HAV-05 — "The Admiral's Letter"
**Source hook:** The formal exchange between Morgan and Don Alonso del Campo y Espinosa — two parties conducting full diplomatic correspondence while in a state of armed readiness; the pirate demanding ransom in the language of treaty; the admiral offering terms in the language of military command. Each letter acknowledges the other party as a competent correspondent, which is itself a form of recognition.

**1367 commission:** A Catalan corsair captain operating in the Aegean has sent a formal ransom demand to a Rhodian merchant, listing the seized cargo, the compensation required for its return, and the deadline. The merchant has drafted a counter-offer. The exchange — the original demand and the counter-offer — must be delivered through an authorized intermediary (a Genoese notary in Famagusta) before the deadline expires, or the corsair will sell the cargo at Paphos. The carrier is the intermediary.

**Route:** FAM → RHD (Famagusta → Rhodes)
**TOKEN:** A leather document case with two letters inside: the corsair's demand (signed with his name and vessel mark) and the merchant's counter-offer (sealed with the merchant house seal). Both are in Catalan/Italian trade register, formal tone throughout.
**Theme:** The formal exchange of demands between two parties who recognize each other's authority even while contesting each other's right — the letter of ultimatum is also a form of recognition; the corsair who writes in the language of treaty has, in that letter, claimed a status that the letter itself creates

### HAV-06 — "The Eyewitness in the Middle"
**Source hook:** Exquemelin was present at every operation he describes, but as barber-surgeon — not in command, not a common mariner. His account has full access and no complicity in the decisions. He saw what he had to treat, not what he helped decide.

**1367 commission:** A Greek physician (Demetrios Agallias) served as barber-surgeon on a Rhodian corsair vessel for three seasons, 1364–1366. He is now in Rhodes, retired from sea service, and has been called to testify before the Hospitallers' judicial committee reviewing the previous decade of corsair operations. His account — kept as a medical journal throughout, not written as testimony — is being carried to the Weimar archive as the only non-interested firsthand account of the operations. He is the person nobody was trying to impress and nobody remembered to silence.

**Route:** RHD → WM (Rhodes → Weimar archive)
**TOKEN:** A physician's journal, three volumes bound together: dates, case notes, treatments, prognoses, and — in the margins of the third volume — observations about what was happening on the ship during the operations he treated. Not written as evidence. Written to remember.
**Theme:** The witness who was present but not responsible sees a different version of events than either the victor or the victim; the barber-surgeon's account — what he had to treat, not what he helped decide — is the only form of testimony that cannot be dismissed as advocacy, because he had nothing to gain from the outcome
