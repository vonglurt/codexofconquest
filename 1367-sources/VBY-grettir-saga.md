## Cycle 1 — Pass 1: Full Telling

### Historical and Literary Context

Grettir's Saga (*Grettis saga Ásmundarsonar*) was composed in Iceland around the early
fourteenth century — late among the family sagas, which gives it unusual richness. It
had time to absorb the conventions of the form and use them knowingly, with an
authorial intelligence that is sometimes ironic and always precise. The source material
is much older: the figure of Grettir circulated as legend through the tenth and
eleventh centuries, and the saga weaves those threads into a historically plausible
frame while keeping its supernatural elements fully intact.

The most persistent scholarly note about the saga is the structural parallel between
its Glámr episode and Sandhaugar episode and the Grendel sequence in *Beowulf* — a
warrior fighting a revenant at night, then descending under water into a cave to fight
a monster and recover the bones of the dead. Whether this reflects direct influence,
shared Germanic source material, or independent parallel is still debated. What is not
debated is that both works are thinking about the same problem: what does strength cost
the person who has it?

In 1367 AD, when our game is set, the saga is nearly contemporary — composed perhaps
fifty years before, and circulating actively in Icelandic manuscript culture. Grettir
is not ancient legend to a 1367 audience; he is a recent story, known and argued over.

### The Story in Full

The saga opens with Grettir's ancestry. His grandfather Önund Tree-foot lost a leg at
the Battle of Hafrsfjord but continued to raid, trade, and endure. The wooden leg
became the family's symbol — not shame but stubbornness. His father Ásmund is a
respectable farmer at Bjarg. Grettir as a boy is difficult in every direction: lazy at
farm tasks, sharp-tongued with everyone, physically formidable even at nine or ten.
He and his father do not get on. The pattern is set early: the world will never find
a shape that fits him.

His first serious trouble comes at a horse-fight. A man named Skeggi insults him. Grettir
kills him. He is sentenced to three years' lesser outlawry and sent abroad to Norway.
In Norway he proves himself in various ways — winning a verse-battle, fighting in a
jarl's service, demonstrating his famous strength in public tests. He also opens an
ancient burial mound, fights the animated dead within the barrow-dark, and takes a
short-sword called Jökulsnaut from the grave-goods. Jökulsnaut will be his weapon for
the rest of his life. The barrow fight is his first encounter with the animated dead,
and he wins it — but the experience leaves something behind, a first shadow of unease,
a knowledge that not everything that moves in the dark is alive in the way he expects.

He returns to Iceland and the pattern repeats. A sequence of incidents — a ship fire
in which men he didn't mean to kill die, the killing of Thorir's sons in a quarrel —
pushes his outlawry further. He receives full outlawry (*skóggangr*): wolf's head, out
of law completely. Any man may kill him without legal penalty and claim the bounty.
He cannot come within a certain distance of any settled farm; he cannot receive
shelter; he cannot buy or sell. He has been made a ghost by law.

He is twenty years old.

**The Glámr episode** is the pivot of the whole saga. Glámr is a Swedish man hired as
a shepherd at the farm of Thorhallsstadir in Vatnsdale. He is not fully Christian —
the saga notes this with significance. He is killed on Christmas Eve by a ghost that
has been troubling the valley. After his death, he becomes the most powerful revenant
in Iceland. He rides the rooftops of Thorhallsstadir, crushing the ridgepoles, driving
the household out room by room. He kills farmhands. The farm empties. The farmer
Thorhall sends word across Iceland. Men come to deal with Glámr and leave quickly or
don't leave at all.

Grettir accepts the challenge.

He spends one night at the farm. Glámr comes when the fire has burned low — enormous,
grey-faced, his eyes full of a strange light. The fight moves through the hall,
shatters the furniture, goes out through the door into the yard. Grettir finally gets
on top of him and draws his short-sword. But at this moment Glámr's eyes open wide and
fill with light, and Grettir cannot move. The saga says only that his strength left him.
He sat on the dead man's chest and could not draw the sword.

Glámr speaks his curse in three parts.

*You shall become full outlaw and live always in the open.* You will have no house.

*You shall always be alone.* Even when others are with you, the solitude will be in you.

*And those eyes — you will always see them.* Every dark place. Every night. My eyes
in every shadow. You will never pass through the dark again without seeing what you
saw here.

Then the strength comes back. Grettir cuts off his head. The body is burned. The farm
is cleansed. He receives no reward that helps him: the curse is already in him.

The rest of the saga follows his outlawry across Iceland over the next twenty years.
He roams the Arnarvatn heath and the highlands, avoiding farms, living rough. Men come
for the bounty and he defeats them all. He is the strongest man in Iceland and has
nowhere to sleep.

He performs an extraordinary deed at Sandhaugar: a farm has lost two men to something
that takes them in the night. Grettir investigates. He dives into the river, swims
upstream under a great waterfall, finds a cave. Inside: a troll-giant and a giantess,
and the bones of the missing men. He kills both creatures. He gathers the bones in a
bag. He swims out through the waterfall pool and ties a rope to the bag so the local
priest, Steinn, can pull it up. Steinn hauls up the bag of bones. Grettir climbs the
rope alone. The farm will no longer lose men. Grettir receives acknowledgment and
nothing that changes his situation.

In his last years, Grettir takes the island of Drangey in Skagafjörð with his younger
half-brother Illugi and a thrall named Glaum. Drangey is nearly vertical cliff on
every side, accessible only through one crack in the rock face. One man can defend it
against many. The farmers of the district jointly own Drangey and use it for grazing;
Grettir's presence is an insult and a loss, and they cannot remove him.

He has no fire. The island is cold and bare. The winter comes.

**The fire-fetch.** Grettir swims to the mainland farm of Reykjaness — miles through
winter water, in the dark, alone. He arrives half-frozen, his hands barely functional,
and asks for fire. He is given it or takes it. He seals a live coal in a clay pot and
swims back with it raised above the surface, his arm above the water the whole
distance. He makes it back. The fire burns on Drangey.

Year after year passes. The district farmers try and fail to dislodge the brothers.
Thorbjörn Öngull, a farmer from the area, cannot beat Grettir in open combat — no
man can — and enlists his foster-mother Thurid, an old woman with knowledge of
sorcery.

Thurid goes to the shore alone. She finds a great driftwood log. She carves runes on
the root-end of it, colors them with her own blood, and speaks words over them. She
walks backwards around the log three times. She pushes it out to sea.

The log washes up on Drangey's beach. Grettir's thrall Glaum tries to bring it in for
firewood. Grettir feels something wrong about it and kicks it away. It washes back
again. He kicks it away a second time. The third time, he chops at it with his axe —
and the axe glances off the rune-carved wood and cuts deep into his own thigh. The
wound festers. In the tenth century, in winter, on an island, this is a death sentence.
Within days Grettir is bedridden and feverish. The wound is poisoned in a way no herb
will address.

When Thorbjörn Öngull hears that Grettir is ill, he gathers fourteen men and sails to
Drangey. The thrall Glaum — bribed, or broken — lowers the ladder. Thorbjörn's men
climb the cliff.

They find a man who can barely stand, burning with fever, propped against the wall with
a sword. Even in this state, even poisoned and weakened, he kills two or three of them
before they overwhelm him. Illugi fights at his side, steady and wordless, until the
numbers tell. Both brothers are killed.

Thorbjörn cuts off Grettir's head to prove the kill and claim the bounty. He is
twenty years old in the outlawry, perhaps forty in total. He died of a dead woman's
sorcery, not a living man's sword.

The saga does not end here. Grettir's brother Thorstein Dromund, hearing of the death,
goes to Constantinople — Miklagarðr, the Norse name for the great city — and joins
the Varangian Guard, the Norse mercenary regiment in Byzantine service. In
Constantinople he meets a married woman named Spes, who is clever, brave, and
ill-matched to her husband. He falls in love with her. He also avenges Grettir: he
kills Thorbjörn Öngull, who had come east and been declared outlaw for the
dishonorable manner of Grettir's killing. Thorstein and Spes escape together, travel
to Norway, and eventually make pilgrimage to Rome, where they die as penitents.

This ending is unusual and late-feeling. Scholars suspect the Rome pilgrimage was
added by the author as a Christian correction — a final peace after all the grief. The
saga refuses to let the last word be waste.

### Themes and Morals

The saga argues that the world is designed to destroy its best people, and that this is
not a tragedy of evil men but of structure. The law cannot accommodate the exceptional.
Grettir's crimes are real but minor; his exile is enormous and final. The gap between
what he does — protects farms, kills monsters, clears hauntings, swims impossible
distances — and what he receives — no shelter, no pardon, no end to the bounty — is
the saga's recurring wound. He is most useful to society while outlaw and would be
most threatening if integrated, and so society keeps him outside.

The Glámr curse adds a supernatural dimension to this structural injustice. It is not
only law that destroys Grettir but a dead man's malice, delivered through the eyes.
Even if the law were lifted, the curse would remain.

Illugi's presence is the saga's one pure, uncomplicated thing. He knows what Drangey
means. He goes anyway. He does not make speeches about it. He is simply there.

### Chapter Notes — Episode by Episode

**Ancestry chapters (Önund Tree-foot and Ásmund)** — The wooden leg that becomes a
family symbol: endurance after loss, not the loss itself. The saga's argument stated
in miniature before the protagonist is born.

**The horse-fight and Skeggi's death** — Grettir kills a man who insults him. The
killing is disproportionate but comprehensible. First outlawry. Pattern established:
he cannot absorb a slight.

**Norway — the jarl's service and the barrow-opening** — He proves himself abroad,
opens a burial mound, takes Jökulsnaut, and fights his first animated dead. First
contact with the supernatural combat that will define his life. He wins, but something
shifts.

**Return to Iceland — the ship fire and full outlawry** — A sequence of accidents and
pride-fueled violence extends his sentence to full outlawry (*skóggangr*). He is a
ghost in law from here forward.

**The Glámr episode** — The saga's structural pivot. Grettir clears the haunting and
earns the curse. The three-part curse maps the rest of the narrative: solitude, fear
of darkness, death in weakness. He wins the fight. Winning is what ruins him.

**The Arnarvatn years** — Years on the heath, alone. Men come for the bounty; he
defeats them. The repetition is deliberate — it shows both endurance and the narrowing
of his world.

**The Sandhaugar cave** — Grettir dives under a waterfall, kills a troll and giantess,
recovers the bones of the dead. He ties a rope; the priest hauls up the bag. No reward
follows that lifts the outlawry. The deeds accumulate. The law does not move.

**The fire-fetch** — Grettir swims miles in winter sea to fetch an ember for Drangey.
The saga's fullest image of his condition: the strongest man in Iceland, one arm above
the freezing water, carrying warmth for his brother.

**Drangey years** — The brothers hold the island. The district tries and fails to
dislodge them. Time passes. The cliff holds.

**Thurid's sorcery and the bewitched log** — The log arrives three times. Grettir's
instinct warns him but cannot stop him. The axe glances and the wound opens. From here
the saga accelerates to its end.

**The final assault** — Glaum lowers the ladder. A small betrayal, not a dramatic
one — just a thrall's weakness. Illugi's last fight is the moral climax: pure loyalty,
pure waste.

**Thorstein Dromund in Miklagarðr and Rome** — A different saga in miniature: court
intrigue, clever woman, foreign revenge, and a good death in Rome. The saga gives
Thorstein what it denied Grettir.

## Cycle 1 — Pass 2: Characters

**Grettir Ásmundarson** — He is not a hero in the mode of Sigurd or Beowulf: he has
no destiny, no divine role, no magical lineage. He is simply the strongest man alive
and cannot be ordinary, and the world has no use for that. His poetry is essential to
understanding him: throughout the saga he composes *lausavísur* — short formal stanzas
that distill his exact state of mind. The verses are often wry, self-aware, darkly
funny. He knows what his situation is and does not deceive himself about it. This
makes his endurance more painful than ignorant endurance would be: he suffers with full
knowledge of the injustice, and continues anyway. His tongue is as sharp as his axe.
He cannot stop himself from making enemies of men who might have helped him. He is
not tragic in the Greek sense of someone who doesn't see; he is tragic in the rarer
sense of someone who sees completely and cannot change.

**Glámr** — Not a living villain but the saga's structural antagonist, and dead for
most of the narrative. He is a Swedish man with imperfect Christianity and a surly
disposition who dies because of a ghost and becomes a more powerful ghost himself —
this is the saga's irony in miniature: the supernatural creates the supernatural. His
power lies in his eyes. The detail is precise and deliberate: when Glámr's eyes open
in the moonlight, the light in them is not hatred or malice but something older. What
he passes to Grettir in that look is not evil but fate. The curse's three parts are
perfectly calibrated to their recipient: they do not destroy Grettir's strength, which
cannot be destroyed. They destroy its usefulness.

**Illugi Ásmundarson** — Grettir's younger half-brother. He appears late in the saga
and his role is simple and absolute: he comes to Drangey when Grettir cannot ask anyone
to come, knowing what Drangey means, and stays until the end. He does not make speeches
about loyalty. He has perhaps one or two lines of dialogue in the Drangey section. He
fights at Grettir's side on the day of the assault and is killed there. The saga treats
this as sufficient description of a good man: he was there; he stayed; he died for it.

**Thurid the witch** — Fascinating because she is not supernatural herself. She is an
old woman who knows how to use sorcery — a craftsperson of harm. Her motivation is not
malice but practicality: she is hired, or she owes something. The log-curse is
meticulous and skilled, performed with precision (the backwards walk, the blood, the
runes, the timing). She is the most dangerous person in the saga because she can do
what no man with a sword can: she can hurt Grettir through an object before he has
decided to touch it. The curse is not aimed at his strength. It is aimed at his
judgment — the one moment he ignores his own warning.

**Thorstein Dromund** — The avenger and the saga's surprise. He gets everything his
brother didn't: a love story, a cleverness-equal in Spes, a successful revenge, and a
good death in Rome with his hand in another's hand. The Varangian Guard section is
warmer and more romantic than everything that preceded it — almost a separate saga
compressed into a few chapters. The author uses him to provide the closure the saga's
logic will not allow Grettir.

**Jökulsnaut** — Not a character but worth noting as an object-character: the
short-sword taken from the barrow mound in Norway, carried for the whole saga, and
present in Grettir's hand at his death. It is one of the few things he can claim for
himself. The barrow it came from was the home of animated dead; the sword is itself
from the world Grettir most inhabits — between the living and whatever moves in the dark.

## Cycle 1 — Pass 3: The Glámr Fight — Full Scene

The night Grettir waits for Glámr, the fire has burned to coals. The other farmhands
refused to stay in the hall. The farmer's wife set food out and left. Grettir lies on
a bench near the door, his cloak over him, Jökulsnaut at his side, and listens.

The first sound is on the roof. The revenant comes heavy on the ridgepole — the timber
creaks, the whole roof structure groaning under something very large and very deliberate.
It moves along the ridge from gable to gable and stops over the hall door. Then it
comes down.

The door opens inward. Glámr has to duck to enter. The fire is nearly out. In the
near-dark, Grettir sees him: enormous, grey-black faced, the face of a man who has been
dead for months. His eyes are open. They have a light in them that is not fire.

Glámr crosses the hall. He does not speak. He goes to the benches and begins to feel
along them with his hands, the way you look for something you dropped in the dark.
He finds Grettir's feet. He pulls.

Grettir braces against the bench-beam and pulls back. The cloak tears — a great rip
down the middle. Grettir lets it go and catches Glámr by the arms instead. Now they
are upright and wrestling, and the sound of it is only impact: bodies against posts,
a pillar going over, the long table shattering when they go through it. Neither of
them speaks. The farmhands outside hear the destruction and do not move.

Grettir's plan — if he had one — was to stay inside the hall where the confined space
might limit the revenant's leverage. But Glámr is stronger than any man alive in the
narrow hall or the open air, and he is dragging Grettir toward the door. Grettir
understands: he will lose this if they stay inside. He reverses, throws himself
backward, and uses Glámr's momentum to take them both out through the door frame at
speed.

They are in the yard. The moon is behind a cloud.

They wrestle across the frost-hard ground. Grettir is thinking: he must get on top.
He trips Glámr over something — a log, a stone — and lands on him, both hands at
Glámr's throat.

He draws Jökulsnaut.

The moon comes from behind the cloud.

Glámr's eyes open. Wide. The light in them is cold and enormous and it fills the whole
of Grettir's field of vision. The saga does not describe what he sees in it. It says
only that his strength left him. The sword did not rise. He sat on the dead man's
chest and could not make the stroke.

Glámr speaks. The voice is enormous and unhurried. He is dying but he has enough left.

*"You shall become full outlaw,"* he says, *"and you shall always live in the open air."*
You will have no house.

*"You shall always be alone."* Even when others are present. The solitude is a condition
now, not a circumstance.

*"And those eyes of mine — you will always see them."* Every dark place. Every night you
enter a building and the fire is low. My face in every shadow that moves.

The strength comes back. Grettir cuts off his head. He builds a fire and burns the
body. The farm is cleansed.

He rides away in the morning. He knows what is coming. He has known since the moment
the light filled the dead man's face and his sword arm would not rise. He did not lose
the fight. He won.

Winning was the thing that ruined him.

---

The detail the saga lingers on — the eyes — is worth dwelling on. The saga is not
normally a lingering text; it moves at the brisk pace of its genre, dispatching events
in two or three sentences. The Glámr scene is different. The fight itself is described
at unusual length. The moment of the eyes is described twice: once as it happens, once
as Grettir remembers it later and cannot explain it. What he saw in Glámr's eyes is
never named. The saga trusts the reader to supply the content from the shape of the
gap.

What we can say: it was not evil. Evil would have made Grettir afraid. What it made
him was *still*. He looked into Glámr's dying eyes and saw something that removed the
possibility of action — not by terrorizing him but by showing him something that was
simply true. The curse is not a punishment. It is a description of what was already
going to happen to this man. Glámr reads the future in Grettir's face and speaks it
aloud, and Grettir's body recognizes it before his mind can refuse it.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

**1. The Barrow-Night** — Grettir descends into a burial mound in Norway to take
the treasure from the grave. The Fighter carries the only torch through the dark
passages while Grettir fights the animated dead — without the light, the sword arm
cannot find its target.

**2. The Glámr Vigil** — The night before Grettir faces the revenant, the last
remaining farmhand at Thorhallsstadir is preparing to flee. A single witness who
stays until morning changes the legal record of the kill. The Fighter must find an
argument that holds the man to the farm until dawn.

**3. The Fire-Fetch** — Grettir has swum miles through winter sea to Reykjaness shore
and arrived half-frozen, his hands too numb to carry the fire-pot reliably past the
patrol zone. The Fighter carries the sealed coal through farmyard, road-gate, and
cliff-path to the water's edge, where Grettir re-enters the sea with it raised above
his head.

**4. Sandhaugar's Bones** — After Grettir kills the cave-troll and the giantess, the
Fighter must carry a linen bag of recovered bones — the dead men's remains — from the
waterfall pool to the church for burial, through a village that knows what the bones
represent but isn't sure it trusts the outlaw's story.

**5. The Rope-Holder** — At Sandhaugar, the priest Steinn holds the rope so Grettir
can climb out of the waterfall pool. Someone on the cliff-top has cut the rope halfway
through. The Fighter must find the damage and splice it before Grettir surfaces and
puts his weight on it.

**6. Illugi's Crossing** — Before Illugi joins Grettir on Drangey, he must cross the
water without being seen by the district farmers who watch the shore by rotation. The
Fighter escorts him to the crossing point, holds off the watchers at the clifftop, and
signals when the approach is clear.

**7. The Head and the Bounty** — After Grettir's death, his head is taken by Thorbjörn
Öngull as proof of the kill. But the bounty is contested: Thorbjörn used sorcery, which
is itself unlawful. The Fighter carries the legal testimony of one witness — a sealed
letter naming the circumstances of the killing — from Iceland to the Althing, where
the bounty award will be argued.

## Theme Check

Existing themes in quest-map.md (all 12, after FRO):

- Grief carried in an object (INV, BHD, GDN)
- Honor vs. shame (MAD, GDN, BHD)
- Love and fate intertwined (MSE, INV, BHD)
- Truth delivered posthumously (LGW)
- Healing carried through hostile law (MAN)
- The carrier becomes the hunted (SEN)
- The token's journey (ALL)
- Ancient witnesses and the relay of questions (KIR)
- Corruption wears the face of mercy; the genuine article has no price (FCO)
- Fate moves through the broken thing; what carries a future must first appear dead (FRO)

**Themes I am avoiding:**

"Loyalty under the long watch" (LHR/STN/TBS) might seem relevant — Illugi's vigil on
Drangey — but those quests are about loyalty to a duty or a person held by someone
else. This quest is about the outlaw's acts being structurally invisible to the law
that could redeem him.

"The carrier becomes the hunted" (SEN) is the closest risk — in the fire-fetch, the
fighter carrying a fire-pot through a patrol zone could become a target. But SEN is
about possession marking the bearer as prey; this quest is about something larger:
the entire context of carrying fire for an outlaw is already outside the law.

**New theme introduced by VBY:**
*"The greatest man alive has no house: heroism that earns protection for all and
shelter for none"* — Grettir is the strongest man in Iceland. He clears hauntings,
kills monsters, saves farms, swims impossible distances. None of it earns him pardon.
The law has no mechanism to credit deeds performed outside it. To carry fire for
Grettir is to do the same thing Grettir does — an act of care that the law cannot
acknowledge.

This is distinct from all existing themes. It is not about the object, not about the
courier being targeted, not about institutional corruption. It is about heroism as a
category that can be rendered legally invisible.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: "The Fire-Fetch"

**The Token · A sealed clay pot containing a live coal, packed in undyed wool,
beeswax-stopped · It weighs almost nothing · It is the only warmth on Drangey ·
It is everything**

---

### Act I — The Asking
*The Sealed Fire-Pot · unlit wool wrapping · in Aldis's hands · the farmhouse at
Reykjaness shore, pre-dawn*

A farmhouse at the sea's edge. Salt through the door-gaps. The fire is low. The woman
— Aldis, a widow, not unkind — stands at the hearth and watches the door.

It opens.

Grettir Ásmundarson fills the frame. He is soaked, his lips pale, his hands at his
sides and shaking. He cannot grip a knife. He cannot carry a sealed pot through a
patrol zone. He asks for fire.

The woman looks past him. She looks at you.

*"One witness,"* she says quietly, meaning: I did not do this alone.

She reaches into the hearth and sets a coal in the clay pot. She presses the beeswax
seal closed. She holds the pot out — not to him. To you. Her eyes say what she cannot:
*take it before I remember the law.*

Grettir's hands close on air. He nods once. He steps back into the dark.

*You carry it. You are the only reason this moment is possible.*

---

### Act II — The Byre-Men
*The Sealed Fire-Pot · warm through the wool · in the fighter's hands · the farmyard,
two men in the byre doorway*

The yard. Frost on everything. Two farmhands in the byre's entrance — men who
couldn't sleep, or who were watching. One of them sees the wool bundle. His eyes move
to the wet footprints that cross the yard toward the road. The other says nothing. He
is looking at the dark where Grettir disappeared.

Slowly: *"That's a fire-pot."*

The road is burning away behind you. Dawn is not far off. The patrol changes at first
light.

*The pot is warm against your chest. It does not care about the lie. It only needs
to reach the shore.*

---

### Act III — The Road-Gate
*The Sealed Fire-Pot · still warm · under the fighter's cloak · the coastal road,
a rope gate, four mounted men, one torch*

The patrol has strung a rope across the cliff road. Four men. One torch. They are
looking for someone specific; they know the swimmer came ashore somewhere tonight.
The captain's eyes move from your face to the shape under your cloak.

You answer his questions. You name a destination. You name what you are carrying —
a different answer.

He looks at you for a long time.

In the road's shadow, fifty yards back, the shape that is Grettir does not move. He
told you: *do not look toward me.*

You do not look toward him.

One of the riders circles behind you. He has seen the silhouette. He draws.

---

### Act IV — The Shore Approach
*The Sealed Fire-Pot · intact, beeswax seal unbroken · in the fighter's hands ·
the cliff path descending to the shore, dark and narrow*

The path down the cliff face is steep and angled. Grettir said: not the main path.
Take the goat path, the one with the loose stone at the second curve.

At the second curve there is a rope, strung at chest height across the path. And
below the rope, a large loose stone positioned to roll. Someone has been here before
you. They expect the swimmer's ally to use this path. They set this for you.

The shore is thirty feet below. Grettir is somewhere in the dark at the water's edge.
There is not enough light to go back.

*The pot is warm against your ribs. The rope is thin. The stone is exactly placed.*

---

### Act V — The Crossing
*The Sealed Fire-Pot · in the fighter's outstretched hands · at the water's edge ·
the shore below the cliff, dawn breaking*

He is there. Knee-deep, turned toward you. His palms are still pale from the first
swim, shaking slightly — not with fear, with cold that has never fully left. He holds
out both hands.

You walk to the water's edge.

He takes the pot. Wraps both hands around the wool. One breath — just the warmth.
His eyes come up to yours. They are winter-sea color.

*"One witness is enough,"* he says. Not repeating Aldis. Meaning something different:
*you being here is sufficient; you don't need to follow me into the sea.*

He turns. He wades out. His arm rises above the surface — the pot held above the
water — and he swims.

On the cliff-edge of Drangey, a silhouette watches. A strip of wool knotted around
a driftwood stake flickers in the sea-wind.

Above the shore, a patrol rider crests the road and looks down.

*You stand at the water with nothing in your hands. A fisherman at dawn. Nothing
to see.*

He rides on.

On the water, a hand holds fire above the surface. The distance to Drangey shortens.

## Cycle 2: The Barrow-Night

### Pass 1 — Plot Précis (The Barrow-Night)

On the Norwegian coast, Grettir hears of a barrow-mound said to hold treasure and an animated dead man who defends it. He breaks the mound open and descends on a rope. Inside: darkness, old wood, old bones, and a barrow-wight — the dead man still sitting in his chair, unwilling to be disturbed. Grettir fights him in the dark, kills him, takes the treasure, climbs out. This happens when Grettir is young, still making his name, before the outlawry defines him. He goes in because the darkness is a challenge and the treasure is there. He needs exactly one thing from another person: light.

### Pass 2 — Fighter's Role (The Barrow-Night)

Grettir gives the Fighter a torch — sealed oil-lamp, to last the job — and tells them to hold it. He says this with the manner of a man who has considered what he needs and determined that this is all of it. The Fighter's commission: keep the torch up and lit through five acts of underground combat, collapsing barrow-wood, and one moment when the wight reaches for the light directly. In Act V, they exit the barrow and the Fighter extinguishes the torch at the threshold — the signal that the darkness has been navigated and the work is done.

### Pass 3 — What This Angle Reveals That Cycle 1 Missed

Cycle 1 was Grettir's heroism as sacrifice — the outlaw who swims winter sea to bring fire to others, earning protection for all and shelter for none. *The Barrow-Night* reveals Grettir's heroism as appetite. This is an earlier Grettir, before the curse and the outlawry define him. He goes into the barrow because it's there — because the darkness is the obstacle and the treasure is the proof. He is not helping anyone. He is proving what he is. What cycle 1 missed: the young Grettir, who takes darkness as a challenge rather than as his permanent condition. Cycle 1 was about what his heroism costs him and others. Cycle 2 is about what his heroism is made of. The Fighter's role: to be the light he needs and would not admit to needing, because a man who goes into barrows alone at night for sport doesn't have a name for requiring company — but requiring someone to hold the light is still requiring someone.

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Barrow-Night*

*Storyline 1 — Torch-Carrier. Grettir descends into a Norwegian burial mound to take the treasure and fight the dead. TOKEN: the Torch — sealed oil-lamp given by Grettir in Act I, held through Acts II–IV as the enabling condition of every blow he lands, extinguished at the barrow threshold Act V.*

**THE TOKEN: THE TORCH** — a sealed oil-lamp with a wick Grettir has cut to last an estimated two hours. He gives it to the Fighter at the barrow entrance and says: "Hold this." That is the full briefing. In Act V, at the barrow's threshold, the Fighter extinguishes it — putting out the light they carried through the dark is the closing gesture, the sign that they have come back to where light already exists.

---

#### ACT I — *The Barrow Entrance*
*The torch: in Grettir's hands, not yet the Fighter's. Location: BWH — burial mound, Norwegian coast, night.*

The mound is a grass-covered hill above the coastal headland. The stone entrance-post is half-buried in heather. Someone cut the entrance seal — old wax, old rope — a long time ago and then thought better of going in. The seal has been re-tied, badly, and the re-tying has the look of someone who needed to prove they were not afraid and was in fact afraid.

Grettir stands at the entrance examining the rope with the look of a man who has already done his accounting and found it acceptable.

He turns to the Fighter and holds out the lamp.

**Grettir:** "Hold this."

He does not explain what for. He begins unwinding the rope from the entrance-post.

*Courage check DC 12: overcome the specific quality of fear that belongs to a barrow entrance at night — not combat fear, which is familiar and manageable; the older fear, the one that belongs to the dark below a stone threshold that was cut for the dead.*

**Failure:** You hesitate at the threshold. Grettir is already inside by the time you move — his feet disappearing into the darkness ahead of you, waiting for the light you are holding, which is not yet underground. The first act has cost three seconds of him waiting in the dark below the threshold. He does not say anything about it.

**Success:** You take the lamp and step over the threshold stone without breaking pace. Grettir looks at you once. He nods in the manner of someone revising an estimate upward. You receive the Torch and descend into the barrow.

---

#### ACT II — *The Chamber*
*The torch: underground, held by the Fighter. Location: BWH.*

The first chamber is narrow and low. Old wood-framing along the walls, dry and dark. The smell of stone and something older than stone. At the far end, in a carved wooden chair, sits the dead man — the barrow-wight — in the shape he had when he was buried, with the additions that come from a long time in the dark.

He moves when the light reaches him.

Grettir moves to meet him. The problem immediately becomes clear: the space is too narrow for both of them to move freely, and Grettir's sword-arm needs a particular angle that puts the Fighter's torch-arm directly in the path of the wight's counter-reach.

*Composure check DC 12: hold the torch in the specific position Grettir needs — arm extended, wrist steady, the light aimed at the wight's upper body — through the first exchange, while the wight's movements in the narrow space make every instinct say: move, step back, put something between yourself and it.*

**Failure:** You step back once. The light angle drops. Grettir's first strike misses what it was aimed at and hits the wooden framing of the wall. The framing is old and dry. The wood dust goes up. The fight continues but the chamber is now darker than it was.

**Success:** You hold the angle. Grettir's first strike is exact. The wight is bigger than it appeared and does not go down — but it is hurt. The fight continues. You have not moved.

---

#### ACT III — *The Reach*
*The torch: in the Fighter's hand, targeted. Location: BWH, second chamber.*

The wight reaches for the light.

Not for Grettir. Not for the treasure. For the torch. It has been in the dark for a long time and the torch is the threat. It is not wrong.

It is also faster than a dead man should be.

*Dexterity check DC 13: pull the torch back and to the side in the precise motion that keeps it out of the wight's grip without dousing the flame, before the wight's hand closes on the housing.*

**Failure:** The wight's hand closes on the lamp-housing. The flame gutters. For two seconds the chamber is nearly dark. Grettir, fighting by sound and memory, lands one blow. Then you get the lamp back and the light returns, but the wick has been disturbed and the lamp will burn faster now. You have less time.

**Success:** You pull the lamp back and to the left in a motion that keeps the flame level — the wight's hand passes through where the lamp was and finds nothing. Grettir, in the same instant, finds what he was looking for. The wight is going to lose this fight. It knows it now.

---

#### ACT IV — *The Treasure Chamber*
*The torch: burning. Location: BWH, innermost chamber.*

The wight is not dead yet — not twice-dead, which is what it needs to be. It has retreated to the innermost chamber, where the treasure is, and the treasure-room is framed with old oak that has been standing for longer than the longship that carries most Norsemen's sense of time. At the far end: a chest, iron-banded, not locked.

The old oak framing is rotting at the joint-points. Grettir's second exchange with the wight — necessary, final — will bring at least one beam down. The Fighter must reach the chest before the beam comes down, push it to the entrance side of the chamber, and hold the torch above the debris in the falling beam's path.

*Athletics check DC 13: cross the chamber before the beam falls, get the chest clear of the collapse zone, and emerge from the debris with the torch still lit.*

**Failure:** The beam falls. The chest is half-buried under old wood. The torch is in your hand and lit but you are pinned under a cross-beam for thirty seconds while Grettir finishes the wight above you. He pulls you out. The chest is here but the retrieval has taken time and the lamp is at half its remaining fuel.

**Success:** You reach the chest before the beam. Grettir's final strike brings the beam down behind you — you have the chest in one hand and the torch in the other and you are past the collapse zone. Grettir looks at you from the near side of the fallen wood with the expression of someone deciding whether to revise their estimate again. He decides yes.

---

#### ACT V — *The Threshold*
*The torch: burning down. Location: BWH, entrance threshold.*

They are at the barrow entrance. The stone threshold above them. The night air visible through the entrance-post gap — the actual night, with the stars in it, which has been there the whole time and was not accessible.

Grettir carries the chest. He stops at the threshold and looks back at the chamber — once, the way a man looks at a room after the accounting is complete.

**Grettir:** "The light held."

He says it as an observation, not a compliment. He steps out.

The Fighter stands at the threshold. The torch is burning at the last quarter of its oil. The barrow behind them is dark now — the wight twice-dead, the wood settled after the fall, the treasure chest in the open air where the coastal wind will smell it.

The correct ending of this commission is to extinguish the torch at the threshold. To carry a lit torch back to camp — down the headland path, through the heather, to where Grettir's companions are waiting — is to carry the underground with you. The torch belonged to the barrow-night. The barrow-night is over.

*Composure check DC 11: extinguish the torch at the threshold — put out the light that carried you through the dark, at the exact place where it is no longer needed.*

**Failure:** You carry the torch back to camp still burning. It's a practical decision — the path is rough and the starlight is thin. But you are carrying the darkness's instrument into a space it doesn't belong to. Grettir, arriving at camp ahead of you, watches you approach with the still-burning torch and says nothing. The lamp runs out on its own an hour later.

**Success:** You extinguish the torch at the threshold with two fingers across the wick — the gesture of a person who understands the grammar of the situation. The darkness inside the barrow is now the barrow's darkness, not something you carried out. You step over the threshold stone into the night air, which is cold and smells of heather and the sea, and you receive Grettir's Coin — pressed into your hand as you pass him on the path, the way Grettir pays debts, without ceremony.

*The Fighter thinks: he needed one thing and he named it and I carried it and held it and he did not drop the fight and I did not drop the light. There is nothing more in this than that. There is also nothing less.*

**Archive category:** Curse Records — The Dying Enemy's Words That Changed the Hero's Condition

**What this angle reveals:** Cycles 1-2 were about Grettir's heroism — the fire-swimmer and the light-carrier. This angle enters the curse that made his heroism impossible to live with. When Grettir wrestled Glámr — a Norwegian ghost-herdsman who had terrorized the district of Þórhallsstaðir — he won and tore off Glámr's head. In his last moments before decapitation, Glámr laid a curse: Grettir would henceforth fear the dark; all his strength would turn to ill; he would be outlawed; he would never achieve the greatness his deeds deserved. The curse was spoken in front of witnesses. No one present disputed that it was effective. The farmer who lived through the haunting wrote down what Glámr said because he wanted a record of what had entered his household and what left with it.

## Pass 4: Source Summaries (June 2026)

### Part 1 Summary
From the genealogy of Önund Treefoot through Grettir's early adventures in Iceland and Norway. Covers his childhood defiance, first outlawry (three winters for killing Skeggi), his proving-grounds in Norway (verse-battles, jarl's service), the barrow-fight where he gains Jökulsnaut, his return to Iceland, and the escalating incidents that lead to full *skóggangr* — wolf's head outlawry at age twenty. The saga emphasizes strength tested against man and ghost alike, the pattern of incident becoming reputation becoming legend.

### Part 2 Summary
From Grettir's return to his mother at Bjarg through the revenge and resolution. Chronicles the killing of Thorbjorn Oxmain (blood-price for Atli), his fugitive years across Iceland, the island stronghold of Drangey with his brother Illugi, and the sorcery-death at Thorbjorn Öngull's hands. Then follows Thorsteinn Dromund's journey to Constantinople, the Varangian service, the love story and legal trap with Spes, their return to Norway in honor, and final pilgrimage to Rome where they seek absolution and build stone retreats to atone. The saga closes with Sturla's testimony: no outlaw was ever so distinguished as Grettir the Strong.
