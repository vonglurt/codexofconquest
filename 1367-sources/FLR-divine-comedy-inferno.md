## Pass 1 — Full Spoiler Summary with Canto Notes

The Inferno is the first cantica of Dante's Divine Comedy: 34 cantos in terza rima, tracing the narrator-pilgrim Dante's descent through Hell on the night of Good Friday through Easter Sunday, 1300. It is simultaneously a journey through the afterlife, a political commentary on medieval Italy, a meditation on justice and pity, and an autobiography.

**Canto I** — The dark wood, midway through life. Three beasts bar the uphill path: a leopard (fraud), a lion (pride), a she-wolf (incontinence). The shade of Virgil appears and offers to guide Dante "through an eternal place, where thou shalt hear the hopeless shrieks, shalt see the ancient spirits sore afflicted, who cry out each one for the second death."

**Canto II** — Dante hesitates. He is not Aeneas, not Paul; what right has he to make this journey? Virgil explains: Beatrice descended from Heaven to ask this of him; she was moved by Lucy, who was moved by the Virgin Mary. The chain of love that sent Virgil is explained. Dante agrees.

**Canto III** — The Gate of Hell: "Through me you pass into the city of woe... Abandon every hope, ye who enter here." The Vestibule: the Uncommitted — souls who were neither good nor evil in life, rejected by Heaven and Hell alike, condemned to run after a blank banner, stung by wasps. The river Acheron; the ferryman Charon. Dante faints at the crack of thunder.

**Canto IV** — Circle 1: Limbo. The virtuous pagans and unbaptized — they are not punished, merely deprived of hope. Homer, Virgil, Horace, Ovid, Lucan greet them; then Aristotle in a meadow with Socrates, Plato, Caesar, Saladin, Euclid. No torment; great sadness.

**Canto V** — Circle 2: The Lustful. Minos judges each soul, wrapping his tail around himself to indicate the appropriate circle. The souls blown forever in a dark whirlwind. Francesca da Rimini speaks: she and Paolo Malatesta were reading the story of Lancelot and Guinevere together; at the passage where Lancelot kisses the queen, Paolo kissed her. Her husband discovered them and killed them both. Her account of love's compulsion — "Love, that releases no loved one from loving, seized me" — is the poem's most famous passage. Dante faints.

**Cantos VI–VII** — Circle 3: Gluttons in the filthy rain; Ciacco the Florentine identifies the political factions. Circle 4: The Avaricious and Prodigal shove heavy boulders at each other in an eternal collision. Virgil identifies many as clergy.

**Canto VIII** — Circle 5: The Wrathful and the Sullen in the Styx. The ferryman Phlegyas rows Dante and Virgil across. Filippo Argenti, a Florentine noble, rises from the slime to grab the boat; Dante does not pity him; Virgil praises Dante's scorn. Argenti is pulled back by other shades and torn apart. The City of Dis appears; fallen angels bar the gate.

**Canto IX** — The Furies appear on the battlements and threaten to summon Medusa to turn Dante to stone. Virgil covers Dante's eyes. A Heavenly messenger descends, opens the gate with his wand, and departs without speaking to them.

**Canto X** — Circle 6: Heretics in flaming open tombs. Farinata degli Uberti rises from his tomb "as if he had a great contempt of Hell." He is a Ghibelline; Dante is a Guelph; they debate Florentine politics across the fire. Farinata prophecies Dante's exile: within fifty months he will know how hard it is to return. Cavalcante de' Cavalcanti interrupts to ask about his son Guido (Dante's friend and fellow poet); Dante's odd response about Guido makes Cavalcante think his son is dead; he falls back into the tomb; Farinata explains Cavalcante's confusion about time — the dead can see the future but not the present.

**Canto XI** — A pause at the edge of Circle 7. Virgil explains Hell's structure: below are the Violent, the Fraudulent, and the Treacherous. The principle is that the worse the sin, the closer to God's reason it is betrayed, and the deeper the circle.

**Canto XII** — Circle 7, Ring 1: Violence against others. The Minotaur guarding the descent, who attacks in rage as they pass. The river of boiling blood (Phlegethon) with tyrants, murderers, and brigands submerged to varying depths. The centaurs patrol the bank. Nessus ferries them across.

**Canto XIII** — Circle 7, Ring 2: The Wood of Suicides. Those who destroyed their own bodies are enclosed in thorny trees; harpies wound the branches so the souls can speak only through their pain. Dante breaks a twig at Virgil's instruction; Pier della Vigna speaks from the bleeding branch. He was chief minister to Frederick II, falsely accused of betrayal by jealous courtiers; imprisoned, blinded, he killed himself. He swears he was never disloyal. He gives a devastatingly formal account: every sentence is layered in legal language that mirrors his double betrayal — betrayed by those he served, betrayed by his own hand. After death, the soul falls to whatever thorn-tree it lands on; the body will hang from its own branch on Judgment Day, never to be re-united with the soul.

**Canto XIV** — Circle 7, Ring 3: Blasphemers lying on burning sand under a rain of fire. Capaneus, who stormed Thebes and defied Zeus, lies spread-eagle on the sand, still defying God: "Such as I was living, such am I dead." Virgil reproves him; Capaneus is unmoved.

**Cantos XV–XVI** — Sodomites walking on burning sand, unable to stop. Brunetto Latini — Dante's teacher and mentor — runs alongside Dante, keeping pace as they talk. He asks Dante to remember his Tesoro. He prophecies Dante's exile and advises him to follow his star. Dante says that Brunetto's image is printed in his mind and grieves him; his affection is not diminished by finding Brunetto here. Three Florentine noblemen also address Dante; they ask him to carry news of them to the living world.

**Canto XVII** — The monster Geryon (fraud: a man's face, a serpent's body, a scorpion's tail). On its back, Dante and Virgil descend to Malebolge. The Usurers sit at the edge of the sand, watching purses around their necks; Dante recognizes three by their blazons.

**Cantos XVIII–XXII** — Malebolge, Bolgias 1–5:
- Panderers and Seducers in two columns, whipped by demons (Jason)
- Flatterers submerged in excrement
- Simoniacs inverted in rocky holes with flames on their feet; Pope Nicholas III speaks, waiting for Boniface VIII and Clement V to arrive
- Diviners with heads twisted backwards (Tiresias, Amphiaraus, Manto, Michael Scot)
- Barrators in boiling pitch; the Malebranche demons provide extended slapstick — they carry a senator from Lucca, quarrel about who dropped him, a soul escapes while they fight, Dante and Virgil must flee

**Cantos XXIII–XXV** — Bolgias 6–7:
- Hypocrites in gilded lead robes, moving with agonizing slowness; Caiaphas and Annas crucified on the ground to be walked over by other hypocrites
- Thieves and serpents in an ongoing metamorphosis: a serpent bites a thief and they exchange natures; Vanni Fucci (who prophesies political catastrophe for Florence) makes an obscene gesture at God

**Cantos XXVI–XXVII** — Bolgia 8: Fraudulent Counselors in individual flames. The greatest cantos in the poem. Ulysses and Diomedes share one flame; Ulysses speaks of his last voyage — after Ithaca, restless, he gathered his old crew and sailed west past Gibraltar. He gave them a speech: "You were not made to live as brutes, but to pursue virtue and knowledge." They sailed into the open Atlantic, south toward the unfamiliar stars, saw a mountain in the darkness (the Mountain of Purgatory), and a whirlwind rose and sank the ship. The sea closed over them. Guido da Montefeltro describes his sin: his political counsel to Pope Boniface VIII — "Promise much, hold little" — for a papal siege; he thought his deathbed penance absolved him, but Francis and a devil argued over his soul at death and the devil won on a technicality: you cannot repent a sin you have already resolved to commit.

**Cantos XXVIII–XXIX** — Bolgia 9: Sowers of Discord, split open by a sword-wielding demon. Mohammed split from chin to anus; he must circle and the wound re-opens each time. Bertran de Born carries his own severed head like a lantern, swinging it by the hair; he raised the son against the father (the Young King Henry against Henry II of England). Master Adam the counterfeiter, in Bolgia 10, scratches his dropsical body and argues bitterly with a shade of Sinon about whose fault was worse; Dante watches their argument too long and Virgil reproves him for lingering over such quarrels.

**Cantos XXXII–XXXIII** — Circle 9: Cocytus, the frozen lake. Traitors:
- Caina: traitors to family (brothers and close kin). Count Ugolino gnaws the skull of Archbishop Ruggieri, pauses to wipe his mouth on Ruggieri's hair, and tells his story. He and his sons and grandsons were locked in the Hunger Tower; the door was nailed shut; they heard the hammering. His sons offer him their flesh ("Father, it would grieve us less, if thou wouldst eat of us"). Gaddo died at his feet crying "Father, why do you not help me?" Ugolino went blind from weeping, groped over the dead bodies for two days, then "fasting had more power than grief." Whether he ate them: Dante leaves it open.
- Antenora: traitors to country. Bocca degli Abati betrayed Florence at Montaperti; Fra Alberigo, whose soul is in Hell while his body still lives (inhabited by a demon).
- Judecca: traitors to lords, completely encased in ice. Satan himself in the center: three faces (red, yellow, black), weeping from each eye, chewing three sinners in three mouths — Judas (head first), Brutus and Cassius (feet first).

**Canto XXXIV** — Descent down Satan's body — he is frozen at the center of the Earth. At the point where gravity reverses, Virgil turns and climbs downward (which becomes upward). They emerge through a rock-passage into the Southern Hemisphere, see stars. "And thence we issued out again to see the stars."

## Pass 2 — Character Deep Dives

**Dante the Pilgrim** — Distinct from Dante the Poet who narrates. The Pilgrim changes. He faints for Francesca; he approves of Argenti's torment; he does not flinch at Ugolino's story; he kicks a traitor in the face in the frozen lake and lies about giving him help afterward. He is afraid almost everywhere. He hesitates before entering. He asks questions and sometimes falls silent at the wrong moments. He learns — or seems to — what Hell is, which is partly the same as learning what justice is, and partly the same as learning that justice and the world's justice do not coincide.

**Virgil** — The guide who knows the way but cannot go everywhere. He is in Limbo himself — not punished, not saved, forever deprived of hope. He is patient, occasionally impatient, visibly pained by aspects of his situation. He lies to Malebranche demons to get through (claiming their route is divinely sanctioned when it is not). He can be fooled — the demons lure them off the shortest path with a detour. He weeps at certain circles. His function is primarily rational: he explains; he guides; he speaks for Dante where Dante cannot speak. He is the best guide available for Hell and is not the right guide for what comes after.

**Francesca da Rimini** — Murdered with Paolo by her husband after their adultery. Her speech has three parts: she describes love's compulsion ("it releases no loved one from loving"), she locates the moment the act occurred (the reading of Lancelot), and she curses the book. She is not asking for mercy; she is explaining. Her explanation is a performance of exactly the sin she is being punished for: she is still making the act someone else's fault. The book. The story. The author. Dante faints — probably because he recognizes something.

**Farinata degli Uberti** — The poem's first great proud sinner. He rises from his flaming tomb "as if he had a great contempt of Hell." The contrapasso: heretics who believed the soul died with the body are enclosed in tombs — encased in the thing they thought was all that existed. Farinata's contempt for his own punishment is part of his character and his sin. He scattered the Florentines twice and opposed their return once; he also opposed their annihilation when the Ghibelline faction called for it. He is wrong about God; he may have been right about Florence. Dante cannot fully decide.

**Pier della Vigna** — Frederick II's chancellor, the most powerful non-royal in Italy until his fall. Falsely accused of betrayal by rivals; imprisoned; blinded; killed himself in despair. His speech from the thorn tree is the poem's most formally artificial: every sentence a double entendre, every word chosen to mirror the double betrayal he suffered (betrayed by those he served; then betrayed by his own hand). He swears absolute loyalty — "I swear to thee that I never broke faith to my lord who was so worthy of honour." Dante does not endorse or deny the claim. The poem simply records it.

**Brunetto Latini** — Dante's teacher, author of the encyclopedic Tesoro, condemned among the sodomites. He runs alongside Dante, keeping pace, never slowing, because stopping is not permitted. Their conversation is one of the most tender in the poem. Brunetto's care for Dante is undiminished by Hell; Dante's grief about finding Brunetto here is explicit and sincere. Brunetto asks Dante to recommend his Tesoro to the living; Dante agrees. The relationship between the beloved teacher and the student who knows the teacher is damned is not resolved — it is simply held.

**Ulysses** — The most philosophically charged soul in Hell. He is condemned for fraudulent counsel, but his final speech is about the opposite of fraud: he tells his crew the naked truth about the human condition — that they were made for knowledge and virtue, not for comfortable ignorance. His condemnation is not for this speech; it is for the broader career of trickery. But the speech is there in the poem, and it sings, and Dante chose to put it there. The voyage ends in shipwreck at the base of the Mountain of Purgatory — the place Dante will ascend. Ulysses went the wrong way to the right destination and died for it.

**Count Ugolino** — The poem's most terrible voice. His situation: frozen in the ice gnawing Ruggieri's skull. His story: the Hunger Tower. His sons offered him their flesh. He went blind. He groped over the dead. "Fasting had more power than grief." Dante does not say what this means. The deliberate gap — whether Ugolino ate his children — is not ambiguity; it is a narrative wound. The point is that the reader must decide. Whatever Ugolino is accused of is what the reader supplied from their own imagination of extremity.

## Pass 3 — Pivotal Scene: Pier della Vigna in the Wood of Suicides

*(Canto XIII)*

Dante and Virgil are in a dark forest. Nothing grows right here: black leaves, gnarled branches, no fruit. Harpies nest in the trees and peck the branches, letting out the pain that is the only voice available. Virgil tells Dante to break a branch.

The branch bleeds.

"Why dost thou rend me?" — a voice from the tree, human, confused, in pain. "Art thou void of all compassion? Men were we, and now are turned to stocks."

It is Pier della Vigna. He does not announce himself; he is identified only by his elaborately double rhetoric, which is instantly recognizable to anyone who knew his correspondence. The style is itself a signature: every noun paired and turned, every predicate qualified with its opposite. He is explaining a betrayal by means of the very language that made his name.

His account: he served Frederick faithfully in everything. He held the keys to Frederick's heart. He was so trusted that the emperor shared his most secret thoughts. Then envy arose in the court — the common disease of courts — and spread through Caesar's mind. Honours, once given to him, turned to grief. His mind, "in scornful mood," killed itself "thinking by dying to escape from scorn."

He insists his innocence with every rhetorical tool he has. "By the new roots of this tree I swear to thee that I never broke faith to my lord who was so worthy of honour."

Virgil asks him to speak further, so that Dante may carry his story to the living world. This is the explicit bargain of the Inferno's most affecting moments: the damned speak because Dante will remember them; their story reaches a world that condemned them before their death and will outlast that condemnation.

But the tree has a complication. When Pier is done speaking, another tree calls to them, shaking in a panic: a soul who hanged himself in his own house, having lost everything in a single day. The harpies come. The wood tears. The shade runs through the thorns, leaving fragments of himself on every branch. No deliberate speech; just the terror of an ending that was not chosen, as opposed to Pier's ending, which was.

The contrast is Dante's argument about self-destruction: Pier's formal eloquence is the mark of a controlled mind that chose its moment; the other shade is the mark of what despair looks like when there is no rhetoric left. Both are here. The wood does not differentiate.

On Judgment Day, their bodies will return to the wood and hang from their own branches; the soul is condemned never to be re-housed in flesh. The body that was violated by the soul's abandonment is not restored. The sentence is specifically: you destroyed the house, so you will have no house.

## Seven Storylines from the Inferno

**1. "The Vigna Deposition"** ← *Selected for vignette and UQF stub*
Pier della Vigna swore his innocence in Hell. A clerk who served in Frederick II's chancellery wrote a sealed deposition naming the specific courtiers who fabricated the charges — jealousy dressed as treason. The document was hidden under a monastery floor for decades. Now the monastery is being dissolved and the legates arrive tomorrow to seize all records. The Fighter carries the deposition to the imperial archive before it disappears into ecclesiastical custody.

**2. "What Brunetto Asked"**
Brunetto Latini asked Dante to recommend the Tesoro to the living. A continuation manuscript of his Tesoretto — the allegorical poem that ends mid-sentence — has surfaced in a Florentine notary's estate. The continuation is disputed: is it Brunetto's hand or a later forger's? The one person who could authenticate it (a former student who knew Brunetto's hand) is dying. The Fighter carries the manuscript from the notary's estate to the student's bedside and back to the archive. *Theme: The fragmentary legacy; the ending that may not be an ending; the teacher's unfinished work and the authentication that would make it whole.*

**3. "Francesca's Book"**
The copy of the Lancelot story that was being read when Paolo kissed Francesca — it still exists in the Malatesta library. The executor of the library is cataloguing it as simply "a French romance." Francesca's niece has written a sealed account of what she witnessed that day, identifying the book by its specific missing page (a page was torn out in the struggle). The account changes the inheritance dispute over the library: the book is evidence, not décor. The Fighter carries the niece's account to the library before the catalogue is closed. *Theme: The object as the scene of a crime it did not commit; the inventory that determines whether evidence is preserved or disappeared.*

**4. "Bocca's Denial"**
Bocca degli Abati is in Antenora for betraying Florence at Montaperti (turning his sword on the Florentine cavalry at the critical moment). He refused to give his name to Dante until Dante tore his hair. He was not convicted in life; he claimed innocence. A sealed account from a surviving cavalryman who saw Bocca's sword turn — written in his last illness — needs to reach the Florentine civic archive before the official account of Montaperti closes. *Theme: The betrayal that was witnessed but never witnessed in a venue that counted; the cavalryman's dying declaration.*

**5. "The Uncommitted"**
In the Vestibule of Hell, the Uncommitted run after a blank banner — people who refused to take sides in life, rejected by both Heaven and Hell. A specific figure from a Florentine political dispute declined to sign either faction's loyalty oath during the 1300 Jubilee; he died without committing. His son needs an authenticated copy of his father's pre-schism position statement — a declaration he wrote before the schism solidified, when taking no side was still an intellectual option rather than a failure of nerve. The archive has conflated him with the blank-banner faction. The Fighter carries the dated pre-schism declaration to restore the distinction. *Theme: The difference between principled neutrality and cowardice is a date on a document.*

**6. "Ugolino's Children"**
Count Ugolino's story ends with the gap. Two of his grandsons survived the political disaster and eventually died in reduced circumstances; one of them dictated a deposition about the Hunger Tower to a Dominican friar based on what his grandfather told him before the imprisonment. The deposition includes the specific terms Ruggieri used to justify the imprisonment — legal language that reveals it was a political execution, not a judicial finding. The Fighter carries the grandson's deposition from the Dominican archive to the Pisan civic register before the Ruggieri family files its counter-claim. *Theme: The account of political murder that disguised itself as judicial process; the testimony that names the mechanism without resolving the moral horror.*

**7. "Geryon's Passage"**
Geryon (the monster of fraud) carries Dante and Virgil down on his back, making a wide circle as he descends. On the side of the cliff they pass, they see a figure — a usurer who has been on the burning sand for decades and who has carved a continuous record of what he witnessed into the stone cliff face: names, dates, accounts of transactions, evidence of fraudulent contracts that ruined him and three other merchants in the same year. The record is about to be lost when the cliff face crumbles. A stonecutter has made a rubbing of the carved text. The Fighter carries the rubbing from the base of the cliff to the commercial court. *Theme: The permanent medium as the desperate last resort; the man who could not speak in any living venue carved his testimony into stone.*

### 5-Act Vignette Play: "The Vigna Deposition"

*Register: French noir — clipped, precise, no sentiment wasted.*
*The Fighter carries: The Vigna Deposition.*
*TOKEN: Granted Act 1. Checked Acts 2–4. Taken Act 5.*

---

**ACT 1 — MBR (Montbrione Monastery)**
*Skill: Insight DC 12*

The last monk is packing his cell into one chest. Brother Tommaso has been here for forty years; his cell has the smell of a room where the same man has worked for forty years, and where the work is now finished. The dissolution order came six months ago; the papal legates arrive at dawn.

He found the document three years ago when a section of the scriptorium floor was repaired. Under the loose stone: a sealed packet in a wax cover, the seal long dried and cracked but unbroken. He recognized the chancellor's device from a letter in the monastery's collection. He sat with it for three years because he did not know who to give it to, and he was afraid of what "giving it to the wrong person" meant when the wrong person controlled access to every archive between here and Rome.

"The legates will seize everything from this building tomorrow," he says. "Including whatever I have not personally carried out of it."

The TOKEN is granted: The Vigna Deposition — a sealed document, the wax cover intact but dried, the imperial chancellery device on the seal; inside, based on the endorsement visible through a gap in the cover, a clerk's sworn deposition naming the specific courtiers who fabricated the charges against the former chancellor.

*Insight DC 12:* Tommaso is asking for something specific: not delivery to any archive, but delivery to an archive where the document will be logged in the general intake record — not filed "under review," not held for examination, not entered in restricted access. The distinction is between a document that exists and a document that can be found. He needs the Fighter to understand this before accepting the commission.

*Success:* "You need it in the general record with a date and a signature," the Fighter says.

Tommaso nods. "If it goes anywhere else, it might as well stay here."

---

**ACT 2 — Road south toward the archive city**
*Skill: Persuasion DC 13*

A papal courier is checking correspondence from dissolved institutions at the road-gate south of the monastery. He has a list of houses; Montbrione is on it. He does not know what is in any given packet; he knows that everything leaving a dissolved institution is technically under papal custody pending review.

"This is personal correspondence of a former imperial chancellor," the Fighter says. "It was never monastic property."

The courier does not look convinced.

*Persuasion DC 13:* The argument has a genuine legal basis: the dissolution order covers monastic records — documents that were owned, maintained, and catalogued by the monastery. This document was hidden under a floor, never inventoried, never entered in any monastic record. The monastery never had custody of it. The dissolution order therefore cannot apply, because the monastery's custody is a precondition of the seizure, and that custody never existed.

*Success:* The courier writes a note to himself to check with his superior and waves the Fighter through.

*Failure:* He demands the packet. The Fighter must either fight (2× couriers) or find an alternate route that adds half a day.

---

**ACT 3 — PAR (Palermo Archive District)**
*Skill: Persuasion DC 14 + Combat: 2× legate's soldiers AC 13 HP 18*

The archive keeper, Messer Fazio, is a careful man. He receives the packet, examines the seal, reads the endorsement. He knows what it is. He is quiet for a long moment.

"The house of origin is dissolved," he says. "Everything from that house is under papal custody pending review."

He is about to enter it in the restricted intake.

From the anteroom, a man in the papal notary's livery — who has been watching since the Fighter entered — rises and produces a formal writ. This specific class of documents from dissolved institutions is under his authority. He directs the two soldiers flanking the door to receive the packet.

*Persuasion DC 14:* Fazio is not corrupt; he is concerned about the precedent. The argument: the dissolution order and the restricted custody protocol both have a gap in them — they assume the monastery had custody, which requires the document to have been in their records. The papal notary's authority derives from the same dissolution order. The same gap that defeats the dissolution order defeats the notary's authority. This is a prior-to-dissolution document that the monastery never officially possessed. It is not a monastic document. It is an imperial document that was physically inside a building for fifty years without belonging to the building.

*Success:* Fazio accepts the argument and enters the packet in general intake.

*Failure:* The soldiers step forward. The Fighter must defeat them (AC 13, HP 18 each) before Fazio can stamp the restricted intake.

---

**ACT 4 — PAR Harbor District**
*Skill: Athletics DC 13*

The general intake requires a duplicate entry in the city register, which is maintained in a separate building on the far side of the harbor district. Without the duplicate, the archive entry can be challenged as incomplete and revised.

A harbor fire has started near the rope-walk. The direct route through the harbor district is blocked: fire wardens, panicking merchants, barrels of pitch being rolled away from the burning building. Two alternate routes: one through the high street above the harbor (possible, but twice the distance), one through the fish market and the chandler's row that runs behind the burning buildings (shorter, but the smoke is thick and the footing uncertain).

The city register closes one hour before vespers.

*Athletics DC 13:* Navigate the blocked harbor district — either the high route at speed or the short route through the smoke — to the city registrar's building in time.

*Success:* The duplicate entry is made. The Vigna Deposition now has two corroborating records.

*Failure:* The registrar has closed and locked the door. The Fighter must argue through a shutter or return at dawn, risking the notary's overnight challenge.

---

**ACT 5 — WM (Weimar Archive District)**
*Skill: Insight DC 12*

The Archivus Sweelinck receives the authenticated packet in his office and opens the cover — just the endorsement, not the seal. He reads carefully. He closes it again.

"The document names a cardinal's great-grandfather," he says, "as one of the principals. I can file this in the general record, where it will be visible to any researcher who asks. Or I can file it in the restricted collection, where it will be preserved but not found without a specific warrant."

He is not corrupt. He is weighing a precedent: historically contested accusations against historical figures, even authenticated ones, have typically been filed in restricted access to prevent political exploitation of partial evidence.

His clerk, who has been listening, has the expression of someone who wants to say something and is waiting to be asked.

*Insight DC 12:* The clerk knows the specific precedent that governs this: a Florentine case in which a similar document — a contemporary deposition naming a specific noble in a political murder — was filed in general access on the grounds that it was prior consistent statement evidence, not accusation. The precedent is in the archive somewhere; the clerk is waiting for the Fighter to ask about prior rulings so he can name it. The Archivus needs the precedent to exist, not just the argument.

*Success:* "Has there been a prior ruling on contemporaneous depositions in contested convictions?" the Fighter asks.

The clerk produces the reference. The Archivus reads for two minutes. He opens the general intake register and enters the deposition with the date and his signature.

The TOKEN is taken: The Vigna Deposition is filed in the general record of the Weimar Archive, visible to any researcher, dated and signed. Pier della Vigna's innocence in human courts is now in the permanent record. His soul remains in the Wood of Suicides. Both things are true. The poem will say the second; the archive will say the first. Both will persist.

## Cycle 2 · What Brunetto Asked

**New angle:** Cycle 1 followed the formal and institutional — divine verdict vs. human record, two separate truth systems that can both be accurate and point in different directions. Cycle 2 enters the personal. In Canto XV, Brunetto Latini runs alongside Dante in Hell and asks one specific thing: "Let my Tesoro be commended to thee, in which I still live, and more I ask not." What cycle 1 missed: the beloved teacher's unfinished work, and what it costs to authenticate it when the only living witness who knew the hand is dying. The continuation manuscript is a gift that arrived after the teacher was already in Hell; the student who can confirm it was never part of the gift.

---

### Token Doctrine

**TOKEN: The Tesoretto Continuation**
A manuscript continuation of Brunetto Latini's unfinished allegorical poem — the pages physically bridge the known mid-sentence ending to new text; the paper shows the age the content suggests; three characteristic spelling habits of Brunetto's hand appear in the first two folios. Ser Goffredo da Prato, a former student who spent three years copying Brunetto's correspondence, is the last person alive who can confirm those habits belong to the hand rather than an imitator. The Fighter carries the manuscript from the Florentine notary's estate to his hillside villa and back to the archive.

### 5-Act Vignette Play: "What Brunetto Asked"

*A Fighter has been retained by the executor of a Florentine notary's estate to carry a disputed manuscript to a dying scholar and back. The TOKEN ITEM is the Tesoretto Continuation: a manuscript picking up from the mid-sentence ending of Brunetto Latini's allegorical poem, unread for decades, unattributed, unchallenged only because no one knew it existed. The Fighter's job is to keep the chain of custody intact, get Ser Goffredo's authentication before he dies, and bring both back to the archive.*

*The Thing · Tesoretto Continuation · unbound folios, rolled in oilskin · executor → Fighter*

---

**ACT I — The Notary's Estate, Florence**

*Afternoon. The late notary's study. His executor, Messer Bernardo, at the desk with a rolled oilskin package and an expression of managed uncertainty.*

*The Thing · Tesoretto Continuation · rolled in oilskin, unread · waiting on the desk*

*The Fighter observes: Bernardo has not unrolled the package in the Fighter's presence. He is not uncertain about the manuscript's value. He is uncertain about who else knows it exists.*

Bernardo explains without ornamentation: the manuscript was in the notary's private chest, not the professional files. It was not catalogued. A Venetian dealer appeared two days after the death asking about "certain literary manuscripts from the collection," which means someone told him it was there. Bernardo needs it authenticated and entered in the civic record before the dealer can acquire a right of first examination — which, under Florentine guild procedure, would give him forty-eight hours of exclusive access and the ability to suppress publication.

The authentication can only be done by Ser Goffredo da Prato, who is eighty years old and has not left his hillside villa in four months.

---

**ACT II — The Road North of Florence**

*The road to the villa, three hours from the city. A man on a brown horse has been visible on the parallel ridge for an hour. He is not in a hurry. He is following.*

*The Thing · Tesoretto Continuation · rolled in oilskin · Fighter's pack*

*The Fighter observes: the brown horse has been there since the gate. The rider has made no move to close distance.*

At the second waystation, the rider comes down from the ridge and introduces himself pleasantly. He is the Venetian dealer's representative, a man named Giacomo. He explains that his employer is a serious scholar with access to the best archive facilities in Venice; the manuscript's interests would be better served in his employer's custody. He is not threatening. He offers a reasonable sum for the package and the Fighter's silence. He is willing to give Bernardo's executor a proper accounting.

---

**ACT III — Ser Goffredo's Villa**

*A hillside house above Florence: terracotta roof, a lemon tree in the courtyard, a smell of old paper in the corridor. Ser Goffredo in a chair by the window.*

*The Thing · Tesoretto Continuation · unrolled on Ser Goffredo's reading table · under examination*

*The Fighter observes: Ser Goffredo's hands are shaking slightly but his eyes are not. He reads without haste. He has been reading for twenty minutes without speaking.*

He has found the characteristic habit — the doubled 'l' in 'villano' and 'villaggio' that appears in every document Brunetto drafted himself and in none of the documents Brunetto's copyists produced. Three times in the first two folios. He looks up at the Fighter. "Where did this come from?" He is not asking who had it. He is asking how it survived. He sets down his stylus and considers this for a moment. Then he picks it up again.

He signs slowly, his name in two lines instead of one, the way his hand has learned to manage the tremor. He attaches the authentication leaf. He says: "Read me the last line of the Tesoretto. The one everyone knows."

---

**ACT IV — The Road Back**

*Two hours south of the villa. Afternoon. The road runs between vineyard walls.*

*The Thing · Tesoretto Continuation · rolled back in oilskin, authentication leaf sealed inside · Fighter's pack · targeted*

*The Fighter observes: three riders have come out of the vineyard gate ahead. Giacomo is not among them.*

The dealer's hired men have been sent independently: they are not here to negotiate. They know the authentication is complete — someone at the villa told them, which means they were watching. They want the oilskin package. The route back to Florence runs through a narrow section between the vineyard walls; there is no way around them.

Combat: 2× hired riders (AC 12, HP 20, short swords and leather)

---

**ACT V — The Florentine Archive**

*Evening. The civic archive's literary intake desk. The notary archivist Ser Aldo, reading glasses, the classification ledger open to the literary acquisitions section.*

*The Thing · Tesoretto Continuation · oilskin package, authentication leaf inside · Fighter's hands → archive*

*The Fighter observes: Ser Aldo has been told about the dealer's interest. He is deciding whether the authentication leaf is sufficient to classify this as Brunetto's work in the general literary record — which would make it publicly accessible — or as a disputed attribution requiring the restricted acquisition review.*

He looks at Ser Goffredo's signature on the authentication leaf. He looks at the date. Ser Goffredo signed it this morning. He is still living — barely, but living. The authentication is a living witness attestation, not a posthumous document. That changes the procedural category. He turns to the general ledger.

"Brunetto Latini, Tesoretto, continuation folios," he says. "Authenticated by primary witness. Filed for public access." He stamps it. He takes the oilskin from the Fighter's hands and sets it on the acquisition shelf.

He looks at the Fighter for a moment. "Ser Goffredo had a request to pass along." He takes a folded note from his desk — it was delivered by the villa's servant an hour ago. He hands it over.

*The note reads, in a hand that is barely legible: "Tell whoever carries it: Maestro Brunetto would be glad."*
