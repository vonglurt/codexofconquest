## Pass 1 — Spine Summary

Howard Pyle's *The Merry Adventures of Robin Hood* is the foundational literary synthesis of the English outlaw-hero tradition, written in 1883 in a consciously archaic prose that blends the cadences of the King James Bible with the episodic structure of the medieval ballad cycle. The book does not tell a single plot; it tells a life — from the day a hot-tempered young yeoman named Robert of Locksley kills one of the Sheriff of Nottingham's foresters in a wager-dispute and becomes an outlaw at eighteen, to the day he dies in a tower room at Kirklees Nunnery, betrayed by a cousin prioress, shooting his last arrow from a casement window to mark his own grave.

Between those two moments, Pyle assembles a world with few equals in English literature for sheer mirth. Robin Hood's Sherwood is not a den of desperate men; it is an alternative kingdom, populated by some hundred and forty "stout yeomen in Lincoln green" who eat venison, drink Canary wine, sleep under the greenwood tree, and answer tyranny with the long bow and the practical joke. Robin himself is not a brooder or a schemer so much as a performance — the boldest, merriest, most shameless trickster in Nottinghamshire, a man who dresses as a potter to sell the Sheriff's wife crockery in her own courtyard, then rides back to Nottingham to play cards with the Sheriff himself at his own board and drink his wine before vanishing into the night. He disguises himself as a beggar, a cobbler, a friar, a broom-seller, and a butcher; he escapes royal guards disguised as the very man sent to hunt him down (Guy of Gisbourne) and rescues Little John from the gallows while wearing a dead assassin's horse-hide cloak. His weapons are cunning, archery, and laughter, roughly in that order.

The band forms gradually across the opening episodes. Will Stutely is taken prisoner by the Sheriff and rescued in a raid by Robin and a dozen men before a public hanging can be carried out. Little John — "seven feet in height," with a fist like a mallet — is met on a log bridge crossing a stream and defeats Robin in a quarterstaff fight before being inducted, christened with his new name, and seated at the feast that follows. Arthur a Bland, the Nottingham tanner, thrashes Robin in a stick fight and joins. Will Scarlet, Robin's own nephew, arrives from exile in a foreign land and is recruited after their first brawl. Friar Tuck, a fat and combative Benedictine who lives in a hermitage near the River Leen, is wrestled into the band through a back-and-forth of wrestling and archery that leaves both men drenched and laughing. Allan a Dale, a wandering minstrel found weeping on a forest path, is helped to recover his true love Ellen from a forced marriage to an elderly Norman knight, with the Bishop of Hereford's own gold chain stripped from his neck and given to the bride at the altar. Midge the Miller's Son joins after besting Robin in a fistfight. The Cook of the Sheriff of Nottingham is recruited mid-service after proving himself with sword against Little John and showing more admirable spirit than his employer.

The book's chief antagonists are the Sheriff of Nottingham, bumbling and choleric, defeated so many times that his humiliations form a running comedy, and the Bishop of Hereford, pompous, venal, and memorable chiefly for the scene in which Robin forces him to say mass in the forest chapel while the outlaws keep guard with drawn bows. A graver enemy appears in the middle section: Sir Stephen of Trent, the villain behind the forced marriage scheme, and above all Guy of Gisbourne, a mercenary tracker in a cloak of horse-hide who is hired by the King to hunt Robin down. The Gisbourne episode is the book's most violent: a mutual ambush in the forest, fought with broadsword while the Sheriff hangs Little John nearby, resolved when Robin slits Gisbourne's throat and dons his horse-hide disguise to bluff Little John's release from the gallows — one of Pyle's most cinematically constructed sequences.

The aristocracy appears in two registers: corrupt nobles who oppress yeomen (Sir Stephen, a succession of unnamed tax-collectors), and honorable knights whom Robin aids. The most important of the latter is Sir Richard of the Lea, whose story forms a sustained novella-within-the-novel: a broken knight who has mortgaged his estate to the Priory of Emmet to ransom his son and cannot repay, rescued by Robin's gift of four hundred pounds of silver. Sir Richard repays the debt, recovers his lands, and remains loyal — sheltering Robin's men, sending warning when the King comes. Sir Richard's son, young Sir Henry of the Lea, appears at court with King Richard and kneels beside his father to defy royal displeasure when Robin Hood is threatened with punishment.

King Richard arrives in Nottingham under pageantry: twenty-eight heralds, five-score knights, barons in cloth of gold, with hair and beard "like threads of gold" and eyes "as blue as the summer sky." He hears tales of Robin at the Guild Hall banquet and the next morning rides into Sherwood disguised as a Black Friar with six companions, carrying a hundred gold pounds to bait his own robbery. Robin stops them on the road, takes fifty pounds, and invites the party to dine in Sherwood — where Robin loses a buffet contest when the friar (the King himself, who has been described as striking "like a thunderbolt") knocks him flat. The mask drops. Robin kneels. The King gives the band a full pardon, takes Robin, Little John, Will Scarlet, and Allan a Dale into his service, and makes the rest royal forest rangers.

After years in the King's service Robin rises to Earl of Huntingdon. Richard dies in battle. Under King John, Robin returns to Sherwood against orders and is hunted; the Sheriff of Nottingham falls with an arrow in his brain in the subsequent forest fight. Then comes the end: fever, Kirklees Nunnery, the treacherous cousin, the opened vein. Little John tears a stone mortar from the ground and hurls it through a studded oak door to reach his dying master. Robin speaks his last, forbids Little John to take revenge on the women, asks to be raised to see the woodlands one more time, then draws his bow and shoots a final arrow from the casement. "Mark where this arrow lodges," he whispers, "and there let my grave be digged." His hand sinks with the bow; something departs with the arrow. Little John lays him down, folds his hands, covers his face, and walks out without a word. At dawn he returns with six yeomen and the sound of weeping fills the glade. Pyle closes with Robin Hood's grave inscription from a stone at Kirklees, given in mock-archaic spelling: "HEAR UNDERNEAD DIS LAITL STEAN LAIS ROBERT EARL OF HUNTINGTUN NEA ARCIR VER AS HIE SAE GEUD." Pyle's final line: "And now, dear friend, we also must part, for our merry journeyings have ended, and here, at the grave of Robin Hood, we turn, each going his own way."

What gives the book its peculiar greatness is the management of tone across that entire arc — boisterous comedy for two-thirds of the text, then a gradual darkening that never feels forced, arriving at genuine grief. Pyle earns the death because he has spent four hundred pages making you love the living man.

## Pass 2 — Character Profiles

**Robin Hood (Robert of Locksley, later Earl of Huntingdon).** A yeoman's son, eighteen when outlawed, never given a precise physical description beyond his speed and archery — Pyle deliberately keeps him slightly abstract so the reader can inhabit the legend. His defining quality is joy: he laughs in tight situations, improvises costumes at a moment's notice, speaks in genial insults, and maintains an almost theatrical refusal to take himself seriously. Yet his moral code is precise: he will not rob women, will not harm the poor, relieves from Church prelates and Norman lords with apparent relish, and shows consistent mercy toward enemies once they are beaten. His sole recorded cruelty — Guy of Gisbourne's death — is framed as the only killing he commits in the whole book, and it is done against a man who was paid to murder him. He is stubborn: he loses the quarterstaff fight with Little John, the stick fight with Arthur a Bland, and the buffet contest with King Richard, and each time he acknowledges the loss cleanly and makes his opponent a friend. His deepest loyalty is to the woodland life itself, and the book's final sorrow is that he cannot go home until he is dying.

**Little John.** Introduced as John Little of Hathersett, seven feet tall with a fist "the size of a good-sized ham." He defeats Robin on the log bridge fair and square, then shows enough humor about the subsequent dunking that Robin christens him on the spot and makes him second-in-command. His function in the band is physical anchor — the man who carries the fight when Robin needs a lieutenant — but Pyle gives him emotional depth in the final chapters. The scene where Little John hears the faint bugle notes across the forest, his "blood rushing like flame into his cheeks," and runs crashing through thorn and brier to reach Robin Hood, is one of the book's most purely affecting passages. When Robin is dying he holds his master's hand, tries to talk cheerfully about getting better, chokes on his own words. He promises no revenge on the nuns only because Robin gently presses the promise from him. He carries the body out at dawn without speaking.

**Friar Tuck.** Formally a hermit of the order at a small cell near the River Leen. Fat, enormously strong, inclined to drink, possessed of a hair-trigger temper and a deep vein of comedy. He is recruited by Robin in a sequence that involves Robin demanding to be carried across the river on the Friar's back (invoking the hermit's sworn duty of charity to travelers), whereupon the Friar dumps him in the water. The subsequent wrestling match ends inconclusively and both men collapse laughing. At the guild-feast for Allan a Dale's wedding he sits in the organ loft and booms the service so loudly the rafters shake. During King Richard's arrival at Nottingham he shouts blessings from the crowd so thunderously that the King laughs and says "I would that I had an army of such."

**Will Scarlet.** Robin's nephew, who had fled England after killing his father's steward in a dispute and lived abroad in foreign lands. Slender and handsome, dressed in scarlet silk when first met — he looks so unlike a fighter that Robin underestimates him completely and is beaten. Pyle uses Will as a gentle counterweight to the band's roughness: his voice is described as "soft" and "gentle" even when he is delivering buffet-punishments after archery misses, and King Richard observes that he looks "too fair and gentle for the greenwood." He eventually returns to his father's house once pardoned.

**Allan a Dale.** A minstrel, introduced sitting on a log weeping over the loss of his love Ellen to a forced marriage with Sir Stephen of Trent. He sings "passing well" — well enough that King Richard says his voice is sweeter than Blondell's (Richard's own legendary minstrel). His role in the band is bard and recorder; he would, Pyle implies, be the man to write down these very stories. He and Ellen follow Robin into royal service, and the last mention of them places them at Robin's side until the end.

**Ellen (the fair Ellen, Allan a Dale's love).** Described as "a fair and gentle maiden" from a yeoman family near the banks of the Rother. Her face at the forced wedding altar is compared to "a fair white lily snapped at the stem" and she watches the proceedings "in bitter despair, like the fawn that finds the hounds on her haunch." She is passive in the rescue scene but Pyle gives her dignity: she does not weep or faint; she stands silent and waits. The half-sixpence she and Allan swore on at the Rother is the book's most tender romantic token. After the wedding she follows Robin's fortunes for the rest of her life.

**Sir Richard of the Lea.** A broken knight from Castle Lea in Nottinghamshire. He sold his estate's mortgage to the Priory of Emmet to ransom his son from a Saracen prison; the Priory now wants to foreclose. When Robin lends him four hundred pounds he is stunned — he expected nothing and had told his wife to dress in plain gown "as befits a poor knight's dame." He repays the loan, recovers the land, and shows gratitude not by words but by acts: warning Robin when the King hunts him, risking royal displeasure to offer his castle as a refuge. His son Henry of the Lea appears at King Richard's court as a trusted knight.

**The Sheriff of Nottingham.** Never given a personal name; he is his office. Comic villain in most episodes — tricked into buying his own horses back, humiliated in his own hall, chased through Nottingham town with a gray goose shaft stuck in him "like a moulting sparrow with one feather in its tail." His actual menace is limited; he gets closest to victory in the Guy of Gisbourne sequence when he captures Little John, and even then Robin's disguise robs him of the prize. He dies in the final battle with a single sentence: "the first man slain in that fight was the Sheriff of Nottingham, for he fell from his horse with an arrow in his brain ere half a score of shafts had been sped."

**Guy of Gisbourne.** The book's only genuine villain with physical menace. A hired tracker who wears a cloak made of horse-hide, "with the tail hanging down behind" — Pyle's single most memorable costume. He is given very little dialogue and no backstory beyond his reputation as a mercenary assassin. His fight with Robin in the forest is the most dangerous moment in the book: Robin trips on a root, falls, and nearly loses the advantage. The outcome is brutal. "When the Sheriff of Nottingham saw whose face it was beneath Guy of Gisbourne's hood" — and Robin blew his bugle — the Sheriff turned his horse and fled without a word.

**King Richard (Coeur de Lion).** Appears only in the penultimate chapter, but Pyle makes his arrival grand — golden hair, blue eyes, a laugh that shakes the table. He is the ideal king imagined by every man the system has wronged: brave, direct, capable of recognizing a loyal heart beneath an outlaw's coat. His pardon of Robin is not charity but recognition. The feast in Sherwood is the book's warmest scene — a king who can laugh at being knocked flat by the man he has just pardoned, and who sleeps on green leaves under a forest tree.

## Pass 3 — Elaborated Scene: The Wedding in the Church at Gamewell

The chapter Pyle calls "Allan a Dale" reaches its climax in a small Norman church, unnamed, in the village of Gamewell. Robin Hood has gathered his band on the night before the wedding, learned the situation from Allan (who was found weeping on a log, dressed in worn gray where three weeks earlier he wore blue silk — the outward sorrow of a man whose luck had turned so completely he had dressed himself in his own grief), and laid his plan. The plan is audacious even by Robin's standards: stop a wedding, in a church, with priests and Norman men-at-arms present, substituting a minstrel for a lord.

Pyle describes the morning as bright and cool. The church fills with wedding guests — friends of Sir Stephen of Trent, the elderly knight, and Ellen's reluctant family. The Bishop of Hereford himself is officiating, which elevates the ceremony from social occasion to instrument of power; the Bishop's presence is a statement that the Church endorses this arrangement. Ellen arrives at the altar in white with flowers in her hair. Her face, Pyle writes, is like "a fair white lily snapped at the stem" — a bride who looks at the altar "in bitter despair, like the fawn that finds the hounds on her haunch." Sir Stephen, who is old and grey and presumably satisfied with the transaction, stands beside her. Everything in the scene is arranged to make resistance seem impossible.

Then the church doors open and Robin Hood walks in.

He is dressed as a harper — "a tattered wandering minstrel" in a short scarlet cloak faded to pink at the edges, his bow slung behind him and a harp in his hands, his face known to no one in the church except possibly the Bishop (who has met Robin at sword-point before and has reason to go pale). He walks up the nave without asking permission and stands between the altar and the congregation. Then he turns to the Bishop and speaks.

The dialogue Pyle gives him is not a demand or a threat. It is a social observation, delivered with perfect composure: "Here be lilies in the cheeks, and not roses such as befit a bonny bride." A minstrel's comment on the aesthetic insufficiency of the proceedings. The Bishop, to his credit, does not pretend to misunderstand. He asks sharply what Robin means by interrupting. Robin explains, still pleasantly, that a harper has the right of the hall at any feast or celebration, and that this feast seems to him insufficiently merry.

The men-at-arms begin to move. Robin puts two fingers to his lips.

From the choir loft, from the side doors, from the vestry entrance, his yeomen appear — twenty-four men in Lincoln green with bows already drawn, every shaft pointed inward. The Bishop of Hereford (who has, in an earlier episode, been forced to say mass in a greenwood chapel at arrowpoint and knows exactly what happens when he argues) sits down and ceases to speak. Sir Stephen of Trent goes the color of his own gray beard.

Robin now calls Allan a Dale from the back of the church. Allan walks up the nave — the minstrel who weeks ago was weeping in the forest, now walking toward the woman he loves in front of fifty witnesses with the broad backs of Robin Hood's men between him and every sword in the room. Ellen turns at the sound of his voice. Pyle does not give her dialogue at this moment; he does not need to. She "gave a low cry" and that is sufficient.

But there is a legal problem. Friar Tuck, in the choir loft with the organ, explains it loudly enough for the whole church to hear: under canon law a marriage requires the parties to be proclaimed on three separate occasions ("cried in church" three times) before it can be solemnized. Sir Stephen's marriage to Ellen was proclaimed once. It is not legally valid. The Bishop knows this perfectly well. He is being asked to perform an improper ceremony.

Friar Tuck, enjoying this enormously, solves the difficulty: he simply cries the banns three times himself in rapid succession, his voice booming off the stone vault, each proclamation louder than the last. The congregation has no choice but to witness. Robin nods. The Bishop, with no remaining avenue of protest, and with twenty-four arrows still aimed in his direction, performs the ceremony. Allan a Dale and Ellen are married in the church.

Then Robin Hood takes the heavy gold chain from the Bishop's neck — "a great gold chain" that marks the prelate's office and wealth — and clasps it around Ellen's throat. This is not robbing the poor. The Bishop of Hereford in Pyle's book is a specific kind of ecclesiastical corruption: a wealthy churchman who uses canon law as a tool of Norman social control. His gold chain going around a yeoman girl's neck is justice as theater, the redistribution of prestige as much as metal. Ellen stands at the altar with the Bishop's chain on, married to the man she chose, in a church that could not stop her.

What makes the scene immortal is not the cleverness of the plan but the tone. Throughout the confrontation Robin remains courteous — even cheerful. He does not threaten anyone directly. He never draws his own bow. He makes jokes. The men-at-arms never quite find their moment to charge because Robin never gives them a clean provocation; the thing is always just slightly more social occasion than armed standoff, until it is over and everyone is married and the soldiers are standing in a church looking at each other. This is Pyle's comedy at its purest: power defeated not by force but by a man who refuses to recognize its authority and is having too good a time to be stopped.

## Seven Quest Seeds

1. **The Wager at the King's Butts** — A royal forester challenges the party to a long-distance archery contest at the King's practice range, with a signed warrant as the prize; win the contest and receive free passage through Sherwood, but the forester has been told to "lose" the warrant and then arrest the winner for theft of royal property anyway. The party must either beat him fairly and survive the betrayal, or discover the trick before loosing a single shaft.

2. **The Debt at Emmet Priory** — Sir Richard of the Lea cannot pay the four hundred pounds he owes the Priory of Emmet by dawn or the monks will seize his lands and turn his household into the road; the party must travel by night through forest paths to deliver the silver before the Priory bell rings Prime. The challenge is not combat but terrain, time, and the corrupt cellarer who has hidden his own copy of the mortgage deed.

3. **Gray Goose at Nottingham Fair** — The Sheriff has announced a golden arrow as prize for an archery tournament in Nottingham town; entering under false names, the party competes while avoiding recognition by the Sheriff's men, and must decide whether to take the prize and slip away or do something memorable enough to let the Sheriff know exactly who won.

4. **The Horse-Hide Man** — Guy of Gisbourne has been given a description of the party and is tracking them through deep forest; he is better at woodcraft than anyone they have faced and will not be drawn into open combat on their terms. The only way to neutralize him is to find his trail before he finds theirs and spring a trap on a man who sets traps for a living.

5. **The Widow's Sons** — Three young men have been taken by the Sheriff on a poaching charge and will hang at noon from the oak tree at the edge of Nottingham common; Little John has gone in disguise to buy time, but his bow has split, and he has been captured. The party must reach him before the Sheriff decides to stop waiting and hang all four at once.

6. **The Prioress of Kirklees** — Robin Hood is feverish and has ridden alone to his cousin's nunnery for bloodletting; she has locked the door, and Little John, barred at the gate, heard three weak bugle notes an hour ago and nothing since. The massive oak door is studded with iron spikes and the nuns will not answer. Getting through the door is the problem; what lies behind it is worse.

7. **King Richard in the Forest** — Seven Black Friars are riding the road between Nottingham and Mansfield with a purse of a hundred pounds; the party is to stop them and take their toll, not knowing that one of the seven is the King of England conducting an impromptu test of the outlaws' loyalty. How the party behaves — whether they rob justly, what they say about King Richard when they do not know he is listening, and whether they give hospitality to strangers — will determine whether the band receives a royal pardon or a royal warrant.

## 5-Act Vignette Play — The Half-Sixpence

**Premise:** A fighter named Wulf serves as silent escort to Allan a Dale, who must carry a message and a broken coin (half a silver sixpence) to a girl in a village one day's march from Sherwood. The coin was snapped in two at the banks of the Rother the autumn before, when Allan and Ellen pledged their faith; he kept one half, she kept the other. If he can place his half against hers, the pledge is fulfilled and the marriage can proceed. But the Sheriff's men are moving on the road, and a travelling minstrel with a fighter escort is exactly the kind of suspicious arrangement that gets searched.

**TOKEN ITEM: The Half-Sixpence (Allan's fragment)**
A jagged half of a silver sixpence, small enough to fit under a tongue, worn smooth on the cut edge from being handled. Introduced in Act I in Allan's keeping. Changes hands in Act III (Wulf must conceal it when Allan is searched). Fulfilled in Act V when the two halves are pressed together.

---

### Act I — The Forest Path, Morning

*Stage direction: Allan a Dale and Wulf set out on the Nottingham road just after dawn. Allan carries his harp under one arm and pauses every few hundred yards to check behind. He holds the half-sixpence between his fingers, turning it in the light. He offers it to Wulf for safekeeping before they clear the treeline — not from cowardice, but from understanding that a minstrel may be searched and a fighter seldom is. Wulf takes it and closes his fist around it.*

*TOKEN STATE: Half-sixpence — in Wulf's left fist, then tucked into his boot.*

*Wulf's observation: The coin is lighter than he expected. He had imagined a love-token would have some weight to it.*

"Go gently, Wulf," said Allan a Dale, smiling a little though his eyes were anxious, "for if aught goes wrong this day, there be worse things a man can lose than his life, and I have lost enough already to know the difference."

---

### Act II — The Toll Gate, Midday

*Stage direction: A Sheriff's tollman and two armed riders stop them on the road outside the village of Blidworth, demanding a travel levy on minstrels and their "goods" (meaning the harp). Allan produces a penny; the tollman says it is insufficient and there is a warrant out for a minstrel matching Allan's description — yellow hair, blue cloak — travelling with a large companion. The armed riders begin to circle. Allan makes himself seem smaller. Wulf does not.*

*TOKEN STATE: Half-sixpence — in Wulf's boot, undisclosed.*

*Wulf's observation: He notices the tollman's ring — a Sheriff's livery ring, worth more than a week's levy. The man is not collecting tax; he is looking for someone specific.*

Wulf set his feet wide on the road and said nothing. The armed men looked at his shoulders and looked at each other. The smaller of them spat in the dust and looked away. The tollman cleared his throat. "Well," said he at last, "pass on — and mind your music plays for honest folk, not outlaws." He said it the way men say things they do not mean to be answered.

---

### Act III — The Inn at Blidworth, Afternoon

*Stage direction: They take shelter at an inn to wait for the riders to pass. A third man is already there — a royal warrant-server who is politely but thoroughly searching every traveller passing through for a package described as "a small metal object, likely a coin or token, carried by a minstrel." He examines Allan's pack, his harp, his belt-purse. Allan submits with steady nerves. The man then turns to Wulf.*

*TOKEN STATE: Half-sixpence — being extracted from Wulf's boot and palmed into his cheek during the search. Wulf's hands stay visible and open throughout.*

*Wulf's observation: He has held his breath under water for longer than this. He has not.*

The warrant-server was thorough, professional, and correct. He found nothing on either man and wrote a short note in his book before bowing and stepping aside. Allan and Wulf did not look at each other until the man had gone. Then Allan said, very quietly, "I owe thee a debt I shall not forget." Wulf worked the coin from his cheek with his tongue and placed it back in his boot without a word.

---

### Act IV — The Village of Gamston, Dusk

*Stage direction: They reach Ellen's father's house at the edge of Gamston as the last light fails. Her father meets them in the yard with a staff and the expression of a man who has decided to distrust everyone until further evidence persuades him otherwise. He is not hostile exactly; he is afraid — afraid of what may follow if the Sheriff's men come looking and find a minstrel in his house. Ellen herself appears at the door behind him, and Wulf sees her face change when she recognizes Allan's voice.*

*TOKEN STATE: Half-sixpence — returned from Wulf's boot to Allan's hand in the yard, in the dark, by touch.*

*Wulf's observation: He has escorted hostages, merchants, priests, a bishop who would not stop talking. He has not previously escorted a man who shook when he touched a coin.*

Her father stood between them for a long moment, his staff still in his hand. Then Ellen put her hand on his arm, once, gently, and he stepped aside. Allan and Ellen stood three feet apart in the dark doorway. The coin was in Allan's hand. Neither of them had said a word yet.

---

### Act V — The Yard at Dawn

*Stage direction: Wulf has kept watch through the night in the yard. At first light Allan comes out with the look of a man who has slept, which is to say he has not slept at all but has arrived somewhere beyond exhaustion at a kind of peace. He shows Wulf the rejoined coin: both halves pressed together, the crack still visible but the faces matching. Ellen is visible in the doorway behind him, wearing the coin on a cord. The father is in the yard now too, and he nods once at Wulf — the nod of a man revising an assessment.*

*TOKEN STATE: Half-sixpence — FULFILLED. Both halves rejoined on a cord around Ellen's neck.*

*Wulf's observation: The coin, whole now, is still lighter than he expected. He thinks it may always have weighed exactly right.*

Wulf shouldered his pack and said nothing. There was nothing to say. The coin had found the other half of itself, and that was what he had been hired to protect. He took the road back toward Sherwood alone, walking in the early sun, and if he had learned anything on this particular errand it was a thing that could not be put into words — something about the specific gravity of promises kept, and the roads men walk to keep them.
