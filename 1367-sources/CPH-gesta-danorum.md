## Pass 1 — Full Telling

Gesta Danorum is the book that invented Hamlet. It did not mean to. Saxo Grammaticus, a Danish cleric writing around 1200 under the patronage of Archbishop Absalon of Lund, was compiling Danish history from its mythological origins through the 12th century. He set down the story of Amleth in the third book as one more tale among many. Shakespeare found it — via Belleforest's French retelling — four hundred years later. The rest is canon. But the Hamlet story is not even Saxo's most interesting material. Starkad the Old is.

Saxo is writing in Latin, deliberately, at a time when Latin meant serious history and vernacular meant entertainment. He wants Scandinavian history to stand beside Roman and Greek history in the European scholarly tradition. He writes in an ornate, highly artificial Latin — deliberately archaic in places, deliberately complex in others — that reflects his awareness of classical models. He is also a rationalist of the medieval Christian type: he presents the Norse gods as ancient human kings who acquired divine reputation over time. Odin is not a god in Saxo's framework; he is an ancient con-man-king who persuaded the Danes to worship him. This euhemeristic reading means that every scene involving gods is simultaneously a scene involving very powerful, very cunning humans. It changes the texture of everything.

The work covers roughly the period from the legendary founding of Denmark to the reign of King Valdemar I (12th century). The first nine books are legendary; books ten through sixteen are increasingly historical. What follows covers the legendary books in detail and the historical books more briefly.

**Book One: Gram and the Foundation**

Dan and Angul are the legendary founders of Denmark and the Angles. Gram — grandson of Dan — is Denmark's first warrior-king. He wins his wife by defeating every rival suitor in single combat; he kills the giant-king Grønerus and takes his daughter; he is eventually killed by the giant-king Svipdag. Saxo is establishing the warrior-code from the first page: kingship is won and held in individual combat, not inherited. A king who cannot fight is already a dead king.

The tone is set: these are men living under the pressure of a universe that tests them constantly. The gods (presented as powerful ancient humans) arrange both the challenges and sometimes the outcomes. Gram is the archetype from which every subsequent hero will be measured against and found somehow less than.

**Book Two: Hadingus**

Hadingus is raised by the giant Hardgreip — a female giant who is also his foster-mother and who tries, in later years, to make him her lover. He refuses. She is killed. Hadingus is a wandering figure who keeps finding himself in the company of an old one-eyed man (clearly Odin) who teaches and guides and uses him. There is a famous passage where Hadingus is taken into the underworld by a woman and sees the shades of the dead warriors still fighting in the fields below. It is a descent narrative, strange and precise.

At the end of Hadingus's life, Odin (now revealed, or strongly implied) appears to him in a vision. Hadingus hangs himself in tribute — a willing death in imitation of Odin's own ritual hanging. The episode is one of the most haunting in the book: the man who has been guided by Odin all his life finally understands the nature of that guidance and enacts the same ritual on himself.

**Book Three: Frotho and Amleth**

Book Three contains the Amleth story, the most-read section of the entire work.

But Frotho precedes it. King Frotho I (Frode the Peaceful) is responsible for the legendary golden age of Danish history — the Frothi Peace — during which no theft or violence occurred anywhere in the kingdom because Frotho's laws were so exact and his justice so complete that crime ceased to function. A ring of gold could lie on the heath for years and no one would touch it. This sounds like idealization but Saxo tells it straight. The world under the right king really can be made orderly. Frotho kills a sea-serpent (the original dragon-kill that later sagas give to various heroes) and takes its treasure.

Then: Amleth.

Horwendil, a Danish chieftain, challenges King Koll of Norway to formal single combat. They fight. Horwendil kills Koll honorably. He is given the Danish king's daughter Gerutha as reward and prize. He and Gerutha have a son: Amleth.

Horwendil's brother Fengo becomes Fengo. He murders Horwendil — not in combat but by ambush and treachery. Then he marries Gerutha. He claims Horwendil was abusing her and he killed him to save her; nobody fully believes this but Fengo is now king and the story is established.

Amleth is a child. He understands exactly what happened. He feigns madness — total, convincing, degraded madness — because he knows that a sane man is a threat and a threat will be killed, and he cannot avenge his father if he is dead. He eats filth. He says things that are simultaneously insane and exactly correct, if you know how to read them. Fengo is not sure whether to be worried.

Fengo's attempts to expose Amleth:
First attempt: sends a beautiful girl to seduce Amleth, reasoning that a truly mad man would have no normal sexual response and a feigned-mad man would forget himself. Amleth's childhood friend warns him; Amleth has sex with the girl in a remote place away from witnesses; no one can prove anything.

Second attempt: hides a counselor beneath straw or rushes in Gerutha's chamber, planning to eavesdrop on Amleth's conversation with his mother. Amleth enters the room, suspects, makes rooster-crowing noises, jumps on the straw, stabs the spy, drags him out, dismembers him, and throws the pieces into the sewer. He then talks to his mother — lucidly, directly, about what Fengo did and what Amleth intends to do — and she weeps and promises to keep his secret.

Third attempt: Fengo sends Amleth to Britain with two companions carrying a sealed wooden tablet (or letter) ordering the British king to execute Amleth. Amleth reads the message while his companions sleep, erases it, writes a new message: execute my two companions, give me your daughter in marriage. Amleth's companions are executed. He marries the British princess. He returns to Denmark with gold.

At home: Amleth claims his own funeral feast has been arranged (he was supposed to be dead). He arrives at the feast, drinks and serves and is apparently drunk, and while the court is insensible sets fire to the hall and burns it with everyone in it. He kills Fengo personally with Fengo's own sword — because Amleth had previously made Fengo's actual sword immovable in its scabbard.

Amleth becomes king.

Saxo's Amleth then dies in a later conflict — he marries a second queen (the Queen of Scotland) who eventually betrays him to another rival king, Wiglek. He is killed in battle. The revenge was complete; the reign was brief; the ending is unsentimental.

**Book Four: Rolf Krake**

Rolf Krake (Hrolfr Kraki) is the greatest of the Danish kings — the one the sagas remember most, the one who appears in Beowulf as Hrothulf, lord of Heorot. His story in Saxo is tragic in the Greek sense.

His origin: his father Helgi raped his own daughter Yrsa, not knowing she was his daughter (she was raised away). Rolf was born of this incest. This is presented not as moral horror but as tragic irony — Rolf's greatness is, in some sense, built on a wrongness he had nothing to do with.

Rolf's twelve champions — the great warriors of Lejre — are the saga's version of Arthur's knights. They have names, specialties, histories. They are loyal beyond reason. The hall at Lejre (Hleiðra) under Rolf is the high-water mark of everything: the most beautiful hall, the most generous king, the finest warriors.

Rolf's half-sister Skuld marries Hjörvarðr, a Swedish king. She resents Denmark. She schemes. She arranges a war. Rolf rides out to meet Hjörvarðr's forces and they are betrayed — Skuld has used seiðr (magic) to raise an impossible army, and when Rolf's champions finally kill what seems to be Hjörvarðr, the body rises and keeps fighting. The champions die one by one. Rolf is killed. Everything ends.

Saxo tells this without sentimentality. The golden age closes because gold ages always close. The betrayal comes from inside the family because it always does. The only honest response to the loss of Rolf Krake is grief, and the saga offers no alternative.

**Book Six: Starkad**

Starkad the Old is Saxo's most philosophically complex character and the one who will generate the quest.

Born with six arms — the mark of monstrous supernatural origin — Thor trimmed him to two because Thor resented Odin's affection for him. Odin gave him three human lifetimes. For each lifetime, Thor imposed a curse: he would commit one act of treachery.

First life, first treachery: Starkad was companion and champion to King Vikar of Norway. Odin arranged a situation that required a sacrifice. Starkad performed what was supposed to be a mock ritual — symbolic hanging, soft reed as noose, no real danger. Odin made the reed into a real rope, the bent tree into a gallows, and Vikar hanged. Starkad thrust a reed-stalk at the king as the "spear" — it became a real spear. Vikar died. Starkad was exiled in shame from Norway and wandered for a generation.

Second treachery: the Battle of Brávellir. The great battle between Harald Wartooth and his nephew Ring. A massive slaughter — Saxo lists champion after champion who fell. Harald died at Odin's own hand (Odin drove his chariot and struck the killing blow — Harald had won victories through Odin's help and now Odin collected his end of the bargain). Starkad fled the battle. This was the second treachery. He was not a coward; the compulsion was divine. He knew this and it did not make it better.

Third treachery: the worst. Starkad was hired — for a large fee of gold — to kill King Áli of Uppsala. Áli was a good king. Gentle, generous to his men, guilty of nothing. Starkad took the gold and entered Áli's hall and killed him. Áli barely had time to be surprised. He fell saying almost nothing.

Starkad rode away immediately. He had the gold. He would carry the knowledge of what he had done for the rest of his very long life — centuries more, in some reckonings. He never stopped wandering. He never stopped fighting other people's battles. He gave away the gold in fragments, as if distributing small payments against an infinite debt. It did not help.

At the very end: Starkad is ancient beyond reckoning, his body ruined by three lifetimes of wounds healed wrong. He can barely walk. He cannot die in a sickbed. He needs a warrior's death — to be struck down in arms — but no one will give it to him. He is too famous. To kill Starkad is to invite the hatred of everyone who loved him; to let him live unmolested is the practical choice. He is trapped alive by his own reputation.

He eventually finds a young man, Hatherus, and offers him gold: strike the death blow. The young man agrees. Starkad is decapitated. His body, still moving on instinct even without a head, lurches toward the young man — and Hatherus kicks the head away. The instinct dies. Starkad's long war with his own fate is finally over.

**Books Seven–Eight: Brávellir and the Late Kings**

Book Eight contains Saxo's great set-piece: the Battle of Brávellir. Harald Wartooth against Ring. A champion catalogue that runs for pages — every notable warrior of the age with his specific fighting style and fate listed. Harald, ancient and blind by this point, has won so many battles through Odin's help that when Odin finally appears as his charioteer and strikes him with his own scepter, the reader understands: the deal is being settled. The death is the cost of the victories.

**Books Nine–Sixteen: The Historical Period**

From around Book Nine the legendary texture gives way to more historically attested material — Danish kings with datable reigns, conflicts with the Carolingian Franks, missionary Christianity arriving, the gradual shift from the pagan warrior-code to Christian kingship. Saxo continues his euhemeristic project: even in the historical sections, the gods-as-humans framework structures the interpretation. The later books include material relevant to the conflict between Denmark and its neighbors, the consolidation of royal power, and eventually the 12th-century politics that are Saxo's own near-present.

The last books read differently — more chronicle, less epic — but they were the politically urgent ones for Saxo's patron Archbishop Absalon. They established the legitimate Danish royal lineage, the authority of the Danish church, and the historical basis for Danish claims against Norway and Sweden.

### Chapter Notes (By Book)

**Book 1** — Legendary foundation: Dan and Angul; King Gram's warrior-marriage code established. Sets the premise: kings are won through combat, held through combat, lost through failure of combat.

**Book 2** — Hadingus: the man shaped by Odin from youth; the descent to the underworld; the voluntary death in imitation of the god. Introduces the divine-human relationship as one of use and tribute simultaneously.

**Book 3** — Frotho the Peaceful and Amleth. Frotho: the ideal king, the gold ring on the heath, the dragon-kill. Amleth: the feigned madman who is the only sane person in the story; the death-order letter altered; the hall-burning; the brief reign and death. Saxo's most-read section; the direct source for Hamlet.

**Book 4** — Rolf Krake, his champions, and Skuld's betrayal. The Danish Arthurian moment: the hall, the twelve great warriors, the traitor from within the family. Narrative function: establishes Lejre's golden age and its destruction, against which all subsequent Danish history is measured.

**Book 5** — Transitional kings connecting Rolf's age to the Starkad period; some genealogical material that is locally important but narratively thin.

**Book 6** — Starkad the Old: three lifetimes, three divine gifts, three compelled treacheries. The killing of Vikar; the flight from Brávellir; the killing of Áli for gold. The ancient warrior who cannot die; the final hired death. The most morally complex figure in the work.

**Books 7–8** — Harald Wartooth and the Battle of Brávellir. The champion catalogue; Odin driving the chariot; the collected payment. Scale and spectacle; the last great pagan battle.

**Books 9–12** — The transition to more historical material; Danish relations with the Franks; Christianity arriving from the south; the gradual shift in how kings justify their power.

**Books 13–16** — The historical Danish kings up to Saxo's own era; the consolidation of the Danish church; Bishop Absalon's campaigns and administrative authority; contemporary politics given historical legitimacy. These books are where Saxo's political purpose is most visible.

## Pass 2 — Characters, Choices, Tone

**Starkad the Old** — The figure who will not resolve. Saxo gives him the longest continuous treatment of any character in the legendary books, and it is because Starkad refuses to be one thing. He is brave (genuinely, without performance — the courage of a man who has been afraid so many times that fear has become familiar and manageable). He is loyal (his grief over Vikar's death, over the flight from Brávellir, over Áli's killing is real and long-sustained). He is treacherous (three times, by compulsion, and each time he knows exactly what he is doing and does it anyway). Saxo does not resolve these facts into a judgment. He presents them in sequence and lets the weight accumulate. The result is the most honest portrait of moral complexity in the entire saga tradition.

**Amleth** — The intelligence at work inside helplessness. What Amleth does with feigned madness is not just survival strategy; it is a sustained philosophical position about how to live in a kingdom where truth cannot be spoken directly. Every insane act contains its own precise meaning if you know the code. When he says things that simultaneously make no sense and make perfect sense, Saxo is showing us a mind that has learned to speak two languages simultaneously — the one the court can hear and the one that is actually true. Shakespeare saw this and magnified it. The original is colder, more tactical, less interested in the inner life.

**Rolf Krake** — The ideal that the world destroys. He is not flawed in a tragic sense; he is destroyed by someone else's flaw (Skuld's resentment). This is the distinction between saga tragedy and Greek tragedy: here the hero does not fall because of internal failure but because the world contains people like Skuld, and generosity cannot protect against betrayal by a half-sister who has decided to be an enemy. Rolf's tragedy is that he built something genuinely good and it was not enough.

**Gerutha** — The woman in the story who is done to, rather than doing. She was Horwendil's wife, then Fengo's wife, then the queen who helped Amleth keep his secret. Saxo is not much interested in her interiority but she is present at every hinge: she was the prize Horwendil won; she was the witness Fengo had to control; she was the person Amleth finally told the truth to. Her role is to hold the knowledge and not speak it until the moment when speaking it is the same as action.

**Saxo himself** — The authorial voice in Gesta Danorum is more present than in most of the sagas. Saxo editorializes, makes Latin classical references, inserts comparisons to Roman history. He is making an argument as he writes: that the Danes have a history worth standing beside Rome's. His condescension toward paganism is formal (it is Christian Latin history after all) but his actual treatment of the pagan material is often respectful and exact. He saves the material he might have suppressed. He tells Starkad's story with all three treacheries intact, in sequence, without simplification. That is the book's most significant moral act.

**Tone of the world** — Gesta Danorum has a different texture from the Icelandic sagas. The Icelandic sagas are spare, paratactic, cold. Saxo is rhetorically elaborate, sometimes overheated, sometimes moralizing. But underneath the Latin ornament the stories have the same bone structure: fate operates, divine influence is real and not benign, the best men are used by forces that don't care about them, and the honest record of what happened is the only form of respect available for the dead.

## Pass 3 — The Killing of King Áli

Starkad has already taken the gold. He took it in a moment of — not weakness exactly; weakness would require that he was uncertain. He was not uncertain. He knew what the gold was for. He took it with full knowledge, which is the worst way to take something like that.

He rides to Uppsala.

Áli's hall is warm. It is always warm in the version Saxo tells: Áli is the king who keeps his hearth-fire generous, who gives his men good ale, who is not feared because there is nothing to fear from him. He is the good king — not Rolf's great golden-age king, not Frotho's law-perfecting king, but the quiet kind: present, reliable, easy to be near. The kind of king whose death would be felt immediately in every small way, the way a fire going out in winter is felt.

Starkad dismounts. His body at this point in his life is a record of three lifetimes of war — scars stacked on scars, old wounds that didn't close cleanly, limbs that work but not the way they once did. He is extraordinary and he is ruined, simultaneously, and has been for a very long time.

He enters the hall. Áli turns and sees him and is pleased. Starkad is a famous champion; to have Starkad in your hall is an honor. Áli's face does the thing faces do when something genuinely good surprises them.

Starkad looks at him.

Everything in him says stop.

He has killed men in anger, in grief, in loyalty, in defense, in the formal logic of battle where the killing and the killed understand each other. He has never done this: entered a warm hall and looked at a man who was glad to see him and then—

He does it.

The sword comes out. Áli has almost no time to change his expression. He falls. He says — in some versions — something mild and surprised, almost a question. Not angry. He didn't have time to be angry. He didn't have time to understand what was happening before it was over.

Starkad walks out. He gets on his horse. He has the gold somewhere and it is already the worst thing he has ever owned.

He will carry the knowledge of that room for the rest of his long life — which is very long, centuries in some reckonings, more years than any man should have to live with anything. He gives the gold away in pieces over decades, as if distributing small payments against an infinite debt. Each piece given away is a piece of the gold and none of it changes the arithmetic. The gold was spent the moment he touched the sword. There is no giving it back.

Later, when he is very old and wants to die and cannot find anyone who will strike the blow, he will think about Áli's hall. He will think about the fact that Áli's face was glad when he walked in. He will think: the hall was warm.

He cannot un-enter it. He cannot make what he did there be different from what it was. The only thing he can do is tell the truth about it, exactly, without simplification, when the time comes — and hope that the record that survives is his version rather than the court's version, which is cleaner and leaves out the arithmetic.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

1. **Starkad's Account** — Ancient Starkad dictates his sealed testimony before dying and hires the Fighter to carry it through those who benefit from the simplified heroic version of his life to the memorial-keepers at Lejre; the authentic record, with all three treacheries intact, is the only honest witness to what a divided life actually was.

2. **Amleth's Altered Letter** — The Fighter is Fengo's independent courier hired to ensure the sealed tablet reaches the British king intact, not knowing Amleth has already altered it during the sea crossing; discovery mid-journey forces a decision about which version of the message to deliver.

3. **Horwendil's Victory Record** — Before Fengo can suppress it, the Fighter must carry the authenticated record of Horwendil's lawful combat-victory over King Koll to the Danish archive at Lejre; Fengo's agents are on the road behind.

4. **Gerutha's Testimony** — After Amleth kills Fengo, his mother Gerutha's sworn statement confirming Fengo murdered Horwendil must reach the lawspeaker before Fengo's kinsmen file a counter-claim; the Fighter carries it through marsh and marshaled opposition.

5. **Rolf's Final Ring** — On the eve of the battle where Rolf Krake will die, he gives his champion Hjalti a ring and a sealed message for the nearest allied king; not asking for help — just a farewell and an accounting; the Fighter is Hjalti's escort through Skuld's forward positions.

6. **The Scald's True Song** — Two versions of Starkad's life exist after his death: the court scald's heroic simplification (three great deeds, noble end) and Starkad's own account (three compelled treacheries, a body that wouldn't die); the Fighter must carry Starkad's version to the archive before the court's version is inscribed as official record.

7. **The Vikar Witness** — A survivor of Vikar's court — old, barely alive — carries the only non-Starkad testimony about the hanging; he wants it delivered to Starkad's family records to show that those present knew it was Odin's doing, not simply Starkad's betrayal; the Fighter carries this exonerating (and damning) document through Starkad's surviving enemies.

**Theme Check**

**Avoiding**: "Fate moves through the broken thing" (FRO) — Starkad is compelled by fate but the theme here is not about fate's agency; it is about the actor's own documentation of what was done through him. "Honor vs. shame" (MAD, GDN, BHD) — those are about social codes and their costs; this is about divine compulsion and the problem of posthumous reduction. "Truth delivered posthumously" (LGW) — Starkad is not yet dead when he writes the account; the document is sent ahead of his death; and it is not love-truth but self-testimony against simplification.

**Chosen Quest: Storyline 1 — "Starkad's Account"**

**New Theme**: *The man fate wrote for betrayal is still the man who betrayed: divine compulsion is not absolution, and the only record that resists simplification into hero or traitor is the one written by the actor himself, before the court's version is inscribed.*

This is distinct from all 21 existing themes because it concerns the specific problem of a divided life being posthumously reduced to a single narrative by people with interests in that reduction — and the actor's own testimony as the only possible counter.

### The Vignette: "Starkad's Account"

**NODE ROUTE**: STK → coastal road (STK, second act) → DAN (harbor settlement) → DAN (road to Lejre) → HEO  
**TOKEN**: Starkad's Sealed Testimony — a wax-sealed document in Starkad's own runic inscription; his account of all three treacheries (Vikar's sacrifice, the flight from Brávellir, Áli's killing) written from the only perspective that was present for all of them; addressed to the memory-keepers of the Danish court; neither apology nor justification — testimony, which is different from both.

**ACT 1 — What Starkad Cannot Simplify**  
*Node: STK (Starkad's Farmstead — the desolate holding at the edge of the settled world where the ancient warrior ends his days)*

The farmstead is barely a farmstead. One building. The fire is adequate. Starkad sits in the one chair that can hold him — enormous, ruined, his body a map of three lifetimes of wounds healed wrong. He does not waste time on explanation. He gives you the sealed document and says: carry this to the memory-keepers at Lejre. Do not let the court scald see it. The scald has his own version. His version leaves things out. This one does not.

He does not say what things. He does not ask for your discretion. He is simply naming the problem and the solution and trusting you to be the distance between them.

*Skill Check: Insight (WIS) DC 12*

**Pass**: You read his stillness correctly. This is not a man making peace with what he has done — there is no peace available for it, and he has known that long enough to stop looking. This is a man ensuring that the record that survives is the one that actually happened. You take the document. You understand why it matters.  
**Fail**: He sees you trying to read him and says: it is a document. You carry documents. That is the work. He does not say anything else. You take it.

*Grant item: Starkad's Sealed Testimony (wax-sealed, rune-inscribed, heavy; smells faintly of ash and old iron)*  
*Activate mission bit: CPH_questActive*

**ACT 2 — Hrærekr's Chase**  
*Node: STK (coastal path, north shore)*

The young man who struck the death-blow — you haven't learned his name yet; he goes by Hrærekr in this district — comes after you on the coastal path. He ran off with Starkad's gold immediately after the killing, as agreed, but he has had an hour on the road to think about what a sealed document leaving Starkad's farmstead might contain. He is worried it contains evidence of their arrangement. He is moving fast and he has convinced himself that catching you is simpler than whatever comes after.

*Skill Check: Persuasion (CHA) DC 13*

**Pass**: You tell him what the document contains, which is not his name — Starkad would not waste a word on the hired blow; it is the three great treacheries that are in there, and his name isn't one of them. He slows down. He thinks about this. He lets you go. He will spend years wondering if you were telling the truth, which is its own punishment.  
**Fail**: He doesn't believe you and comes in swinging. You end the fight without killing him — he is scared, not evil, and Starkad would find it undignified if the document's carrier left bodies on the coastal path. He lies in the bracken. You walk on.

*Combat (if triggered): Hrærekr (the hired killer)*  
**AC**: 12 | **HP**: 18 | An opportunist, not a warrior; he overestimates what he can do and underestimates what he is afraid of. He fights badly when frightened.

*Grant item: Hrærekr's bronze cloak-pin (drops when he falls; a common piece; the kind of thing young men wear when they want to look older than they are)*

**ACT 3 — The Scald's Offer**  
*Node: DAN (harbor settlement — a coastal trading village between Starkad's holding and Lejre)*

The court scald finds you at the harbor settlement. He was not following you; he has been working this route for weeks, which means someone told him to expect a document. He is well-dressed, professionally charming, and genuinely believes what he is about to say. He tells you he has spent six months writing the definitive account of Starkad's life. He will show you it in exchange for the sealed document — not to suppress Starkad's version, he says, but to reconcile it with the historical record. He is very good at the word reconcile.

*Skill Check: Persuasion (CHA) DC 14*

**Pass**: You ask him what he would reconcile the Áli killing with. He pauses — not because he doesn't have an answer, but because his answer requires more words than the pause reveals he has ready. You hand him nothing. He watches you walk away with the specific frustration of a man who was told this would be easy.  
**Fail**: He makes his case well enough that you hesitate; he sees the hesitation and increases the offer. You still say no, but late and visibly, which costs you something you can't quite name — the document still gets through, but less cleanly.

*Grant item: A harbor toll-stamp (the harbourmaster's wax impression on a travel strip; proves you passed through the legal settlement; useful at road-checkpoints)*

**ACT 4 — The Court's Outriders**  
*Node: DAN (road to Lejre, open marshland)*

Three men on horseback are posted on the road ten miles outside Lejre. They are not robbers — they are wearing the court colors. Someone with authority sent them ahead to receive any documents traveling toward the memory-keepers. Their instruction is probably: bring all documents to the king's steward first, to be assessed before archiving. This is a normal procedure that is also, in this case, a mechanism for suppression. They ask you to hand over what you're carrying for official conveyance.

*Combat: Court outriders (×3)*  
**AC**: 14 | **HP**: 16 each | Professional, well-armored, neither cruel nor particularly committed. They are doing a job. They will not fight to the death for an administrative instruction but they will fight to do their job properly.

*Skill Check after combat: Athletics (STR) DC 12 — get past the last outrider while keeping the sealed document intact.*  
**Pass**: Clean through. The document arrives sealed.  
**Fail**: The document is slightly damaged — the wax seal is cracked — but the inscription is intact. Embarrassing but deliverable.

*Grant item: A court travel-pass (taken from the outriders; useful at the final gate)*

**ACT 5 — The Archive**  
*Node: HEO (Lejre — the Danish Royal Hall's archive chamber, adjacent to the great hall)*

The memory-keeper is a small, precise, old man in a room full of wooden boards and vellum strips. He has been in this room for thirty years recording what kings want recorded. He looks at the cracked seal (if applicable), at the inscription, at your travel-pass, and then he opens the document and reads it. He takes a long time reading it. When he is done he sets it on the recording table — the place where things go that will be copied onto vellum permanently. He says: this will be recorded as delivered. Then he gives you something for your trouble, and you walk out into the great hall where the carved beams still hold the stories of the gods, and Starkad's own name is somewhere in those stories, and now so is his version.

*Skill Check: Insight (WIS) DC 12*  
**Pass**: You see the memory-keeper make a small notation in the margin of his current register — not the content of the document, just the date and the provenance and the fact that it arrived sealed. He is a professional. The document will be inscribed exactly as written, without the court's improvements.  
**Fail**: You cannot read whether his notation is protective or procedural. The document is accepted. You have done what you were sent to do. Whether the full truth survives after this is a question for a different carrier.

*Take item: Starkad's Sealed Testimony*  
*Grant item: The memory-keeper's archive token — a small bone disk with a stylized hand impressed on it; the sign of something properly received and entered into the record*  
*Activate mission bit: CPH_questComplete*
