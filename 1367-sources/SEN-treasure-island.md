## Pass 1 — Full Telling

I finished this book and I kept thinking about Silver at the end of the supper scene — sitting back half out of the firelight, eating heartily, joining quietly in the laughter. Bland, polite, obsequious. He had killed men. He had planned to kill everyone at the table. And here he was, passing the salt. That image doesn't leave you.

Treasure Island opens as a frame story: Jim Hawkins, now grown, writes down what happened that one summer because certain people have asked him to. What happened is this: a broken sailor named Billy Bones rented a room at Jim's parents' inn on the English coast — the Admiral Benbow — and lived there in a state of loud, rum-soaked dread, watching the road for a "one-legged man." Billy sang one song and one song only: *fifteen men on the dead man's chest, yo-ho-ho and a bottle of rum*. He paid Jim sixpence a week to watch for that one-legged man. Then a blind beggar named Blind Pew found him, put a folded paper in his hand, and left. Billy Bones looked at the paper, said "The black spot — I've got six hours," and died of a stroke on the parlor floor.

The black spot is a pirate death warrant. You receive one, you're finished.

Jim and his mother go through Billy's sea-chest before Pew's gang arrives. They find a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Pew and his men come — they trash the inn looking for it — Pew is trampled by galloping horses in the road while escaping a government revenue officer. That death is quick and brutal and entirely without ceremony. Jim takes the oilcloth packet to Squire Trelawney and Dr. Livesey. Inside the packet: a detailed map of an island where Captain Flint, the most feared pirate of his generation, buried his treasure. Gold and silver, seven hundred thousand pounds.

The Squire is a rich fool who immediately goes to Bristol and starts hiring a ship and crew while talking, loudly, about buried treasure to everyone he meets. The doctor is wiser but cannot stop him. They get a ship — the Hispaniola — and a captain, Smollett, who distrusts Trelawney's loose tongue from the first. They also get a cook: Long John Silver, one-legged, with a parrot on his shoulder named Captain Flint. Silver is everything at once. He's jovial, helpful, fatherly with Jim, brilliant at managing men, and the ringleader of a mutiny he's been organizing since Bristol.

Jim is young enough and observant enough to find out. One night, too hot to sleep in his cabin, he climbs inside an empty apple barrel on deck. He can't get out. Silver sits down against the barrel and talks. Jim, hidden inside, hears everything: Silver's plan, who's with him, when they'll move (after the treasure is found and loaded), and how. Jim brings this to Smollett. The captain listens, thanks him, and says: do nothing yet. We're outnumbered. We need to get to the island first.

This is the structural heart of the book. For three hundred pages, you read a thriller in which the good guys know the mutiny is coming, the mutineers don't know that they know, and both sides play an intricate pretense of normalcy. Stevenson is very good at suspense.

They reach the island. Before the mutiny can be formally launched, Jim slips ashore and vanishes into the trees. He meets Ben Gunn — a castaway, one of Flint's former crew, marooned three years ago on this island when he made the mistake of suggesting they come back for the treasure. Ben Gunn is half-wild, lives on berries and goat meat, and has been using his three years productively: he found the treasure, dug it up, and moved it to a cave by the two-pointed hill. He tells Jim this almost in passing. Jim barely registers it. He doesn't tell anyone.

Meanwhile, the mutiny breaks open. The honest men — Smollett, Trelawney, Livesey, a few loyal sailors — take the island's stockade and raise the Union Jack. The pirates attack. Joyce is killed. Hunter is beaten so badly he dies the next day. Smollett is wounded. The pirates are repulsed but the honest men are down to five able-bodied fighters. It's a siege.

Then Jim slips away again, this time at night, alone, without telling anyone. This is the move that earns him the doctor's coldest rebuke later: *when Smollett was well you didn't dare go off; when he was ill and couldn't help it, by George, it was cowardly.* Jim finds Ben Gunn's coracle — a tiny wooden bowl of a boat — and uses it at night to cut the Hispaniola's anchor rope and set her adrift. Then, rocking half-mad in the coracle for an entire night at sea, he wakes to find himself alongside the drifting Hispaniola. He climbs aboard. Below decks: Israel Hands, wounded; O'Brien, dead, killed by Hands in a drunken fight. Jim and Hands cut a deal — Jim will sail the ship to a safe berth, Hands will get medical attention. They beach the Hispaniola at North Inlet. Then Hands retrieves a hidden dirk, creeps up the shrouds after Jim, throws it, and pins Jim's shoulder to the mast. The jolt of the blade loosens Jim's grip. He fires both pistols simultaneously. Hands falls into the sea. The dirk drops out of Jim's shoulder when Jim shudders. Jim climbs down, crosses the island, returns to the stockade at night — and finds it held by the pirates. Silver's parrot, perched in the dark, screams *Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!* and wakes everyone.

This is where Silver becomes the most interesting figure in the book. He protects Jim from his own crew. He talks them down when they want to cut Jim's throat. He privately tells Jim: *you're my last card and I'm yours. Back to back.* This is entirely cynical — Silver needs Jim as a potential witness who might save him from the gallows — and it's also, somehow, something else. Silver does what he says he will do. He keeps Jim alive. He endures the Black Spot a second time, produces the treasure chart (which the doctor, inexplicably, has given him), and talks his crew back into following him by sheer force of personality. He is fifty times more capable than anyone on either side.

The doctor comes every day to treat the sick pirates — Silver has made a deal, exchanged the stockade and stores for permission to move freely. This deal makes no sense to Jim or to us until the end: Livesey had already gotten Ben Gunn's secret out of him the previous afternoon. The treasure is already moved. The chart is worthless. Livesey gives it to Silver because it buys safe passage to where they need to be, and because when the pirates follow that map to an empty hole, they'll be broken. The doctor is playing chess three moves ahead.

The treasure hunt is one of the great setpieces in English adventure fiction. Six pirates, Jim on a rope, Silver in the lead, crossing the island under hot sun. They find a skeleton laid out by Flint's hand with mathematical precision — feet pointing south-west, hands raised above the head, the whole skeleton a compass needle aimed at the island where the bearing was to be taken. Flint laid this out himself, alone, after killing the six men who'd helped him bury the treasure. Silver reads the bearing: *this is Flint's joke.* The pirates are terrified. And then, from the trees, a thin high voice begins singing: *fifteen men on the dead man's chest — yo-ho-ho and a bottle of rum.* Flint's own song. The pirates are white-faced, paralyzed. Silver is the only one who keeps his nerve and reasons aloud that a ghost wouldn't have an echo — and someone identifies the voice: it's Ben Gunn, imitating Flint. They shake it off and march on.

The empty treasure pit. The boards of the packing cases, the shaft of a pick broken in two, the branded name WALRUS — Flint's ship. Seven hundred thousand pounds: gone. The pirates look at Silver, and Silver has already moved. He slips Jim a pistol. They put the pit between themselves and the mutineers. Then musket fire from the trees: the doctor, Gray, and Ben Gunn, ambushing. Two pirates dead in ten seconds. Silver shoots Merry at close range as he goes down. Three survivors run for the beach.

The rest is resolution. Ben Gunn's cave: Smollett by the fire, and in the far corner, barely visible in the firelight, heaps of gold coins and stacked bars of silver. Three days of loading. Silver is allowed to walk free about camp — everyone is civil to him and nobody trusts him. The three surviving mutineers are left on the island with supplies; they can't be taken home without hanging them, and they can't be taken safely aboard. They kneel on the beach as the Hispaniola sails away, and one of them — furious, desperate — fires a musket shot over Silver's head through the main sail. Then they're gone.

At a Spanish American port, Silver cuts through a bulkhead at night, takes a sack containing three or four hundred guineas from the treasure, gets into a shore boat with Ben Gunn's connivance, and disappears. They are all, says Jim, quite pleased to be quit of him so cheaply.

The book ends with the accounting: Smollett retires. Gray becomes a ship's officer and a family man. Ben Gunn gets a thousand pounds and spends it in nineteen days — he was given a lodge-keeper's job after, and is apparently very good at singing in church. Silver is gone — Jim hopes he found his old Negress and lives in comfort with her and the parrot, "though his chances of comfort in another world are very small." The bar silver and the arms Flint buried separately are still on the island. Jim won't go back for them. His worst dreams are the sound of surf on that island's coasts, and Silver's parrot screaming in the dark: *Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!*

---

**Historical moment:** Stevenson wrote this in 1881–1883, serialized in a children's magazine called *Young Folks* before book publication. He was thirty when he started it, ill with tuberculosis most of his adult life, living in drafty Scottish houses and later in France. He drew the map first — literally sketched a treasure island for his stepson Lloyd's entertainment one rainy afternoon — and the story followed the map. It is not a historical novel; its setting is vague 18th century. But it belongs to a specific Victorian anxiety about the British colonial project: what happens when the violence that built the empire turns on the empire-builders? Flint's treasure is the accumulated product of murder, slave trafficking, and piracy — and the honest men sail into that world and come back rich. Nobody is clean. The book doesn't moralize about this, which is part of why it has lasted.

It is also a book about fathers. Jim has no real father (his actual father dies early, barely mentioned). He accumulates father figures: Silver, who lies to him; Smollett, who is fair but cold; Trelawney, who is warm but foolish; Livesey, who is the most honest and the least warm. None of them are adequate. Jim survives by outgrowing all of them.

**Themes:** Greed as the engine of the plot — every death in the book is downstream of someone wanting gold. The complexity of villainy — Silver is not a symbol, he's a person, and Stevenson refuses to simplify him. The cost of being the one who knows — from the apple barrel onward, Jim carries information that endangers him. The gap between adventure as imagined and adventure as lived — Jim wants the romance of pirates right up until they start killing people he knows. And most quietly: what it costs to grow up, and the fact that you can't go back once you know what death looks like.

### Chapter Notes

**Ch. I–II** (The Old Sea-Dog at the Admiral Benbow): Billy Bones arrives at the Benbow, pays Jim sixpence a week to watch for a one-legged man, drinks rum and terrifies the customers; the atmosphere of dread is established before a single pirate arrives.

**Ch. III** (The Black Spot): The blind beggar Pew delivers the Black Spot to Billy Bones; Billy dies of apoplexy before the pirates can collect him; the death of the book's first catalyst, announced with a piece of blackened Bible verse.

**Ch. IV–VI** (The Sea-Chest / The Last of the Blind Man / The Captain's Papers): Jim and his mother raid Billy's chest as the inn is attacked; they get the oilcloth packet; Blind Pew is run down by revenue horses in the road; the packet goes to Trelawney and Livesey — it's a treasure map.

**Ch. VII–VIII** (I Go to Bristol / At the Sign of the Spy-Glass): Trelawney hires the Hispaniola and its crew; Jim meets Long John Silver at the Spy-Glass Inn; first impression is entirely favorable — Silver is warm, capable, father-like; Jim has no idea.

**Ch. IX–XI** (Powder and Arms / The Voyage / What I Heard in the Apple Barrel): The ship sails; Jim, sleepless in the heat, hides in an empty apple barrel; overhears Silver, Dick, and Hands planning the mutiny in full; Jim's childhood ends in that barrel.

**Ch. XII** (Council of War): Jim brings the news to Smollett, Trelawney, Livesey; they resolve to wait; the pretense of normalcy continues; land is sighted.

**Ch. XIII–XIV** (How My Shore Adventure Began / The First Blow): Jim slips ashore impulsively and vanishes into the island's trees; meets Ben Gunn; hears a shot and a scream — one of the honest men killed; the mutiny has formally begun.

**Ch. XV–XVI** (The Man of the Island / Narrative Continued by the Doctor: How the Ship Was Abandoned): The doctor's party finds the stockade; Smollett decides to hold it; they move supplies from ship to shore under fire; Captain Flint (the parrot) keeps screaming at the worst moments.

**Ch. XVII–XXI** (Narrative Continued by the Doctor: End of the First Day's Fighting): The honest men hold the blockhouse under the Union Jack; pirate assault repulsed; Joyce killed, Hunter mortally beaten, Smollett wounded; the arithmetic of survival is grim — five able men versus twenty.

**Ch. XXII–XXIV** (How My Shore Adventure Began / The Ebb-tide Runs / The Cruise of the Coracle): Jim slips away at night, finds Ben Gunn's coracle, cuts the Hispaniola adrift in the dark; drifts alone at sea until morning; spots the unmanned ship nearby.

**Ch. XXV–XXVII** (I Strike the Jolly Roger / Israel Hands / "Pieces of Eight"): Jim boards the Hispaniola; finds Hands wounded, O'Brien dead; they sail together to North Inlet; Hands attacks, pins Jim to the mast with a thrown dirk; Jim shoots Hands; Hands falls into the sea dead; Jim descends and heads back to the stockade.

**Ch. XXVIII–XXIX** (In the Enemy's Camp / The Black Spot Again): Jim walks into a stockade now held by pirates; Silver's parrot wakes everyone; Silver protects Jim, makes the deal; the pirates give Silver the Black Spot again; Silver produces the chart and talks his way back to captain.

**Ch. XXX** (On Parole): Dr. Livesey arrives in the morning to treat the sick; Silver arranges for Jim to speak with Livesey privately; Jim is scolded, weeps, refuses the doctor's offer to run; tells Livesey where the ship is; Livesey hints that all is not as it seems — Ben Gunn's secret is now in play.

**Ch. XXXI–XXXII** (The Treasure-hunt — Flint's Pointer / The Voice Among the Trees): The treasure hunt; the skeleton laid out as a compass bearing; Ben Gunn's voice imitating Flint terrorizes the pirates; Silver reasons them down; the third tall tree is in sight.

**Ch. XXXIII** (The Fall of a Chieftain): The treasure pit is empty; Silver arms Jim; ambush from the trees; Merry and two others killed; three survivors flee; Ben Gunn has had the treasure for two months.

**Ch. XXXIV** (And Last): Three days loading gold onto the Hispaniola; three mutineers abandoned on the island; Silver escapes in port with a sack of coins; the voyage home; the accounting of what each survivor did with the money; Jim's nightmares.

## Pass 2 — Characters

**Jim Hawkins** — narrator, innkeeper's son, approximately twelve years old. He is not special; he is simply present and attentive. His rashness (the apple barrel, cutting the ship adrift, the solo return to the stockade) repeatedly saves everyone and repeatedly terrifies everyone. The doctor calls it cowardly; the captain says Jim is "too much the born favourite." He ends the book haunted. The adventure has not been worth it in the way he expected.

**Long John Silver** — ship's cook, former quartermaster under Flint, ringleader of the mutiny. One leg, cork-and-wood prosthetic, a crutch he uses like a weapon when needed. Parrot on his shoulder named Captain Flint. Silver is the most alive person in the book. He is a liar, a manipulator, a killer — and also brave, funny, fatherly, brilliant. He genuinely likes Jim. He is also capable of planning Jim's murder without it contradicting the liking. His final act is to steal from the people who have just saved his life. He escapes. He probably lives well. Stevenson cannot bring himself to punish him properly.

**Captain Smollett** — professional sailor, iron-willed, humorless, correct. He distrusts the whole venture from the start and is right about everything. He holds the stockade, makes the hard calls, and ends the book retired. He is the moral spine of the expedition and the least interesting person in it.

**Squire Trelawney** — rich, generous, loose-tongued, incompetent. He finances the voyage, does the hiring, and then boasts about the treasure in every tavern in Bristol. He means well. Every near-death in the book is traceable to his blabbing. He is not punished for this; he gets his share of gold.

**Dr. Livesey** — ship's doctor, magistrate, the sharpest mind on the honest side. He treats the pirate sick without flinching, gives Silver the chart because it's the only move that saves everyone, and is the first to understand what Ben Gunn's secret actually means. His coldness toward Jim ("as you have brewed, so shall you drink, my boy") is fair and still hurts.

**Billy Bones** — catalyst, dead by Chapter III. A broken pirate living in terror, paying a child to watch the road. His sea-chest is the origin of the whole story. He is simultaneously pathetic and frightening — a drunk who sings the same song every night and dies of fear when the paper arrives.

**Ben Gunn** — three years marooned on the island, fed on goats and berries, half out of his mind, entirely crucial. He found the treasure. He moved it. He is the wild card that makes the whole ending possible. He is also childlike, terrified, easily manipulated, and genuinely funny. He gets a thousand pounds and spends it in nineteen days.

**Israel Hands** — wounded mutineer, pragmatic, dangerous. Jim's antagonist in the shroud-climb. He makes a reasonable deal, breaks it the first moment it's convenient, and dies for it. He is not a villain of grand ambition; he is just a man who will always take the knife if he has one.

**Blind Pew** — brief and terrifying. He arrives at the Benbow in the middle of the night, tapping his stick, finds Jim's hand in the dark, presses a folded paper into it with perfect precision. He is killed under horses' hooves while running from the revenue men. He is in the book for perhaps six pages and is the most frightening thing in it.

**Captain Flint (the parrot)** — Silver's parrot, named for the pirate Flint. She says one thing: *Pieces of eight.* She never lies. She reveals Jim's return to the stockade in the dark, waking the pirates at the worst possible moment. She is still with Silver at the end, and Jim hears her voice in his worst dreams.

## Pass 3 — Elaborate One Scene

**Jim in the Apple Barrel (Chapter XI)**

It is a warm night. Jim cannot sleep in his cabin. He goes up on deck for fresh air and decides to get himself an apple from the big barrel lashed to the foremast. The barrel is nearly empty. He has to lean in to reach the apples at the bottom. He is small enough — and tired enough — that he loses his balance and slides in.

He is sitting in the dark bottom of the barrel, slightly damp, apple-scented, too tired to climb out, when he hears feet on the deck above him. Someone sits down against the barrel. Jim cannot see anything. He can hear perfectly.

Long John Silver is talking to a young sailor named Dick. Silver's voice is as easy and warm as it always is — he sounds like a man telling a bedtime story. He is explaining how he lost his leg in Hawke's service and kept his money and bought himself a tavern in Bristol and courted respectability all his life, and how none of that matters now because there is a map and there is gold and after the treasure is loaded and stowed, every honest man on this ship will be dead, beginning with the captain.

Jim does not move. He barely breathes. Silver talks for a long time. He explains the plan with the patience of a man who has thought it through many times. He names names: who's with him, who's wavering, how many guns they have. He explains the economics of gentlemen of fortune — you spend it, you're broke, you spend it again — and why this time will be different. He is teaching Dick. He is generous with the lesson.

At one point old Morgan comes and sits nearby. Silver uses him as an example of a man who has sailed under Flint and is still alive, which is an achievement, and a man who has drunk his share and has nothing to show for it, which is the lesson. Morgan doesn't notice anything wrong with being used this way.

Jim crouches in the dark and understands: everyone he has talked to in the last week, everyone he has liked and trusted, is in this plan. The ship is already lost. The honest men don't know it yet.

What strikes me about this scene is not the information Jim learns but the way he receives it. He doesn't panic. He doesn't move. He is twelve or thirteen years old and he is sitting in a barrel in the dark listening to the architecture of his own death being discussed with the calm warmth of a man who enjoys explaining things, and Jim's response is to hold very still and pay very close attention. He counts. He names names in his head. He files it away.

This is what the barrel makes Jim: not a hero, not an agent — a witness. The barrel is where Jim stops being a boy who enjoys the romance of pirates and becomes a boy who knows what they are. The romance does not die; it becomes accurate. He knows now, exactly, what he is dealing with. He will carry that knowledge for the rest of the book, and in some sense for the rest of his life. The last line of the novel is Silver's parrot waking him from his worst dreams: *Pieces of eight. Pieces of eight.* The barrel is where those dreams were made.

Silver finishes talking. He tilts his head back against the barrel's stave, and Jim can hear his breathing change — satisfied, at ease. The night air comes across the deck. Someone calls out that land has been sighted.

Jim climbs out of the barrel as soon as the deck clears. His legs are cramped. He says nothing. He finds the squire and the doctor and the captain and tells them everything. He is precise. He does not forget a name.

## Seven Storylines

1. **The apple barrel** — Jim overhears Silver's mutiny plan; the burden of being the one who knows; carrying secret knowledge across a ship full of killers. *Themes: the cost of knowing, the carrier becomes the hunted.*

2. **The oilcloth chart** — from Billy Bones's death through Bristol docks to the Hispaniola's hold; the map that marks its bearer; every person who touches the chart is in danger from the people who want it. *Themes: the object that makes you a target, possession as peril.*

3. **Ben Gunn's three years** — the marooned man's solitary vigil; he found the treasure, dug it up, moved it, and waited; the accidental hero who rewrites everyone's ending by acting alone. *Themes: the unsanctioned act that saves, the castaway's strange patience.*

4. **Silver's two sides** — the mutiny plot maintained through charm; the shifting alliances; the bargain with Jim; the final escape; a man who is simultaneously everything the honest men are and their exact opposite. *Themes: the shape-shifter's honor, the alliance that holds because both sides need it.*

5. **Blind Pew's delivery** — the black spot; a blind man navigating a hostile road to find a specific hand in the dark and press a death warrant into it; the messenger who is the message. *Themes: the carrier's body as danger, blind precision.*

6. **The empty treasure pit** — the moment of collective defeat; Silver's instant adaptation; the shift from treasure to survival; Stevenson's meditation on what greed does to men in the moment the thing they've been pursuing is simply gone. *Themes: the trap that closes around the hunters.*

7. **Jim in the shrouds** — the fight with Israel Hands; the boy pinned to the mast by a dirk; his pistols both loaded, fired simultaneously by the shock of the thrown blade; Hands in the green water below. *Themes: survival against a man who is simply stronger, the lucky moment as its own kind of mercy.*

## Theme Check

**Existing threads to avoid:**
- Loyalty under the long watch (LHR, STN, TBS)
- Grief carried in an object (INV, BHD, GDN)
- Honor vs. shame (MAD, GDN, BHD)
- Love and fate intertwined (MSE, INV, BHD)
- Truth delivered posthumously (LGW)
- Healing carried through hostile law (MAN)

**Threads this book could introduce:**
- The carrier becomes the hunted — possession of the chart marks its bearer; the object of value turns its holder into prey.
- The shape-shifter's bargain — Silver as the man who is simultaneously on both sides; the alliance that holds between enemies because both need it.
- The empty center — greed's structure: the thing pursued collapses at the moment of arrival.

**Chosen:** *The carrier becomes the hunted* — a document of value marks its bearer; delivery is survival; the Fighter who holds the chart is the Fighter the pirates are looking for.

Avoided: this is not grief (the chart is not a mourning-object), not healing (no medicine, no exclusion), not loyalty (no long watch — it's active flight), not posthumous truth (the chart's contents are for the living), not honor/shame (no code being violated). The theme is purely about what it means to carry something everyone else wants to kill you for.

**Quest subject:** Storyline 2 — *The Oilcloth Chart.*

## 5-Act Vignette Play — "The Oilcloth Chart"

**TOKEN:** Captain Flint's Oilcloth Chart — a salt-stained map on folded oilcloth: an island in the Caribbean, three red crosses, handwritten bearings in a dead man's hand.

**Journey:** ADM (Admiral Benbow, coastal inn cliff) → TL (Tilbury docks, Bristol's harbor stand-in) → TL (the Spy-Glass alley, Silver's territory) → VS (Visby underground passage) → WM (Weimar archive gate, Squire's factor)

**Tone:** French noir. The chart is the grammatical subject. The Fighter witnesses. Cool, precise, observational. Every scene smells of salt or sawdust or candle wax.

---

### Act I — The Admiral Benbow

**Location:** ADM (Admiral Benbow, coastal cliff village — lantern-lit inn, dark sea below, chalk road running inland)
**Type:** skill_check

**Scene:** Before dawn. A boy named Jim presses an oilcloth packet into your hands in the inn's back doorway. His voice is steady. "A man died here tonight. These men — the ones coming down the road — they're looking for this. Get it to Trelawney at the Anchor in Tilbury. Don't stop. Don't show it." Through the window: torchlight. The tap of a blind man's stick on the chalk road.

**Skill Check:** Stealth DC 12 — slip through the gap in the back garden wall before Blind Pew's party reaches the kitchen door.

**Fail Text:** A hand catches your sleeve in the dark. You pull free and go over the wall anyway — but they saw you. Someone on the road shouts. You run with the chart still in your coat and the knowledge that they have your description.

**Success Text:** Over the wall. Down the lane. Behind you, the sound of men breaking a door. The chart is an inch of folded cloth against your ribs. You don't stop until you can smell the harbor.

**Grant Item:** Captain Flint's Oilcloth Chart — *A folded map on salt-stained oilcloth: three red crosses, an island, bearings in a dead man's cramped hand. Heavy as nothing, worth everything the wrong men want.*

---

### Act II — The Tilbury Docks

**Location:** TL (Tilbury — harbor rope-and-salt, merchant smells, dawn light on water)
**Type:** skill_check

**Scene:** Bristol harbor, dawn. Long John Silver keeps a tavern here — the Spy-Glass — and his eyes are on every ship and every face. A one-legged man leans against a barrel at the dock gate, pipe in his teeth, scanning arrivals. His green parrot says: *Pieces of eight. Pieces of eight.* He is not looking for anything in particular. He is looking for everything.

**Skill Check:** Deception DC 14 — you are a rope merchant's runner, come to deliver an invoice to the chandler's. You have no packet. You have no map. You've never heard of Billy Bones.

**Fail Text:** Silver tilts his head and smiles at you like a man greeting a friend. "That's a heavy coat for this weather." His hand moves toward your side. You don't wait. You're through the dock gate before he finishes the sentence — but he watched which way you went.

**Success Text:** Silver looks you over, nods at the parrot, and turns back to the water. You walk to the chandler's at an invoice-clerk's pace and don't run until you round the corner. The parrot's voice carries three streets.

---

### Act III — The Spy-Glass Alley

**Location:** TL (alley behind the chandler's, timber and tar, midmorning shadow)
**Type:** combat

**Scene:** Two men step out of the alley mouth. They have the patient look of men who have been waiting. "The packet, friend. Gentle-like." One has a marlinspike. The other has a knife. They are not Silver's men — they're freelancers who saw Silver notice you and guessed you were worth following.

**Combat:** 2× Bristol Dockhands (CR 1/4, 11 HP each, club/knife, +3 to hit, 1d6+1 damage). Initiative advantage if you acted on Silver's look and came here carefully.

**Fail Text:** You're down in the mud with a boot on your wrist. The taller man takes the packet from your coat, weighs it, tucks it into his. When you come round, the alley is empty. You have to find another way.

**Success Text:** Both of them are on the ground. You don't look back. You're through the alley and onto the merchant street in twenty seconds. The chart is still in your coat. Your ribs ache. You keep walking.

---

### Act IV — The Visby Passage

**Location:** VS (Visby underground market — stone cold, torchlit, Warlord Mordus's territory)
**Type:** skill_check

**Scene:** Underground. The fastest route north passes through Visby's market warren. A fence with a Jolly Roger tattooed on his neck is selling information from a booth between a pie vendor and a locksmith. A buyer in a black coat is asking questions. You catch three words: *chart*, *Bristol*, and *one-legged*. The buyer's back is to you. His watcher is not.

**Skill Check:** Perception DC 13 — notice the tail before he notices you noticing him.

**Fail Text:** You don't see the boy until he's already slipped away between the stalls. Your route is known. When you reach the market's north gate, there are two men standing to either side of it who weren't there when you came in.

**Success Text:** You see the boy's eyes flick to you and then away — that's the tell. You double back through the fish-gutting row, come out through the cooper's entrance, and arrive at the north gate alone. The watcher is still looking at the wrong door.

---

### Act V — The Weimar Archive Gate

**Location:** WM (Weimar — archive-haunted, scholarly, evening candlelight in high windows)
**Type:** escort

**Scene:** Squire Trelawney's factor is a thin man in a blue coat who is waiting at the archive gate with his hands in his pockets and the look of a man who has been rehearsing patience for several hours. He takes the oilcloth packet from you, breaks the seal with one thumbnail, spreads it on the gatehouse table. Three red crosses. An island. His eyebrows rise once — then he rolls it tight, slips it under his coat, and hands you a coin.

"The squire will want to move quickly," he says. He is already thinking about ships.

**Fail Text:** The factor never comes. You wait in the gatehouse doorway until the candles are lit in the archive windows and the gate-keeper starts looking at you with a question forming. Whoever took the chart, it wasn't the squire's man. The chart is in other hands now, and the island will not be what anyone hoped.

**Take Item:** Captain Flint's Oilcloth Chart

**Grant Item:** Piece of Eight — *A Spanish silver coin, worn smooth, pressed into your palm without ceremony. "There's more where that came from," said the factor, already turning away, "if the island is what he says."*
