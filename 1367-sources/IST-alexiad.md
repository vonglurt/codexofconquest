## Cycle 1 — Angle: The partisan historian whose love for her subject preserves what it was meant to conceal

### What this angle sees that prior cycles would miss
(First cycle — no prior angles to avoid.)

This angle focuses on the Alexiad's most paradoxical feature: Anna Komnene wrote to honor her father and condemn his enemies, and in doing so she preserved evidence of Alexios's moral failures and created the most vivid portrait of his greatest enemy. The love for Alexios is so specific that it names every act she defended. The hatred for Bohemund is so intense that she described him in extraordinary physical detail — the only contemporary Byzantine account of a Norman leader that functions simultaneously as condemnation and portraiture. The angle: bias as preservation mechanism. You can only describe what you cannot stop looking at.

## Pass 1 — Full Telling

Anna Komnene was born in the purple — born in the Porphyra, the special birthing room in the palace, which meant she was a child of a reigning emperor, which meant she was, by birth, a possible heir. She was Alexios's firstborn, brilliant and educated, fluent in Homer and medicine and philosophy, and she expected to matter in the way that only power lets a person matter. Then her brother John was born and she was set aside. She married the Caesar Nikephoros Bryennios, whom she would spend her life trying to make into a different kind of man than he was. When her father died in 1118, she conspired to put Bryennios on the throne instead of her brother. The conspiracy failed. Her mother Eirene, who had supported her, died shortly after. John II exiled Anna to the Kecharitomene convent she and her mother had founded. She never left it. There, in her fifties and sixties, she wrote the Alexiad.

The Alexiad is a history of her father Alexios I Komnenos's reign (1081–1118). It is also the only major historical work written by a Byzantine woman. Anna knew both of these things and wrote with the particular intensity of someone doing the only act of power still available to her. She says in the prologue: waves of calamity have struck her "like a raging sea," and she must write before grief overwhelms her reason. She is not being rhetorical. She means it precisely. The book is how she did not drown.

The Alexiad covers thirty-seven years of Byzantine history. Anna was not present at most of what she describes — Alexios's seizure of the throne happened when she was an infant; the Norman wars when she was a young child — but she had access to her father's advisers, her husband's notes, her mother's memory, and the palace documents. For the First Crusade (1096–1099), she was thirteen to sixteen years old: old enough to witness the Crusaders' passage through Constantinople, too young to be at the formal meetings. She describes what she saw through palace windows and what her father told her at dinner.

Alexios came to power by a coup. He was a military commander who, with the Doukas family's support, entered Constantinople while the incumbent emperor Nikephoros III was still in power, seized the city, and took the throne. He let his troops loot the city for three days. Anna defends this — she is always defending him — but the defense is specific enough that the looting is documented in her account.

The Normans came immediately. Robert Guiscard, Duke of Apulia, invaded Byzantine territory in Illyria (modern Albania) in 1081. He had been looking for an excuse to take Byzantine lands since before Alexios came to power, and the dynastic chaos of the 1070s had given him several. The battle of Dyrrachium (1081) was a Byzantine defeat; Alexios fought well but was outmaneuvered. He fell back, regrouped, used Venetian sea power (paying with extensive trade privileges — the moment Venice's Mediterranean dominance began), and harassed the Norman supply lines. Robert died of fever on Kefalonia in 1085. His son Bohemund took over and was eventually forced to withdraw.

The Pechenegs — Turkic nomadic tribes from north of the Danube — invaded multiple times. Alexios handled them through a combination of military pressure, diplomacy, and the strategic use of the Cumans (rival steppe nomads) against them. The decisive battle of Levounion (1091) virtually destroyed the Pecheneg threat. Anna covers this with admiration: her father was a good general, and she understood generalship.

Then the Crusaders came.

In 1095, Alexios had sent ambassadors to Pope Urban II requesting mercenary assistance against the Turks who had taken much of Anatolia. He received, two years later, 100,000 people — the First Crusade. The People's Crusade arrived first: Peter the Hermit's unruly mass of pilgrims, farmers, and minor knights who had no military organization and began looting Byzantine territory immediately. Alexios got them across the Bosphorus as fast as possible. Most of them were killed by the Turks at Nicaea before the proper armies arrived.

The proper armies arrived between August 1096 and April 1097. Godfrey of Bouillon. Raymond of Toulouse. Hugh of Vermandois. And Bohemund of Taranto.

Anna was thirteen when Bohemund came to Constantinople. She describes him in the passage that scholars quote most often from the Alexiad: he was uniquely tall among tall men, she says; his waist alone was wider than a normal man's shoulders; he was blond in a way that was unusual among Normans, most of whom were dark; his eyes were blue-grey and quick and never entirely still; when he stood in a room he filled it not through aggression but through the specific gravity of a person who has never once doubted his right to be present. She hated him. She goes on for eight pages. At the end of the eight pages, you know what Bohemund of Taranto looked like better than you know the appearance of any other person named in a 12th-century document.

Alexios swore the Crusaders to alliance and got them across the Bosphorus. He insisted on keeping Nicaea — the Crusaders captured it, but Alexios negotiated the surrender directly with the Turkish garrison and took the city before the Crusaders could enter and loot it. The Crusaders were furious. This is the first of the misunderstandings that would define Byzantine-Crusader relations for the next century.

The Crusaders took Antioch in 1098. Bohemund kept it for himself, breaking his oath to return conquered territory to Alexios. Alexios did not march south to join the final push on Jerusalem; the Crusaders always resented this. Jerusalem fell in 1099. Bohemund became Prince of Antioch. Alexios spent the next eight years trying to get it back through diplomacy and military pressure.

In 1107, Bohemund invaded Byzantine Illyria in a new Norman campaign, commanding a large force with the support of Pope Paschal II. Anna describes this with controlled fury — the Pope had given his blessing to an invasion of the Christian empire. Alexios's defensive strategy was masterful: he avoided pitched battle and cut Bohemund's supply lines until the Norman army starved and scattered. Bohemund was forced to sign the Treaty of Devol (1108), acknowledging Byzantine suzerainty over Antioch. He never went back to Antioch. He died in 1111.

The Bogomil heresy: late in his reign, Alexios became concerned about the Bogomil sect (dualist heretics who rejected the material world, the church hierarchy, and the cross). He identified the leader as a monk named Basil. Rather than arrest him immediately, Alexios invited Basil to the palace privately, received him as a potentially interested listener, and had monks concealed behind a curtain to take notes. Basil, believing he was converting the emperor, explained the entire structure of the sect: its doctrine, its hierarchy, and the names of its senior disciples. Alexios then drew back the curtain and revealed the scribes. Basil and his twelve senior disciples were arrested. After a public trial at the Hippodrome, Basil was burned. His disciples recanted and were imprisoned.

Anna approves of this. She explains that the deception was justified by the threat. The approval is more disturbing than the method: she finds nothing problematic about her father using the forms of hospitality and trust as a trap.

Alexios's health failed in his last years. He suffered from gout, poor sleep, and unspecified internal pain. Eirene, his wife, did not leave his bedside. Anna describes his final days with the precision of medical observation — she was trained in medicine and attended him — and with grief so specific it is painful to read. He died in August 1118. John II, his son, was immediately proclaimed emperor. The succession was settled before Alexios's body was cold.

Anna ends the Alexiad by saying she cannot continue: "What is left is the abyss of grief." The sentence is accurate. The book stops. She went back to her convent and died there, probably around 1153.

### Chapter Notes (Books I–XV)

**Book I:** Alexios's background; the Doukas alliance; the coup against Nikephoros III; the looting of Constantinople (defended); Alexios's early military experience.

**Book II:** The Komnenian family consolidation; Alexios's brother Isaac; the early Norman threat taking shape.

**Book III:** First Norman war begins; Robert Guiscard invades; the siege of Dyrrachium (1081) — Alexios is defeated; the retreat and reorganization; the Venetian alliance and its cost.

**Book IV:** The campaign against the Normans; Robert Guiscard's death from fever (1085); Bohemund retreats; relative peace on the western front; the Pecheneg threat builds from the north.

**Book V:** The Pecheneg invasions; Alexios's military solutions; the Cuman alliance; the battle of Levounion (1091); the Pecheneg threat ends.

**Book VI:** The Cumans and Turkish pressure; various diplomatic negotiations; Anna begins to appear in the narrative as a witness, noting things she saw as a child.

**Books VII–VIII:** Political consolidation; the Doukas family tension; various military episodes on the eastern border.

**Book IX:** The People's Crusade — Peter the Hermit's army; Anna's famous description of the Celtic host "like a sea without a shore"; Alexios gets them across the Bosphorus; most are killed by Turks.

**Book X:** The proper Crusade armies arrive; Godfrey of Bouillon; the Crusaders' camp outside the walls; Alexios's management strategy; the oath-taking.

**Book XI:** Bohemund arrives — Anna's eight-page physical description; the alliance sworn; the Crusaders take Nicaea; Alexios negotiates the surrender directly; Crusaders are furious at being excluded; the army moves south.

**Book XII:** The Crusaders at Antioch; Bohemund keeps Antioch; the broken oath; Alexios does not march to Jerusalem; the Crusaders' resentment.

**Book XIII:** Post-Crusade Byzantine-Norman relations; Alexios's attempts to recover Antioch through diplomacy; the Treaty of Devol negotiations (1108); Bohemund defeated and humiliated.

**Book XIV:** Late reign politics; the Diogenes plot (Nikephoros Diogenes, son of a former emperor, plots against Alexios); the Bogomil heresy identified; Basil the Bogomil received at the palace; the curtain confession; the arrests and burning.

**Book XV:** Alexios's final illness; Eirene's attendance; the medical descriptions; Anna's presence at his deathbed; his death and the immediate succession; Anna's grief; the Alexiad ends.

## Pass 2 — Slower

### Key characters

**Anna Komnene:** The narrator, never invisible. She is present in the text as a pride that cannot quite conceal itself and a grief that cannot quite contain itself. She quotes Homer to compare her father to Achilles and means it. She calls herself educated in "all branches of philosophy" and this is not boasting: she was genuinely learned. Her failures of judgment are always visible through the excellence of her observation — she is too specific in her condemnations for them to remain only condemnations.

**Alexios I Komnenos:** Pragmatic, intelligent, charming, ruthless when necessary. He came to power by a coup. He kept power by being better at Byzantine politics than anyone else in the room. He was a good general in the sense that matters most: he knew when not to fight. His religious policy was careful; he understood the theological problems of his time without being trapped by them. His treatment of the Bogomils is the clearest evidence of his moral method — not illegal, but cold. Anna cannot see his coldness. Her defense of it is so specific that it is preserved.

**Empress Eirene Doukaina:** Alexios's wife and Anna's mother. Dignified, intelligent, politically active. She supported Anna's conspiracy after Alexios's death. She is described with admiration that is more restrained than the admiration for Alexios — Eirene is the parent who was always clearly human; Alexios is the parent Anna turned into a principle.

**Bohemund of Taranto (Hauteville):** Anna's most vivid character, which she would not have chosen to be true. He was genuinely dangerous to the Byzantine empire — the Norman campaigns under his leadership were the most serious military threat Alexios faced after the Crusade. Anna hates him for good reasons. She cannot stop describing him.

**Nikephoros Bryennios (Anna's husband):** He began his own history of the Komnenian family before he died. His drafts survive in fragments. He was mild, educated, and loyal — not the man Anna wanted him to be when she tried to make him emperor. She mourns him in the prologue as her "dear lord" and does not describe him in physical detail. He is the absence in the text.

**Robert Guiscard:** The elder Norman duke. Brutal, magnificent, died before he could finish. Anna describes him with the compressed hostility she uses for people who were already dead when she wrote and therefore cannot be in the room to make her look at them.

**Basil the Bogomil:** The heresy's leader. He comes to the palace believing he is converting the emperor. He is completely convinced. The account of his conviction — that he had found in Alexios the opening he had been waiting for — is the most psychologically complex scene in the Alexiad. He was wrong about everything, and he was wrong with complete confidence, and Alexios let him be wrong while taking careful notes.

---

### Pivotal choices

**Alexios's decision not to march to Jerusalem:** The central controversy of his reign. He was fully capable of marching south after Antioch fell to the Crusaders in 1098. His reasons for not doing so — supply lines, Byzantine-Crusader tensions, the political situation at home — are all defensible. His decision left the Crusaders feeling betrayed and let Bohemund consolidate control of Antioch permanently. Anna defends it. The defense is specific enough that we can see both the reasons and the consequences.

**The curtain confession:** Alexios invited Basil the Bogomil to the palace, created conditions of trust, and had the conversation documented in secret. This is legally a deception that produced evidence. Anna approves without hesitation. The approval tells you something about Anna's ethical world that the act itself does not quite tell you about Alexios's.

**Anna's own conspiracy:** Not covered in the Alexiad (which ends before her fall from favor), but present as negative space. Everything about the exile that produced the book was the result of her judgment that Bryennios could be made emperor. She was wrong. She does not say so.

---

### Tone of the world

Byzantine dignity under pressure. The empire is smaller than it was a century ago. The Turks have Asia Minor. The Normans want Illyria. The Crusaders are guests who will not leave. Alexios manages all of this through intelligence, patience, and charm — the diplomatic toolkit of a court that knows it cannot simply fight its way back to its former extent. Anna writes in the register of a person who was raised to believe that this empire was eternal and is working through her discovery that it is not.

## Pass 3 — The Bohemund Scene (Book XI, the arrival at Constantinople)

*Bohemund of Taranto, son of Robert Guiscard, arrives at Constantinople with the Crusade armies, autumn 1096. Anna is thirteen years old. She does not record her age. She records his dimensions.*

He is described arriving with a retinue of Norman knights, all mounted. The Norman horses are the largest horses anyone in Constantinople has seen outside of illustrated manuscripts. Bohemund's horse is the largest horse in the retinue.

Anna describes his height first — she always starts with height, and with Bohemund she spends longer on it than she does on anyone else. He was taller than any man she had seen. Not tall in the way of tall men, who still have ordinary proportions; tall in a way that reorganized the space around him, so that when he walked into a room the room's geometry changed slightly, the way a large ship changes the look of a harbor.

Then his hair: blond, she says, in a color she describes as like ripe wheat. This mattered in the Byzantine court because court aesthetics valued dark hair; blondness was associated with the northern barbarians. On Bohemund it read as another form of the same aggression she attributed to everything about him — his blondness was a blondness that did not apologize for itself.

His eyes: blue-grey (she uses a word that hovers between those colors). Quick. Never entirely fixed on one thing, but never obviously scanning, either — the eyes of a man who has learned to see everything while appearing to look at only what he is looking at.

She describes his voice: she says it was like thunder, but she immediately says this is wrong — it was not threatening, it was a sound you wanted to hear again. She notices this about herself, that she wanted to hear his voice again, and she does not know what to do with it. She keeps going. She describes the structure of his shoulders, the particular way he sat on a horse (leaning back rather than forward, a posture that suggested he had all the time in the world), his hands.

At the end of the description, she says: "He was of such a nature that if one examines all his characteristics carefully, he appears to have been the enemy of one's tranquility — and yet he was also the most admirable thing one had ever seen."

She hates him for seventeen more chapters and never loses the thread of admiration.

In the actual meeting with Alexios, Bohemund swears the oath of alliance. He swears to return all conquered Byzantine territory. He swears to acknowledge Byzantine suzerainty. Alexios gives him magnificent gifts: money, cloth, food, horses, objects of Byzantine imperial production that communicate both wealth and condescension. Bohemund takes the gifts and accepts the tone. He is too intelligent not to understand the condescension and too pragmatic to object to it. He will take the money and the suzerainty and leave Antioch for himself when the moment comes.

Anna knows all of this, writing forty years later. She describes the meeting as a skilled adversarial negotiation that Alexios won and that Bohemund immediately began to undo. She is right. What she cannot see — or cannot acknowledge — is that Bohemund's keeping of Antioch permanently redirected Crusader power away from Byzantine reconquest, which was arguably the best strategic outcome available to the First Crusade and a disaster for Byzantine ambitions in equal measure.

She was thirteen when this happened. She remembers the height and the voice and the way he sat on the horse and forty years is not long enough.

## IST-01 — Full Vignette Spec: "The Enemy's Portrait"

### Source Hook

Anna Komnene wrote her account of Bohemund of Taranto in Books X–XIII of the Alexiad — eight pages of condemnation that are simultaneously the most detailed physical description of a Norman leader in any contemporary source. A 13th-century Norman genealogist, working in Sicily, recognized what Anna had accidentally preserved. He annotated his copy of the Alexiad's Bohemund section with cross-references to Hauteville family records, identifying each of Anna's descriptive details (height, bearing, voice, eye color) in the Norman genealogical tradition. The annotations transform Anna's Byzantine chronicle into a Norman pedigree document.

In 1367, the annotated manuscript has come to light in a Constantinople monastery. The Byzantine scholar Georgios Kantakouzenos recognizes its double nature: the Byzantine text is invaluable and the Norman annotations are politically dangerous — Crusade advocates can use them to frame Byzantine-Norman relations as a long history of Greek treachery toward heroic Norman Crusaders. Georgios needs the manuscript in the Weimar archive, where it will be classified as a neutral scholarly primary source rather than as Crusade propaganda.

### Logistics

**Year:** 1367
**Commission city:** Constantinople (Byzantine capital under John V Palaiologos — a city aware of being surrounded)
**Route:** CON (Constantinople Byzantine scholar's house) → RGS (Ragusa Dalmatian waystation) → WM (Weimar archive)
**Travel:** Sea from Constantinople to Ragusa; overland from Ragusa north to Weimar
**Dangers:** Byzantine harbour inspection; Venetian manuscript trade interception at Ragusa; Dominican scholarly enthusiasm on the road; Weimar classification crisis

### Cast

**Georgios Kantakouzenos** (commissioning scholar): A 50-year-old Byzantine library cataloguer, distantly related to the former imperial family. He is not political; he is worried about the manuscript's fate. He knows the Weimar archive by reputation. He cannot travel himself. He writes the covering letter in precise Greek with a Latin summary attached.

**Harbour Gate Inspector** (Act II): A careful Byzantine customs official. Not corrupt. Worried about procedure. Has been stopping manuscripts this week.

**Venetian Factor** (Act III, Ragusa harbour): Professional, pleasant, not interested in scenes. Has a standing purchase order for Alexiad manuscripts from a Venetian noble who has the other two copies of the Bohemund section.

**Dominican Friar** (Act IV, road north of Ragusa): Enthusiastic Byzantine scholar. Quotes Anna from memory. Wants to copy the manuscript. Sincere, not hostile. Has a portable writing kit.

**Archivus Sweelinck** (Act V, Weimar): The Weimar archive's head. Sees the classification problem immediately. Requires the carrier to help resolve it.

### TOKEN

**Object:** A thick folio — Anna's Greek text in black ink, Norman genealogical annotations in dark red, two centuries apart, on the same pages. Wrapped in oilcloth. Heavy.

**Granted:** Act 1 — Georgios wraps it himself and hands it to the carrier.
**Checked:** Acts 2–4 — the oilcloth remains sealed.
**Taken:** Act 5 — Sweelinck accepts the package and carries it to the stacks. He does not return it.

**ACT I — THE COMMISSION**
*The Annotated Alexiad · flat on the table, unwrapped, its two inks visible · held by neither party · Kosmosoteira monastery library, Constantinople, morning*

A narrow room above the Bosphorus. Water light on the ceiling. Georgios stands with his back to the table, looking at the carrier. On the table behind him: a thick folio, two colors of ink on every page. Greek text in black, careful and dense. Norman annotations in dark red, cramped and enthusiastic.

"She hated him," Georgios says. "She hated him for twenty years. And because she hated him, we know what he looked like, what he smelled like, the specific way he stood when he was being courteous and meaning the opposite." He pauses. "Every other account of this man tells us he was a great soldier. She tells us which eye was slightly higher than the other."

He wraps the manuscript himself. His hands are steady.

*The carrier notices he does not look at the manuscript while he speaks. This seems wrong, then seems correct: he has been looking at it for three days and must look away.*

**Skill check — Persuasion or History, DC 12:** Georgios is trying to explain the manuscript's double nature but keeps contradicting himself. Extract a single clear instruction: what to tell the harbour inspector, what to tell anyone who asks about the marginalia, and what word to give the Weimar intake desk.

**failText:** The carrier leaves without the delivery instruction. At Act 5, the Weimar intake clerk's classification problem will be harder to resolve without the framing.
**successText:** Georgios gives one sentence: "The annotations do not change what she wrote. They reveal what she preserved without meaning to." **ACT V — THE CLASSIFICATION PROBLEM**
*The Annotated Alexiad · unwrapped on the intake desk · visible to the clerk and Archivus · Weimar archive, morning*

The intake clerk looks at it and calls the senior archivist. The senior archivist looks at it and calls Sweelinck.

Sweelinck reads the first page. Then the last. Then he opens to the Bohemund section and reads one paragraph of the Greek and then one annotation in French. He closes the manuscript. He opens it again. He sets it down.

*He is quiet for a long time.*

The problem: a Byzantine chronicle and a Norman genealogy commentary in the same volume, separated by two centuries of authorship, inseparable on the page. The intake form has one field for primary classification. Two competing scholarly traditions, both legitimate, both primary, both wanting precedence.

**Skill check — History or Persuasion, DC 12:**
- History: Anna Komnene's text is primary; the Norman annotations are secondary additions that change the document's function but not its nature. Both are primary sources in their own right. The Weimar archive's existing precedent is to file the original and note later additions with a cross-reference.
- Persuasion: Note that creating a new category here is itself a scholarly act, not merely an administrative one. Georgios's covering letter names the dual nature explicitly. The Archivus has the authority to act on that framing.

**failText:** The Archivus accepts the manuscript but files it provisionally pending classification review. Three-day wait. The outcome is the same permanent accession; the process takes longer and Anna's new category arrives late.
**successText:** Sweelinck nods. "Byzantine Women Historians," he says. "First entry in that category." He lifts the package. He walks to the stacks. He comes back empty-handed. "She hated him on every page. And now we have the best description of him that exists. That is what we do here — we keep what people preserved without meaning to." He signs the intake certificate. You receive Sweelinck's Intake Certificate.

## Cycle 2 — Angle: *The Nicaea Receipt — The Alliance That Was a Bilateral Deal*

**What this angle reveals that cycle 1 missed**: Cycle 1 showed the partisan historian who preserves what she meant to condemn — bias as accidental archive; the enemy's portrait surviving in the hostile gaze. Cycle 2 shows the diplomatic record of a famous victory that was not, in fact, a joint victory: Alexios secretly negotiated Nicaea's surrender with the Turkish garrison before the Crusaders could storm the walls. The city surrendered to Byzantium; the Crusaders received the news as a fait accompli. The receipt — signed by both Alexios's representative and the Turkish garrison commander — is the document that proves the Crusade's first great liberation was a bilateral transaction the Crusaders were excluded from. In 1367, it is dangerous to three parties simultaneously.

---

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Nicaea Receipt*

#### The Receipt

*The Nicaea Surrender Receipt — a sealed folio, two columns — the left column in Greek with Alexios's ambassador's signature and the imperial seal; the right column in Turkish with the garrison commander's seal; two lines at the bottom that name the terms: the garrison will surrender the city to Byzantine authority, the Crusade will be informed after the transfer is complete; dated June 1097; the Crusaders entered the city to find the Byzantine flags already flying. Both sides agreed to this. The Crusaders were not a party.*

---

**ACT I**

*The Nicaea Surrender Receipt — sealed folio, both seals intact — Constantinople Byzantine imperial scholar quarter, morning.*

The scholar's name is Demetrios. He is sixty, has been at the imperial scriptorium for thirty years, and has spent the last two years trying to get authorization to transfer the Nicaea Receipt to a neutral archive before the current Ottoman pressure on Constantinople makes it a liability or destroys it. He has the authorization now — a single sheet from the imperial secretary, countersigned.

"Three parties want this document destroyed," Demetrios says. He sets the folio on the desk. "The Latin Church does not wish to explain why the Crusade's first victory was a diplomatic arrangement that excluded the Crusade. The Crusader-descended families in the East do not wish to acknowledge that Nicaea's 'liberation' was managed before they arrived. And the current imperial administration finds the sophistication of the negotiation an uncomfortable reminder of what Byzantine diplomacy used to be." He pauses. "The Weimar archive is the only institution in Europe that will file this as what it is, not as evidence for someone's argument."

*— He has been building this argument for two years. He needs to know the Fighter has understood it in two minutes.*

> **Dialogue DC 12.** Tell Demetrios what the category is: not Byzantine diplomatic triumph, not Crusade betrayal, not Turkish capitulation — but a bilateral agreement between two parties from which a third party was excluded, and whose significance lies precisely in that exclusion. The archive files it under the excluded party.

**Fail:** Demetrios studies the Fighter for a moment. "You're going to argue this is a victory document for Byzantium." He is not hostile; he has heard this before. "Try again. Think about who wasn't at the table."

**Pass:** He pushes the folio across. He adds the authorization sheet folded inside the cover: *transfer authorized to neutral carrier, Demetrios Katakalon, imperial scriptorium, Constantinople.* "The Ottoman garrison is two miles outside the walls. Please move quickly."

**TOKEN GRANTED: Nicaea Surrender Receipt** — sealed folio, Byzantine and Turkish seals, Demetrios's authorization sheet inside; proof that Nicaea was already Byzantine before the Crusade reached the walls.

---

**ACT II**

*The Nicaea Surrender Receipt — in the Fighter's satchel — Constantinople harbor, late morning.*

The imperial chamberlain's office sent a runner. The chamberlain's name is Andronikos; he was not consulted about the transfer authorization and has just been informed by a colleague who noticed the secretary's counter-signature. He is at the harbor master's office when the Fighter arrives.

"The authorization was issued without senior review. There is a standing protocol that documents relating to Crusade history must be reviewed by the imperial diplomatic bureau before transfer." He has a cleaner document: a provisional recall notice. He is not threatening. He is a bureaucrat who has found the correct hook.

*— He is right about the protocol. He is wrong about whether this document falls under it: the Nicaea Receipt predates the Crusade's formal establishment of any shared history with Byzantium; it is a bilateral Byzantine-Turkish document, not a Crusade document.*

> **Skill Check: History DC 12.** Establish that the Nicaea Surrender Receipt is a Byzantine-Turkish bilateral document dated before any Crusade-Byzantine formal agreement, and therefore does not fall under the Crusade history review protocol; the protocol covers documents recording joint Byzantine-Crusade actions; this document records an action from which the Crusade was excluded.

**Fail:** Andronikos files the provisional recall and places the folio under a three-day hold. The Fighter waits at the harbor inn while the classification question is forwarded to the diplomatic bureau. The bureau agrees by the second day; the hold is lifted.

**Pass:** Andronikos considers. He is genuinely consulting his protocol knowledge. After a moment: "Pre-Crusade bilateral." He produces a transit clearance: *bilateral Byzantine-Turkish document, June 1097, pre-Crusade agreement category; not subject to Crusade history review protocol.* He attaches it to the folio's exterior.

**TOKEN STATE 2**: Byzantine diplomatic bureau's transit clearance attached — *pre-Crusade bilateral agreement, not subject to Crusade history review protocol.*

---

**ACT III**

*The Nicaea Surrender Receipt — folio with transit clearance — Rhodes Hospitaller commandery, two days later.*

The Hospitaller commandant's archivist is named Brother Konstantin. He is thorough, well-educated, and handles incoming documents from Byzantine-Turkish contact zones as a matter of course. He reads the transit clearance with interest.

"Pre-Crusade bilateral." He turns the folio over. "Nicaea receipt." He looks up. "I think we may have jurisdiction to hold this for preliminary review. The Hospitaller Order is the inheritor of Crusade archival authority for the Levant; any document affecting Crusade territorial history—"

*— The argument is creative. It is also wrong: the Hospitaller jurisdiction over Crusade archival materials covers documents from the Crusade period, not documents from the period that defined the Crusade's political context before it arrived.*

> **Skill Check: Persuasion DC 13.** Tell Brother Konstantin that Hospitaller Crusade archival jurisdiction covers documents generated during or because of Crusade operations; a Byzantine-Turkish surrender agreement made before the Crusade reached Nicaea is definitionally outside that scope; the document affects Crusade history only as context, not as record.

**Fail:** Konstantin files a preliminary review hold and sends a query to his superior. The superior returns a negative on jurisdiction by the next morning. The hold is lifted.

**Pass:** Konstantin accepts the argument. He is a good canonist; he recognizes an overreach. "Fair point. The document was made before the Crusade's operations in that theater." He writes a quick endorsement: *reviewed by Hospitaller commandery Rhodes — jurisdiction declined; document falls outside Crusade archival scope; confirmed pre-operational bilateral.* He attaches it below the transit clearance.

**TOKEN STATE 3**: Hospitaller jurisdiction-declined endorsement added — *outside Crusade archival scope; confirmed pre-operational bilateral.*

---

**ACT IV**

*The Nicaea Surrender Receipt — folio with two endorsements — sea road from Rhodes toward the northern route, night.*

Two men are waiting at the waystation above the harbor. They are not Hospitaller agents; they carry no identifying marks. They move with the practiced ease of men hired for a specific task and paid enough that the task is all they intend to do.

*— Latin Church. They cannot argue the document into a drawer, so they sent the kind of argument that works in the dark.*

> **Combat: 2 opponents — AC 13, HP 22 each.** They want the folio and are prepared to work for it; they stop at half health when the remaining option looks more expensive than the pay.

**Fail:** They take the folio. The Fighter pursues; the waystation keeper saw which road they took.

**Pass:** Both down or broken. Road north clear. Both endorsements intact.

**TOKEN STATE 4**: Carried through Latin Church ambush. All endorsements undamaged.

---

**ACT V**

*The Nicaea Surrender Receipt — folio with transit clearance and two endorsements — Weimar archive, the desk of Archivus Sweelinck, morning.*

Sweelinck reads both columns of the receipt. He reads the transit clearance. He reads Brother Konstantin's endorsement. He reads the date — June 1097 — and traces it against his knowledge of the Crusade's arrival at Nicaea.

"They were not in the city when this was signed." He looks at the seals — Byzantine, Turkish. "The city surrendered to Alexios before the Crusade reached the walls." He sets the folio down. "This document has three parties' names on it. None of them are the Crusade. And the Crusade's first victory is premised on this document's events." He picks up his pen. "What category does a document belong in when its subject is a party that wasn't present at its signing?"

*— He needs the category: not the signatories' archive; not the Crusade victory records; the party that was excluded.*

> **Skill Check: History DC 11.** Tell Sweelinck: file it under the Excluded Party. The Crusade's first great act was premised on a deal made without them. The document that proves this belongs in the Crusade archive — not as their triumph, but as the context that made their triumph a diplomatic reality they did not control. The receipt filed under "First Crusade — Nicaea" will say more about what the First Crusade actually was than any Crusade document does.

**Fail:** Sweelinck files it under Byzantine Diplomatic Records — bilateral agreements with Turkish authorities. He revisits the classification after the Fighter explains; creates a cross-reference to First Crusade Contextual Records.

**Pass:** Sweelinck writes a new filing entry: *First Crusade — Nicaea; contextual document, bilateral pre-Crusade agreement; party excluded: the Crusade; significance: the first liberation of the Crusade was a diplomatic outcome negotiated without Crusade participation.* He files the folio.

He says, after a moment:

"Every victory has a receipt. Nicaea's was signed before the victory was announced."

**TOKEN TAKEN: Nicaea Surrender Receipt**

The folio enters the Weimar archive under First Crusade — Nicaea. The Byzantine authorization sheet, the pre-Crusade bilateral transit clearance, and the Hospitaller jurisdiction-declined endorsement are indexed separately. The Crusade's first liberation is on record as what it was.
