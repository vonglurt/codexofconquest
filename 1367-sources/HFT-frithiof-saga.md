## Cycle 1 — Pass 1: Full Telling

### Historical and Literary Context

Esaias Tegnér (1782–1846) was a Swedish bishop, poet, and professor who became the
preeminent Swedish romantic poet of his generation. His *Frithiof's Saga* was composed
between 1820 and 1825 in twenty-four cantos, each in a different classical verse form —
an extraordinary technical feat that made the poem simultaneously a romantic epic and
a virtuosic formal exercise. It became one of the most widely translated Swedish works
of the nineteenth century, rendering the Old Norse source material into the romantic
register of Tegnér's era: warmer, more sentimental, more concerned with love and
redemption than the medieval original.

The source behind Tegnér's poem is the *Friðþjófs saga hins frœkna* — a late Icelandic
fornaldarsaga (legendary saga) composed around the 14th century, which was itself based
on even older legendary material about a hero named Friðþjófr ("Peacemaker-Thief") from
the Norwegian district of Sognefjord. The medieval saga is tighter, less romantic, and
ends somewhat differently. Tegnér emphasized the love story and the redemption arc;
he added moral weight that the medieval version carried more lightly.

In 1367, Tegnér has not been born — his poem is five centuries in the future. But the
underlying medieval saga material is very much alive, and the legend of Friðþjófr of
Sognefjord belongs to the same oral tradition that produced the other Norse sagas
in our list. A 1367 audience in the Nordic countries would know this story's shape
even without Tegnér's version of it.

### The Story in Full

Frithiof and Ingeborg grow up together in Sognefjord. Frithiof is the son of Thorsten,
a great warrior, and his ward. Ingeborg is the daughter of King Bele. They are raised
as children of the same household and fall in love naturally, inevitably.

When King Bele dies, his sons Helge and Halfdan inherit. Frithiof asks for Ingeborg's
hand. Helge refuses: Frithiof is of lower birth, a chieftain's son but not a king's.
The match would be beneath the royal family. Ingeborg is promised instead to the old
king Hring (Ring), who rules a neighboring district and is a wise, old, honorable man
she does not love.

Frithiof, furious and bereft, goes to Balder's holy grove to see Ingeborg before her
wedding — this is forbidden, the grove is sacred, Balder's peace protects it. In his
grief and anger, he takes Ingeborg's arm in the sacred grove. One of the sacred pillars
falls into the fire; the sacred flame goes out. The peace of Balder, which protects
the whole district, is broken. Frithiof has committed sacrilege.

The brothers banish him, sending him on an impossible errand: to collect tribute from
the Orkney Islands, which has never been paid. They clearly expect him to fail and die.

Frithiof's ship — the great longship Ellida, his inheritance from his father — makes
the voyage through impossible weather. The Orkney jarl receives him, pays some tribute,
and Frithiof returns. But when he comes back, Ingeborg is gone. She was married to
Ring during his absence.

The brothers use the desecration of Balder's grove as a pretext to burn Frithiof's
ancestral farm and banish him permanently.

Frithiof becomes a Viking. He raids foreign coasts. He amasses wealth and reputation.
He cannot forget Ingeborg.

After years at sea, Frithiof arrives at Ring's court in disguise — calling himself
"Thief," the ironically accurate false name. He is welcomed as a guest by the
honorable old king, who may know who he is and chooses not to say. Frithiof lives at
Ring's court through the winter, close to Ingeborg, close to the king who has
everything he wants. He is treated with every honor. He refuses, despite opportunity,
to harm the old man who has been nothing but decent to him.

In the spring, Ring falls ill and dies. On his deathbed, he reveals that he always
knew who Frithiof was and valued him for his restraint and his honor in a difficult
position. He gives Ingeborg his blessing to marry Frithiof.

Frithiof returns to Sognefjord. Helge, in a confrontation, tries to kill him and falls
into the water and drowns — the sea that Frithiof could not have. Halfdan becomes king
and makes peace with Frithiof.

Frithiof becomes king of his ancestral lands. He rebuilds Balder's temple — the sacred
building he destroyed in the moment of his greatest grief. He relights the sacred fire.
This is the saga's ending: not revenge, not triumph, but reparation. The thing he broke,
he rebuilds. The fire he extinguished, he relights.

Tegnér makes this the moral center of the poem: the man Frithiof becomes is the man
who can do this. The violent, grieving, self-destructive young man of the early cantos
and the mature man who rebuilds Balder's temple are the same person who took a different
road.

### Themes and Morals

The saga argues — with the particular warmth of the romantic tradition — that a man is
not what his worst moment made him. Frithiof commits sacrilege in grief's extremity.
He is punished for it. He earns his way back through restraint, endurance, and
eventually the work of rebuilding. The reparation is not symbolic: he actually rebuilds
the temple, actually relights the fire, actually completes the repair.

Tegnér's version adds a specifically Christian-adjacent moral note: the sacred must
be honored, it can be violated, and the violator can make it right. This sits lightly
on Old Norse material and fits the 1367 world comfortably.

The arm-ring is the saga's most famous image: Ingeborg gives it to Frithiof before his
banishment. He offers it to Ring as tribute at their first meeting. Ring puts it on
Ingeborg's arm, who is at table. The ring that carried their love was given as the
price of passage and ended up back on her arm. It is a gift to an enemy that returns
to the giver's love.

### Chapter Notes — by Canto group (Tegnér's version)

**Cantos 1–5: The Childhood, the Love, Bele's Death** — Frithiof and Ingeborg grow
up, fall in love, are separated by inheritance and class. The idyllic opening that the
rest of the poem exists to recover.

**Cantos 6–9: The Refusal, Balder's Grove, the Sacrilege** — Helge's refusal of the
match, Frithiof's forbidden visit to the sacred grove, the sacred fire extinguished.
The poem's first break.

**Cantos 10–14: The Orkney Voyage** — The impossible tribute mission; Ellida's journey;
Frithiof's competence and suffering. He earns respect he cannot use at home.

**Cantos 15–18: The Viking Years** — Frithiof as a raider; his reputation grows;
his grief does not diminish. The arm-ring appears and disappears.

**Cantos 19–22: Ring's Court** — The disguise; the winter with Ring and Ingeborg;
Frithiof's restraint despite opportunity; Ring's knowledge and approval.

**Cantos 23–24: The Return, the Reparation** — Helge's death; Halfdan's peace;
the rebuilding of Balder's temple; the relighting of the sacred fire. The ending
is the work, not the triumph.

## Cycle 1 — Pass 2: Characters

**Frithiof** — The saga's argument in a person. He is everything a Norse hero should
be — strong, brave, loyal, a great seafarer — and the worst moment of his life is
when he takes Ingeborg's arm in a sacred grove in grief and rage and brings the
sacred pillar down. He is not a villain for this. He is a man who broke something
in a moment he could not govern, and the question the saga asks is whether he can
become the man who can repair it. The answer is yes, but it takes everything he has.

**Ingeborg** — She does not choose her situation; she is chosen for. She is promised
to Ring, and she goes, and she is a good wife to Ring, and she loves Frithiof, and
none of these things are contradictory in the world she lives in. Tegnér gives her
more interiority than the medieval saga; she understands exactly what has happened to
her and accepts it without becoming passive. Her arm-ring travels away from her and
comes back to her on Ring's arm; this is a summary of her situation.

**King Ring** — The saga's most honorable figure and its most interesting. An old man,
a good king, a man who accepts the love Ingeborg gives him while knowing what she
gives Frithiof. He invites Frithiof into his house knowing who he is and watches to
see what kind of man comes with the grief. When Frithiof proves himself by restraint,
Ring blesses the future he cannot give Ingeborg himself. He is the person in the story
whose goodness is never contingent.

**Helge** — The rigidly class-conscious brother, the one whose refusal sets everything
in motion. Not a villain but a man whose one significant decision makes the world
worse. He drowns in the sea that Frithiof loved. The saga is not subtle about this.

## Cycle 1 — Pass 3: The Rebuilding Scene

Frithiof comes to Balder's grove.

It is not the grove he remembers. He destroyed the last building here years ago in
grief's extremity — or the sacred fire going out did, which is the same thing. The
sacred enclosure has been tended but not rebuilt; the Norsemen of the district could
not rebuild it without the sacred fire, and the sacred fire went out when the pillar
fell into it. A temple of Balder with a common fire is not Balder's temple.

Frithiof carries the flame. He obtained it from a temple in a neighboring district
where the sacred fire of Balder was kept. It traveled with him in a clay lamp, held
against his side, sheltered from the wind, given every advantage he could give it.
He has brought it through the mountains and the coastal road without it going out.

He approaches the stone foundation where the altar was.

This is the moment Tegnér dwells on. Not the building's construction — he has
organized workmen for that, it will take a season. Not the political reconciliation
with Halfdan, which has its own scene. This moment: Frithiof, alone, carrying a
flame that came from the right place, kneeling at the altar foundation.

He sets the lamp down. The flame is small. He cups his hands around it.

He extinguished this fire in grief. He can only relight it now because he survived
the grief — because the years of exile and the Orkney voyage and the Viking winters
and Ring's court and Ring's death made him into someone who could carry a sacred flame
without dropping it or sheltering it badly or giving up when the wind came up.

The flame is transferred to the new lamp he has prepared for the altar. It lights.

Balder's peace is restored. Not because the temple is beautiful — it isn't yet, it's
a stone foundation with a new lamp on it — but because the fire is right. The building
will be built around this fire. Everything else follows from this.

He puts his hands on the ground at the altar's base. The stone is cold under his
palms. He stays there for a long time.

## Seven Storyline Ideas

**1. The Sacred Flame** — The new temple of Balder is built but cannot be consecrated
with common fire. Frithiof needs the sacred flame from the still-burning temple at
Alrekstaðir, carried in a clay lamp by the Fighter through wind and mountain road and
harbor crossing to the new altar.

**2. The Arm-Ring's First Journey** — Ingeborg gives Frithiof her arm-ring before
his banishment. The Fighter must carry it safely from the farewell scene to Frithiof's
ship before Helge's men, who are watching, realize what she has given him.

**3. The Tribute Refusal** — At the Orkney Islands, Frithiof has collected some tribute
but not all. The Fighter must carry the partial tribute to Jarl Angantyr and persuade
him to seal it with his mark — the official acknowledgment that makes the payment
politically valid even if not numerically complete.

**4. Ring's Deathbed Recognition** — King Ring is dying. He knows who Frithiof is and
wants to give his blessing formally. He needs a witness who is not one of his court —
someone who will carry the account of the blessing back to Ingeborg after he dies. The
Fighter is that witness and carries Ring's spoken word.

**5. The False Name** — Frithiof at Ring's court calls himself "Thief." Ring's
chamberlain has begun to suspect the disguise. The Fighter must carry a false document
establishing "Thief's" origin before the chamberlain's investigation reaches the court
archives.

**6. The Arm-Ring Returns** — Frithiof gave the arm-ring to Ring as tribute. Ring
placed it on Ingeborg's arm. When Ring dies and Frithiof is declared king, the arm-ring
must be formally returned from the estate inventory to Ingeborg's keeping. The Fighter
carries it through the estate dissolution proceedings.

**7. Helge's Last Crossing** — Helge goes to confront Frithiof at the shore. He
carries a weapon made in violation of the truce. The Fighter has seen the weapon made
and must decide what to carry: a warning to Frithiof, or silence.

**Themes I am avoiding:**

"Grief carried in an object" (INV, BHD, GDN) — the arm-ring is a love-object not a
grief-object; but the sacred flame quest avoids object-as-memory entirely.

"Healing carried through hostile law" (MAN) — that is about medicine crossing legal
barriers; this is about sacred continuity crossing desecration.

"The token's journey" (ALL) — every quest has a token that moves; the distinction here
is that the flame's *origin* is what makes the reparation valid, not just its delivery.

**New theme introduced by HFT:**
*"Reparation requires the original fire: rebuilding without the right source is
performance, not restoration"* — Frithiof broke Balder's sacred flame; he can rebuild
the temple stone by stone but only the flame from a still-sacred source makes it
consecrated; the Fighter carries this flame through everything that would extinguish it,
because the act of reparation requires the correct instrument, not just the correct intention.

This is distinct from all 18 existing themes. It is about the conditions for genuine
reparation versus symbolic gesture.

## 5-Act Vignette Play: "The Sacred Flame"

**The Token · A clay lamp holding the sacred flame of Balder, taken from the still-burning
temple at Alrekstaðir · carried in both hands · cannot be relit from any common source ·
must reach the new altar at Balder's grove before the new temple is consecrated**

---

### Act I — The Lamp-Lighting
*The Sacred Flame · just lit · in the clay lamp · the temple at Alrekstaðir,
pre-dawn, the priest of Balder at the altar*

The temple at Alrekstaðir is one of the few places in the district where Balder's
sacred fire never went out. It burns here still, tended through all the years of
the other fire's absence. The priest — an old man who remembers when there were two
fires — has been told why you have come.

He does not light the lamp himself. That is not his role. He holds the sacred flame
out on the altar lamp, and you must take it — pick up the carrying-lamp, hold it to
the flame, withdraw it. The transfer is the act. The lamp is now yours to carry.

He gives you one instruction: *"The flame must not go out before it reaches the altar.
If it goes out, it is common fire again and cannot serve. Come back and we begin again."*

*The clay lamp is warm in both hands. The flame is small and steady.*

---

### Act II — The Mountain Road
*The Sacred Flame · sheltered in the clay lamp · the mountain road between
Alrekstaðir and Sognefjord, wind coming up*

The mountain road rises. Wind comes down the valley from the north — not a storm,
but the kind of steady cold wind that has been extinguishing candles and lamps since
before roads were cut through these hills.

The lamp is cupped in both hands. Both hands occupied means slower footing on the
rocky switchbacks. The flame is steady but small. A sustained gust and it will go.

There is a section of the road — a gap between two rock faces — where the wind
channels and arrives from an angle that is almost the worst possible for a carried
flame.

*Both hands on the lamp. Your body as the windbreak. The gap is twenty feet wide.*

---

### Act III — The Harbor Crossing
*The Sacred Flame · still lit · in the clay lamp · the harbor,
a ferry across the fjord-arm, the sea air*

The harbor crossing between the coastal road and the Sognefjord district is a small
ferry — one boatman, a flat bottom, the salt air coming in from the fjord. The ferry
rocks. Salt spray comes over the gunwale when a wave catches the bow.

Carrying an open flame across a rocking boat in salt spray is a different problem
from carrying it through mountain wind. The movement is unpredictable. The spray
extinguishes things that wind doesn't.

The ferryman offers to take the lamp and set it in the stern-box — a small wooden
enclosure he uses for deliveries. He says he has carried lit lamps across before.
He may be right. He may be mistaken about this specific flame.

*The lamp must not go into the stern-box. But both hands occupied means a difficult
crossing.*

---

### Act IV — The Last Road
*The Sacred Flame · intact · approaching Balder's grove · the coastal track,
Frithiof ahead, the new temple foundation visible*

The track from the harbor to Balder's grove runs along the coast. Frithiof is ahead,
visible, walking toward the foundation stones. He does not look back. He has been
carrying something too — the weight of the reason he is walking this road — and he
does not yet know the flame has arrived.

Two men at the grove's edge: workmen who have been building, curious about the lamp.
They want to help. One of them wants to light a torch from the flame — he has been
working in the dark since dawn.

*The flame is not for torches. The flame is for the altar. The workman is not
wrong to need light. But this is not that lamp.*

---

### Act V — The Altar Stone
*The Sacred Flame · in the fighter's hands · the last ten feet ·
the altar foundation, Frithiof kneeling, the new lamp prepared*

Frithiof is kneeling at the altar foundation with the new lamp he has prepared for
the altar — a bronze lamp, better-made than the clay carrier. He is waiting.

He hears you approach. He turns and sees the clay lamp still lit. Something in his
face does something you cannot quite name — not relief, not exactly, but the specific
expression of a man who has been living with the possibility of this moment for years
and is now inside it.

He holds the bronze lamp out. You hold the clay lamp to it. The flame transfers.

He sets the bronze lamp on the stone. The flame is small and steady in the new lamp.
Balder's peace begins again from this point, from this specific flame, from the only
source that could give it. The temple will be built around this.

*You receive the Clay Carrier's Shard — the clay lamp, now empty and still warm,
the object that carried the flame from its source; Frithiof hands it back to you
when the transfer is complete.*

## Cycle 2 — Ring's Deathbed Recognition

**What cycle 1 missed:** The Sacred Flame was the material requirement — the original fire, the one legitimate means of restoration, the act that converts a rebuilt stone into a consecrated temple. Ring's Deathbed Recognition reveals what was always true at the center of the saga and never formally stated: King Ring has known since the first week who "Thief" was. He invited the disguise to continue because it suited a recognition he had already made privately. Now he is dying and he wants the private recognition to become a witnessed fact, and he needs a witness who is not entangled in his court's politics — someone Ingeborg will trust. The Fighter is that witness. Cycle 1 was about what fire makes possible. Cycle 2 is about what a word can do before the court's version fills the space.

**New angle:** Ring calls the Fighter to his deathbed privately. He dictates his recognition — that he always knew, that he chose to welcome Frithiof, that this changes everything about how his death should be understood. He presses his signet ring into the Fighter's hand. The Fighter carries it through the court to Ingeborg's chamber before the chamberlain's version can arrive first.

---

### Pass 1 — Token

**TOKEN: Ring's Signet Ring** — taken from Ring's own hand on his deathbed, pressed into the Fighter's palm with the formal recognition spoken over it. The ring is the seal of Ring's private acknowledgment: that he always knew who "Thief" was, that the welcome was genuine, that Frithiof has no debt to expunge and no wrong to answer for. Carried to Ingeborg's chamber before the court composes its official account. At Act V, placed in Ingeborg's hand with the exact words Ring spoke — no more, no fewer.

### Pass 2 — Seven Storyline Ideas

**1. The Sacred Flame** *(USED — Cycle 1)* — Balder's sacred flame, carried from Alrekstaðir to the new altar.

**2. The Arm-Ring's First Journey** — Ingeborg's arm-ring carried to Frithiof's ship before Helge's watchers.

**3. The Tribute Refusal** — Partial tribute carried to Jarl Angantyr for his sealing mark.

**4. Ring's Deathbed Recognition** *(CHOSEN — Cycle 2)* — Ring calls the Fighter to his deathbed. Dictates his formal recognition that he always knew who Frithiof was and welcomed him anyway. Presses his signet ring into the Fighter's hand. The Fighter carries it to Ingeborg before the court's version fills the space.

**5. The False Name** — A false document establishing "Thief's" origin before the chamberlain's investigation closes.

**6. The Arm-Ring Returns** — The arm-ring carried through estate dissolution proceedings back to Ingeborg's keeping.

### Act I — The Summons
*Ring's Signet Ring · on Ring's right hand · Ring's private chamber,
a low fire, the court not yet assembled, the king propped on pillows*

Ring has sent for you specifically. Not a herald, not a court witness, not Frithiof
himself — you, who are not entangled in the obligations of this court, who came here
as something other than a partisan, who can be trusted to carry a word without
turning it into a word that serves your own position.

He is propped on pillows. He is clearly dying. He is entirely lucid.

*"I always knew who he was,"* he says. *"The first week. There is a way a man holds
his hands when he has been trained in a different fighting style than the one he is
pretending to use. I knew within seven days. I chose to say nothing. I want her to
know that the welcome was genuine — not a trap, not blindness. I chose it. Take the
ring. Say exactly what I say."*

He holds out his right hand.

*The fire is low. The room is quiet. The chamberlain is outside the door and will
be in this room as soon as Ring calls him, which Ring has chosen not to do yet.*

**DC 12 Insight — understand the full weight of what he is asking: not comfort, not ceremony, but precision; you are the carrier of a fact that has specific words; no paraphrase, no softening, the words exactly as he says them.**

*If you fail:* You understand the emotion but not the mechanism — you hear "go tell Ingeborg he loved her" when what he said is precisely different from that and the difference matters.

*If you pass:* You understand: the recognition is a specific statement about a specific choice made at a specific moment. The words are the thing. You nod. He begins.

---

### Act II — The Dictation
*Ring's Signet Ring · being transferred · Ring's private chamber,
the fire, the words being spoken for the one time they will be spoken*

He tells you what to say. It is four sentences. He speaks them slowly and has you
repeat them back. You repeat them incorrectly once — the order of the second and
third clause — and he corrects you. You repeat them again. Correctly this time.

He holds out his right hand. The signet ring is on his forefinger. He works it off —
it costs him something, his hands are failing — and he presses it into your palm.

*"When she has heard the four sentences, give her the ring. She will know what it
means. Don't explain it."*

The ring is warm from his finger. The four sentences are in your mouth, in the order
he specified.

*Outside the door, you can hear the chamberlain's footstep. He has been patient.
He will come in now unless you leave first.*

**DC 12 Composure — hold the four sentences in exact order; they are the commission; the ring is the seal; you have perhaps thirty seconds before the chamberlain enters.**

*If you fail:* You leave correctly but one of the clauses has transposed in your memory. By Act V you will deliver three sentences and a paraphrase.

*If you pass:* Four sentences. Exact. In the order he specified. You close your hand around the ring and you are through the door before the chamberlain's hand reaches the latch.

---

### Act III — The Corridor
*Ring's Signet Ring · in the Fighter's closed hand · the corridor outside
Ring's chamber, the chamberlain at the door, court members in motion*

The chamberlain is in the corridor. He has been outside this door for an hour.
He knows someone was inside. He has not yet entered because he is, above all things,
a careful man.

He looks at you. He looks at your closed hand.

*"The king is resting. What business did you have?"*

He is not accusing you. He is the man whose entire function is to manage the flow
of information through this court, and you have just come out of the king's private
chamber carrying something in a closed hand, and he would like to know what it is
and where it is going so he can decide whether it needs to pass through him first.

It does not pass through him.

**DC 13 Persuasion — you are attending to a small personal commission for the king; it is not court business; it is entirely ordinary; the chamberlain does not need to be involved.**

*If you fail:* The chamberlain insists on knowing the nature of the commission. You lose time and he is now watching Ingeborg's corridor specifically.

*If you pass:* The chamberlain looks at you for one more moment, then steps to the side. He will ask again later. Later is fine. You are already at the end of the corridor.

---

### Act IV — Ingeborg's Door
*Ring's Signet Ring · in the Fighter's hand · the passage to Ingeborg's
private rooms, a guard at the turning, one of the king's household men*

The passage to Ingeborg's rooms is attended by one of the household guards — one
of Helge's old people who stayed on after Helge died, who is loyal to the court's
structural interests rather than to Ring personally, and who knows that the correct
procedure for delivering messages to the queen is through the chamberlain's office.

He is not going to let you through on your own authority.

**Combat — Court Household Guard (AC 13, HP 24, short sword)**

*If you fail (reduced to 0 HP):* You are held in the passage while the chamberlain is summoned. By the time you reach Ingeborg, his version has preceded you.

*If you pass:* The guard is down. You have perhaps four minutes before the corridor fills. Ingeborg's door is at the end of the passage.

---

### Act V — Ingeborg's Chamber
*Ring's Signet Ring · in the Fighter's open hand · Ingeborg's chamber,
a window, morning light, Ingeborg at a writing board, turning*

She turns when you enter. She reads your face before you speak. She has been sitting
at this board since early morning, knowing something was happening in the corridor
outside Ring's chamber and unable to go to him directly while the court is assembled.

You open your hand. The ring is there.

She looks at it. She does not take it yet. She is waiting for the four sentences.

You say them. Exactly as he said them. In the order he specified.

*"He knew from the first week. He saw the fighting posture in the second exercise.
He chose to say nothing because the welcome was already given and already meant.
Frithiof has no debt. None of what happened here was a trap."*

She looks at you for a moment. Then she takes the ring from your palm.

She does not ask you to repeat anything. She does not ask for context. She holds
the ring and looks at it. The window is bright. Outside, in the court below, you
can hear the chamberlain beginning to organize the formal announcement of the king's
condition.

*Ring's Signet Ring rests in Ingeborg's closed hand. The four sentences are in the
room. The chamberlain's version, when it comes, will come into a room where this
one was already said.*

**DC 11 Composure — say the four sentences in the exact order Ring specified; do not add context; do not soften; let the room do its own work.**

*If you fail:* You say three correctly and paraphrase the fourth. The paraphrase is close. But it is not what he said, and she knows it is a paraphrase, and the difference between "I chose it" and "it was genuine" is a difference she will carry for the rest of her life.

*If you pass:* Four sentences. His words. She closes her hand. You have carried what he asked you to carry and it arrived without transformation.

## Cycle 4 — *Angantyr's Receipt*

**Archive category:** Tributary Records — The Partial Payment Acknowledged as Complete by the Right Authority, First Entry

**What this angle reveals:** Cycle 3 was about a love token. This angle is about a political problem: Frithiof was sent to collect tribute from the Orkney Islands that has never been paid, on a mission everyone expects him to fail. He collects some tribute but not all. The political validity of the collection depends not on the sum but on the Orkney jarl's sealing mark — his official acknowledgment that the tribute transaction occurred. The Fighter carries the partial tribute to Angantyr and must persuade him to seal it as payment. The seal is the politics; the sum is secondary.

---

**THE TOKEN: `angantyr_tribute_receipt`** — Angantyr's Sealed Receipt. A piece of birchwood with the Orkney jarl's notch-mark and his thrall's impression at the bottom — the official acknowledgment that tribute was received, in a form the Norwegian court recognizes as valid. Without the seal, the partial tribute is a failed collection. With it, the mission is complete.

---

**ACT I — The Orkney Hall**

*Grant act. Location: Angantyr's hall on the Orkney main island — a solid Norse hall, a jarl who has not paid tribute in twenty years and has opinions about why.*

Angantyr is not hostile. He is curious: he has not seen a Sognefjord man since the original tribute demand, and that man never came back. He received Frithiof with the formal courtesy due a genuine warrior and a worthy ship.

The tribute question is complicated. He has the goods — he has always had them — but he did not pay because the political situation in Sognefjord made payment look like endorsing a faction he had not endorsed. The accession of Helge and Halfdan was contested; paying tribute to a contested king is a political statement.

**Persuasion DC 13** — give Angantyr a reason to seal the partial payment that does not require him to endorse the political claim he has been avoiding for twenty years. Frame it as a personal transaction between warriors, not a political submission.

---

**ACT II — The Counting**

*Check act. Location: Angantyr's hall — the tribute goods being counted, a thrall with the official marking tools.*

Angantyr has agreed to seal the receipt in principle. The counting begins: the goods available, the shortfall. The shortfall is significant. Angantyr's thrall is noting both numbers.

Angantyr looks at the numbers.

**Angantyr:** "The seal can cover what is here. But the receipt will name the shortfall."

A receipt that names a shortfall is technically a record of partial failure. The alternative: a receipt that names only what was paid, not what was owed. The difference is in what the document says about the original obligation — whether it acknowledges the full sum or only the delivered sum.

## Pass 4: Source Summary (June 2026)

### Complete Narrative Arc Summary
Frithiof's Saga (Tegnér's 1825 romantic epic) traces a single unified narrative: from the childhood love between Frithiof and Ingeborg through his exile, trials, and redemptive return. The saga opens with Frithiof as a young warrior proving himself in contests, then taking Balder's sacred arm-ring as an oath-token, which brings divine judgment and exile upon him. He is forced to leave Sognefjord. The middle section chronicles his travels and the famous storm at sea—a trial that tests both his courage and his character. The saga's final movement shows his return, his restitution (returning the arm-ring, restoring his honor), and his union with Ingeborg. Unlike the medieval sagas or the Poetic Edda, Tegnér's Frithiof emphasizes love and redemption as much as honor and war. The narrative arc is romantic and Christian in sensibility, making it distinct among the Norse sources.
