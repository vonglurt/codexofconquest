## Pass 1 — Overview

The Ramayana is one of the two great Hindu epics (the other being the Mahabharata). Valmiki's Sanskrit original comprises approximately 24,000 couplets in seven books (kandas). Griffith's 1870 verse translation renders the first six books in English rhymed couplets, preserving the epic's ceremonial register.

The story follows Rama, prince of Ayodhya and avatar of Vishnu, from birth through his exile, the abduction of his wife Sita by the demon-king Ravana, the war to recover her, and his eventual return to rule. The epic is simultaneously a narrative of dharma (right action), devotion, kingship, and loyalty — with Rama as the ideal man and Sita as the ideal wife, both tested to the limits of endurance.

The Ramayana contains several features with no parallel in other sources processed: the origin myth of poetry itself (the first sloka composed from grief), the trial-by-bow bride-price, Kaikeyi's deferred boon as a political weapon, the leap of Hanuman across the sea, and Sita's fire ordeal as proof of chastity that nonetheless fails to protect her.

Part 1 of 12 covers: the epic's frame (Valmiki's invocation, Brahma's commission, Narad's synopsis), the mythology of the setting (Dasharatha's horse sacrifice, Vishnu's incarnation plan, Ganga's descent), and the beginning of Book I proper (Rama's birth, Vishwamitra's visit, the killing of Tadaka, celestial weapons given to Rama, the march toward Mithila). The full story arc is recovered from Canto I's complete synopsis.

## Pass 2 — Structure

### The Six Books (Kandas) — Story Arc

**Book I: Bala Kanda (Book of Youth)**
Valmiki composes the epic after Brahma's commission. Narad summarizes the whole story. Ayodhya described: a gleaming city, King Dasharatha childless and grieving. He performs the Asvamedha (horse sacrifice); the sage Rishyasring officiates; a divine being emerges from the sacrificial fire bearing nectar for the three queens. Each drinks; Kaushalya bears Rama (Vishnu's avatar, half his essence), Kaikeyi bears Bharat (one quarter), Sumitra bears twins Lakshmana and Shatrughna (one quarter between them).

The sage Vishwamitra arrives requesting Rama to guard his sacrifice from demons. Dasharatha is terrified, tries to refuse. Vasishtha persuades him. Rama and Lakshmana depart with Vishwamitra: they receive the spells Bala and Atibala. They kill the demoness Tadaka in the Forest of Tadaka. Vishwamitra gives Rama 36 named celestial weapons. They travel toward Mithila, hearing long mythological stories en route (Ganga's descent, Sagara's 60,000 sons, Bhagiratha's penance).

At Mithila, King Janak displays the bow of Shiva: an iron bow so heavy it cannot be moved by demons, gods, or ordinary men. Janak has vowed his daughter Sita to the man who can string it. Rama lifts, strings, and breaks it. The earth shakes. Sita is given to Rama in marriage. Lakshmana weds Urmila. Rama's other brothers also marry Janak's nieces. The weddings are celebrated. On the road home, Rama defeats Parashurama (a different Rama, bearer of Vishnu's bow), who tests him; Rama proves himself the true avatar.

**Book II: Ayodhya Kanda (Book of Ayodhya)**
Dasharatha prepares Rama's coronation. The minister Manthara poisons Kaikeyi's mind: if Bharat is king, Kaikeyi's son rules; if Rama is king, Kaushalya's line holds power. Kaikeyi uses two boons promised by Dasharatha long ago on a battlefield where she saved his life: first, Bharat on the throne; second, Rama exiled 14 years into the forest. Dasharatha is shattered but cannot refuse — the boons are sacred debts. Rama accepts the exile without bitterness, departs with Sita and Lakshmana. Dasharatha dies of grief. Bharat returns, is horrified, pursues Rama to the forest to beg him to return. Rama refuses — the king's word is sacred. Bharat takes Rama's sandals and places them on the throne, ruling as regent in Rama's name, living austerely in a small village outside Ayodhya, refusing to sit on the throne himself.

**Book III: Aranya Kanda (Book of the Forest)**
Fourteen years in the Dandaka Forest. Rama and Lakshmana visit hermits, kill demons. The demoness Surpanakha falls in love with Rama, is disfigured by Lakshmana. Her brothers Khara and Dushana attack with armies; Rama kills 14,000 demons. Ravana, hearing of this, is enraged. He traps Rama and Lakshmana by sending the magic deer Maricha to lure them away, then abducts Sita in their absence. The vulture Jatayu tries to stop Ravana and is mortally wounded. Rama finds Jatayu dying and learns the direction of Sita's abduction. Rama and Lakshmana, lost and grieving, encounter the demon Kabandha, kill him, and release his trapped spirit — Kabandha counsels Rama to seek alliance with Sugriva the monkey-chief.

**Book IV: Kishkindha Kanda (Book of Kishkindha)**
Rama meets Hanuman on the way to Kishkindha. Alliance with Sugriva: Rama kills Bali (Sugriva's brother who took his wife and throne) with an arrow from ambush. Sugriva is restored to his kingdom and his wife. The four quarters of the earth are searched for Sita. The bird Sampati (Jatayu's brother) reports Sita is in Lanka across the sea. Hanuman is identified as the one who can leap the sea — son of the Wind God, unrestricted in size and speed.

**Book V: Sundara Kanda (Book of Beauty)**
Hanuman leaps 200 leagues to Lanka. He finds Sita under an Ashoka tree, tormented but refusing to give up. He gives her Rama's ring; she gives him a gem from her hair. He destroys the Ashoka grove and kills five of Ravana's captains. He allows himself to be captured so he can confront Ravana; his tail is set on fire; he burns the city and escapes. He returns to Rama with Sita's token.

**Book VI: Lanka Kanda (Book of Lanka) / Yuddha Kanda**
Rama approaches the sea. The Ocean God appears and agrees to hold still if a bridge can be built. Nala (son of the divine craftsman Vishvakarma) builds a stone bridge. The monkey army crosses. Ravana's brother Vibhishana defects to Rama. Battle: Kumbhakarna (Ravana's giant brother) is killed; Meghanada (Ravana's son, who earlier had defeated Indra himself) is killed by Lakshmana. Ravana comes to battle; after a long duel Rama kills him with the divine arrow of Brahma. Vibhishana is crowned king of Lanka. Sita is brought out — Rama, publicly, under pressure to demonstrate she is not compromised, asks her to prove her purity. Sita walks into fire. The Fire God Agni brings her out unharmed and certifies her chastity. Rama reclaims her, returns to Ayodhya in the flying chariot Pushpaka, meets Bharat, is crowned king. The Golden Age begins.

### Part 1 Detailed Content (Lines 1–597)

**Cantos I–IV (Lines 62–144):** Invocation to Valmiki. Narad's complete synopsis of the entire story. Brahma's commission of the poem. The rhapsodists Kusha and Lava, Valmiki's twin sons, perform the epic in Rama's own court.

**Cantos V–VII (Lines 149–178):** Ayodhya described. King Dasharatha and his court.

**Cantos VIII–X (Lines 179–210):** Sumantra's speech about the prophecy. Rishyasring the forest-hermit lured to civilization by dancing girls; he marries Shanta; officiates at Dasharatha's sacrifice.

**Cantos XI–XIII (Lines 213–234):** The horse sacrifice fully described. 21 stakes, 300 victims, three queens touch the horse. Rishyasring promises Dasharatha four sons.

**Cantos XIV–XV (Lines 239–270):** The gods petition Brahma to stop Ravana. Vishnu agrees to incarnate. The divine nectar descends from the fire; the queens drink. Each queen conceives.

**Canto XVI (Lines 275–280):** The Vanaras (monkey warriors) created by the gods to serve Vishnu-Rama in battle — each born of a god, each possessing divine power.

**Cantos XVII–XIX (Lines 285–322):** Rishyasring departs. The four princes are born. Rama described: lotus-eyed, dark, with all auspicious marks. He and Lakshmana are inseparable from childhood.

**Cantos XX–XXIV (Lines 327–366):** Vishwamitra arrives. Demands Rama to guard his sacrifice. Dasharatha's terror; Vasishtha persuades him. Vishwamitra reveals the demons are servants of Ravana.

**Cantos XXIV–XXXII (Lines 371–462):** Rama and Lakshmana depart. Bala and Atibala spells given. The Forest of Love (Anga). The Forest of Tadaka — the demoness's origin story. Tadaka killed by Rama. The celestial arms (36 named weapons) given by Vishwamitra to Rama. The Retreat of Vishnu (Perfect Hermitage). Vishwamitra's sacrifice: six days of watching; Marica and Subahu attack; Marica thrown 100 leagues out to sea; Subahu killed.

**Cantos XXXIII–XLIV (Lines 467–596):** March toward Mithila. The river Sona. Vishwamitra's lineage told. The birth of Ganga: Himavat's daughter given to the gods, then made to descend to earth through Bhagiratha's penance. The legend of Sagara's 60,000 sons (they dig through the earth, are burned by Kapila, their ashes must be washed by Ganga). Bhagiratha's thousand-year penance. Shiva agrees to hold Ganga in his hair.

### Part 2 Detailed Content (Lines 1–699 of part2of12.txt)

**Cantos XLV–LII (Ganga's descent continued, ocean churning):** Bhagiratha's thousand-year penance succeeds; Shiva holds Ganga in his matted hair for a year, then releases her in seven streams. The ashes of Sagara's 60,000 sons are washed and liberated. The churning of the cosmic ocean by gods and demons using Mount Mandara as the churn and Vasuki the great serpent as the rope: Vishnu in tortoise form holds the mountain from below. From the churning emerge: poison (drunk by Shiva to save the world), Lakshmi the goddess of wealth, the divine physician Dhanvantari bearing the pot of Amrit (immortality nectar), the wish-fulfilling horse, the divine physician's gifts, and finally the Amrit itself. Demons seize the Amrit; Vishnu incarnates as a beautiful woman (Mohini) to distract them; gods drink; demons are defeated. The demon Rahu, disguised as a god, drinks some Amrit before Vishnu's disc severs his head — creating the demons of eclipse.

**Cantos LIII–LIX (Diti's cycle, Ahalyá's release):** Diti, mother of the defeated Asuras, asks Indra's help in bearing a son who will destroy him; Indra enters her womb and cuts the fetus into 49 pieces — these become the Maruts, storm gods loyal to Indra. Then: Ahalyá's story. The sage Gautama's wife Ahalyá is approached by Indra in Gautama's exact form. The text is explicit: she recognizes him but yields. Gautama catches them, curses Indra (who loses his testicles, later replaced by ram's testicles by the gods), and curses Ahalyá to become stone until Rama's foot touches her. Rama touches her; she is released; the episode is concluded with Gautama taking her back.

**Cantos LX–LXV (Vishwamitra's prior history — Vasishtha's cow):** Vishwamitra was originally a king, not a sage. He visited Vasishtha's hermitage with 10,000 soldiers. Vasishtha's wish-fulfilling cow Shabala fed the army entirely. Vishwamitra tried to buy the cow; Vasishtha refused at any price. Vishwamitra's soldiers tried to take her by force; the cow generated armies from her body (Pahlava warriors from her tail, Sakas from her dung, Yavanas from her urine) and defeated them all. Vishwamitra's 100 sons attacked Vasishtha and were burned to ash by his rod. Vishwamitra resolved to achieve brahminhood through penance in order to defeat Vasishtha on equal terms — this is the cause of his entire quest for sage status.

**Cantos LXVI–LXVII (Trishanku's sky commission):** King Trishanku asked Vasishtha to conduct a sacrifice that would raise him bodily to heaven. Vasishtha refused (impossible; not dharmic). Trishanku went to Vishwamitra, who accepted out of rivalry with Vasishtha. Vishwamitra conducted the sacrifice; the gods refused to accept it. Vishwamitra, in a fury, began creating an entire second heaven and a second set of gods by force of penance. The gods, alarmed, agreed to admit Trishanku to a special region of the sky — where he hangs upside-down forever, as a compromise between the gods' refusal and Vishwamitra's insistence.

**Cantos LXVIII–LXIX (Sunahsepha's sacrifice):** King Ambarisha had vowed a human sacrifice to appease Varuna. He bought Sunahsepha, son of the sage Richika, for the victim price. Sunahsepha was bound to the sacrificial post. He prayed to the gods through Vishwamitra's instruction; each hymn he sang loosened his bonds slightly. He was released and adopted by Vishwamitra. The text presents Sunahsepha as willing (he agreed to the role) and the transaction as legal — Richika sold his own son.

**Cantos LXX–LXXIII (Menaka and Rambha, Vishwamitra's brahminhood):** Two tests of Vishwamitra by Indra. First: the apsara Menaka is sent to seduce him during his penance. She succeeds; they live together for years; Vishwamitra eventually shakes her off in shame and redoubles his penance. Second: the apsara Rambha is sent. Vishwamitra recognizes the trap immediately and curses Rambha to become stone for 10,000 years. After another thousand years of unbroken penance, Brahma grants Vishwamitra full brahminhood. Vasishtha acknowledges it. The rivalry ends.

**Canto LXXIV (Sita's birth from earth — Janak's vow):** Janak, king of Mithila, was plowing a ritual furrow in his sacrificial field when a baby girl appeared in the furrow. He named her Sita ("furrow"). She grew in the palace without a named mother; her bride-price was the bow of Shiva. Janak made the vow: the man who can string Shiva's bow marries Sita.

**Cantos LXXV–LXXXII (Bow trial, four marriages):** Arrival at Mithila. The bow is housed in a building requiring 5,000 men to carry it on wheels. Rama lifts it one-handed, strings it, draws it — the tension is so great the bow shatters with a sound like a thunderclap. The earth shakes. Janak proclaims the match. Rama is to wed Sita; Lakshmana weds Sita's younger sister Urmila. Messengers are sent to bring Dasharatha and the court from Ayodhya. Bharata and Shatrughna are also to marry: two daughters of Janak's brother Kushadhwaja — Mandavi and Srutakirti. Four weddings performed simultaneously. The gods shower flowers.

**Canto LXXXIII (Parashurama's challenge):** On the road home, Parashurama (the sage with an axe, avatar of Vishnu, destroyer of the Kshatriya caste 21 times over) intercepts the party. He challenges Rama: if Rama is truly worthy of the bow he broke, can he now draw Vishnu's own bow? Rama takes it, draws it fully, aims an arrow at Parashurama — asks where it should be aimed (it cannot be aimed at a brahmin). Parashurama points to his own accumulated spiritual merit from past austerities. Rama releases the arrow; Parashurama's merit is consumed and he stands diminished. He acknowledges Rama as Vishnu's true avatar and departs.

**Book II begins — Cantos I–VI (Ayodhya Kanda: Coronation preparation):** Ayodhya is lavishly described. Dasharatha, old and sensing his end approaching, announces that the coronation of Rama will take place tomorrow. The city erupts in joy. Preparations begin: garlands, banners, ritual materials. Everyone in Ayodhya is described as approving — the people love Rama, the court loves Rama, even the gods signal approval. The night before the coronation is the pivot on which the entire next book turns.

**Cantos VII–XI (Manthara's conspiracy, Kaikeyi's boons):** Manthara is Kaikeyi's hunchbacked nurse. She sees the coronation preparations from the palace roof and runs to warn Kaikeyi: if Rama is crowned, Kaushalya's line holds power; Kaikeyi and her son Bharata will be reduced to servants of Rama's household. Kaikeyi at first rejects this — she loves Rama. Manthara persists through five separate arguments. She reminds Kaikeyi of the two boons Dasharatha promised her on the battlefield of the gods (when she served as his charioteer and saved his life; she extracted a deferred right to two wishes). Kaikeyi is persuaded. She strips off her jewelry, puts on a single rough garment, and enters the "mourner's chamber" — a formal room of complaint that a Kshatriya queen has the right to use to force her husband to hear her. Part 2 ends as Dasharatha enters, finds her there, and she begins to speak. The actual boons have not yet been named at the text cut point.

### Part 3 Detailed Content (Lines 1–573 of part3of12.txt)

**Cantos XII–XIX (Kaikeyi names the boons; Rama's acceptance):** The morning of the coronation. Bards wake Dasharatha with praises; he summons Rama. Kaikeyi names both boons directly to Rama: Bharat to be crowned, Rama exiled 14 years to Dandaka Forest. Dasharatha cannot speak — Kaikeyi speaks for him. Rama accepts without hesitation or bitterness, citing filial duty (the son who refuses his father's word loses the merit of all his past acts). Lakshmana erupts in rage, offering to seize the throne by force and kill those who resist; Rama talks him down systematically, attributing everything to Fate, refusing to call their father or Kaikeyi guilty. Kausalya collapses and laments; Rama soothes her, asking her to remain with the king while he is gone, citing the Vedic principle that a woman's first duty is to her living husband.

**Cantos XX–XXVIII (Sita insists; the people's grief):** Rama tells Sita he is going alone; Sita refuses, lists all the woes of the forest he describes and says none of it frightens her when he is near; she cites brahmins who once prophesied she would live in the forest. Rama argues at length (lions, serpents, hunger, rain); Sita responds with a taunt — "Why did your father call you a hero when you are a woman in a man's form?" — and threatens to drown herself if left behind. Rama relents. The city learns Rama is leaving; women weep from rooftops and balconies; crowds block the chariot route. Rama distributes his entire wealth before departure — a specific scene where the brahmin Trijata throws his staff to claim 1,000 cows as far as it lands across the river.

**Cantos XXIX–XL (The bark coats; formal departure):** Kaikeyi publicly produces bark hermit coats and hands them to Rama and Lakshmana in front of the assembled court. Rama ties his own on over his royal robes without protest. Sita is confused by the knot; Rama ties hers for her over her silk dress. Vasishtha protests sharply to Kaikeyi that Sita has no obligation to wear hermit dress — she is not exiled. Dasharatha protests too; Kaikeyi's face shows neither shame nor give. Rama formally asks forgiveness of all the queens for any offense he may have given. Kausalya's elaborate multi-verse blessing. Sumitra's specific charge to Lakshmana: "Regard Rama as your father, Sita as your mother, and the forest as Ayodhya." Sumantra's chariot: Rama mounts, Sita mounts, Lakshmana mounts. The crowd follows, clinging to the chariot's frame, weeping. Dasharatha pursues on foot until he collapses.

**Cantos XLI–XLVII (Tamasa River; the false track):** The chariot reaches the Tamasa River before nightfall. Rama camps. The people of Ayodhya who have followed all lie down and sleep on the riverbank, expecting to be present in the morning. In the night, Rama wakes, sees them sleeping, and tells Sumantra to slip away quietly before dawn and drive north first — a deliberate false track — then turn south and proceed toward the forest. When the crowd wakes, there are wheel tracks going north and none returning east; they cannot determine which road Rama took and finally give up. The false track is Rama's last gift to them: without it they would follow until they died.

**Cantos XLVIII–LIII (Ganga crossing; first night in the forest):** Rama crosses several rivers of Kosala: Vedashruti, Gomati, Syandika — all named, each with specific sacred associations. He reaches the Ganga. Guha the Nishada king — an old friend, sovereign of a tribal people on the Ganga's bank — comes to meet him with food and beds and staff. Rama refuses all except horse fodder; he accepts only what a hermit may accept. Sumantra is formally sent back with a multi-part message for Dasharatha (I don't grieve), for Kausalya (look after the king), for Bharat (treat all queens equally and serve with duty), and implicitly for Kaikeyi (let Bharat be crowned; your boons are honored). Sumantra refuses to go and has to be argued into it. Rama and Lakshmana get their hair braided into hermit coils with fig-tree sap. They cross the Ganga in Guha's boat; Sita prays aloud to the river goddess with specific vows (100,000 cows, 100 jars of wine, offerings to all shrines on the bank) if Rama returns safely. On the far bank the chariot is left behind permanently; they enter the forest on foot. First night: they shoot a deer, roast it over a fire of branches, eat, and sleep under a great fig tree. Lakshmana keeps watch all night.

### Part 4 Detailed Content (Lines 1–605 of part4of12.txt)

**Cantos LIV–LVI (Chitrakuta established):** Rama's party reaches Bharadvaja's hermitage at the confluence of the Yamuna and Ganga (Prayag). The sage welcomes them and directs them to Chitrakuta hill, 10 leagues to the south. They cross the Yamuna on a self-built raft; Sita prays to the Yamuna goddess with vows matching her earlier Ganga prayer. At Chitrakuta they find the sage Valmiki (the epic's narrator is introduced as a forest hermit). Lakshmana builds a leaf cottage; they shoot and dress a deer, perform the house-consecration rites with the meat, and establish a permanent home. Rama describes Chitrakuta at length to Sita — the mountain's mineral colors, hermits bathing in the Mandakini river, the birds and elephants and bears — and says he is content.

**Cantos LVII–LXII (Sumantra's return; Dasharatha's illness and death):** Sumantra returns to Ayodhya with the empty chariot. The city weeps at the sight. He is brought to Dasharatha who demands a full account. Sumantra reports Lakshmana's angry words (blaming the king entirely) and Sita's silent grief (tears, no speech). The king asks to hear everything again and then tells Kausalya the story he has never told before: on a night hunt in his youth he shot an arrow at a sound in the river and killed a young hermit boy who was filling a water jar for his blind parents. The blind hermit's parents touched the dead body, cursed Dasharatha: "as you grieve for your son, so shall you die." Dasharatha says the time has come. He dies in the night, lying beside Kausalya, weeping "Rama! Rama!" The palace women find him dead at dawn. His body is embalmed in oil — the brahmins refuse to cremate him until Rama returns.

**Cantos LXIII–LXXVI (Bharat's return and fury):** Vasishtha summons Bharat urgently from Kekaya. Envoys travel a long road (many rivers named) in 7 nights; Bharat has a prophetic dream of his father on an ass-drawn chariot going south, carried by a dark laughing woman — the standard death-dream in the epic's system. He returns in 7 nights at full gallop. Kaikeyi tells him: your father is dead, Rama is exiled, you are king. Bharat's grief and rage: he rejects Kaikeyi entirely as his mother, then delivers a long formal catalog of curses against anyone who gave consent to the exile. He falls prostrate; Kausalya finds him there; he swears he knew nothing and approved nothing. Funeral: 10 days mourning, 12th day gifts to Brahmans, 13th day gathering of ashes from the pyre. Shatrughna catches the hunchback Manthara in fine jewels and drags her through the palace; Bharat stops him before he kills her — Rama would hate a matricide.

**Cantos LXXVII–XCII (Bharat's march to Chitrakuta; Bharadvaja's feast):** Bharat declares to the court assembly: Rama is king; he himself will go to the forest and bring Rama back; he orders road-builders and engineers to clear a path. A massive army assembles — 9,000 elephants, 60,000 chariots, 100,000 cavalry. They march to the Ganga; Guha suspects hostile intent and arms his fishermen, then is convinced by Bharat's declaration of love for Rama. They cross the Ganga to Prayag; Bharat approaches Bharadvaja's hermitage alone on foot, with only priests. Bharadvaja tests Bharat ("Did you come to kill your brother?"); Bharat protests innocence. The sage then entertains the entire army for one night by calling on Vishvakarma: a celestial palace appears, 20,000 divine nymphs arrive, rivers turn to milk and wine, every food appears in abundance. The army refuses to leave ("This is heaven; we will not go to Ayodhya or Dandaka"). In the morning Bharadvaja tells them where Chitrakuta is.

**Cantos XCIII–XCVI (Chitrakuta idyll; the crow):** The army spots smoke from Chitrakuta. Bharat halts them and goes ahead with a small party. Meanwhile Rama has been showing Sita the beauties of the mountain — its mineral-colored cliffs, the hermits bathing in the Mandakini, a monkey encounter, the Ashoka grove where they weave flower crowns and Rama paints Sita's brow with red mineral ore. Then: a crow repeatedly pecks Sita's feet. Rama fires a magic arrow at it charmed with a spell; the arrow pursues the crow through all three worlds; the crow surrenders. Rama cannot withdraw the arrow, so asks the crow what it will sacrifice. The crow offers one eye; lives.

**Cantos XCVII–XCVIII (Lakshmana mistakes the army; Rama's rebuke):** Lakshmana climbs a Sal tree and sees the army approaching. He misidentifies the Kovidara-wood flag as Bharat coming to kill Rama and seize the whole kingdom. He rages, offering to destroy the entire army, kill Bharat, Kaikeyi, all of them. Rama calmly corrects him: Bharat has done nothing wrong; we could see no fault in him at any point; probably he is coming to restore the kingship; I would sooner die than spill a brother's blood. Lakshmana repents and stands ashamed. Part 4 ends with Bharat's army camped around Chitrakuta hill, spread half a league in every direction.

### Part 5 Detailed Content (Lines 1–707 of part5of12.txt)

**Cantos XCIX–CIII (Bharat reaches Chitrakuta; funeral libation):** Bharat's army surrounds the hill. Bharat goes alone on foot to Rama's leaf cottage. The brothers meet. Rama learns from Bharat that Dasharatha is dead. The brothers go to the Mandakini river to perform the funeral water-libation — the specific rite for a father who has died in the carrier's absence. The libation is done in silence.

**Cantos CIV–CXI (The debate over Rama's return):** Bharat pleads five times across multiple cantos for Rama to return and rule. Rama refuses each time, citing one principle: the king's word is a sacred debt that the son cannot dissolve. Between the pleas, the philosopher Javalin makes a full atheist argument to Rama — gods do not exist, the afterlife is a priestly invention, dead men have no will and living oaths to them have no force; therefore Rama should return. Javalin argues this explicitly and at length. Rama rejects it entirely: truth is the highest dharma, not the living man's comfort. He says he could sooner lose Lakshmana, Sita, or his own life than break a promise. Javalin immediately retracts — he only spoke thus to test Rama's resolution. Vasishtha then recites the full genealogy of Rama's lineage from Brahma through Manu and Ikshvaku to Dasharatha — the recitation that certifies Rama's fitness to be exactly as bound by duty as he is. Bharat's final refusal is accepted.

**Canto CXII (The Sandals as regency):** Bharat asks Rama to place his golden sandals on Bharat's feet. Rama places them. Bharat removes them and places them on the royal elephant's head. He declares publicly: these sandals will be installed on the throne of Ayodhya; all state business will be conducted in their name; he will rule only as their agent; if Rama does not return on the fourteenth year's last day, Bharat will enter fire.

**Cantos CXIII–CXV (Nandigram; Bharat as regent):** Bharat departs with the sandals installed on the throne at Nandigram, outside Ayodhya proper. He wears hermit dress, grows matted hair, eats only fruit. Every state petition is laid before the sandals first. He refers all decisions to them. The city of Ayodhya is described: silent, dark, cats and owls roaming empty streets, music and flowers gone, the river pools dying. The description is one of sustained civic grief.

**Canto CXVI (The hermits' warning; demons in Dandaka):** The hermits near Chitrakuta tell Rama that Khara's demons (Ravana's brother's forces) have been intensifying attacks on the forest saints — rending bodies, extinguishing sacred fires, defiling altars. The hermits cannot curse the demons without losing all their accumulated penance-merit. They ask Rama to protect them or depart. The saints then leave their own hermitage. Rama refuses to leave — he will protect them.

**Canto CXVII (Atri's hermitage; Anasuyá's teaching):** Rama and party move to the hermitage of the sage Atri. Atri's wife Anasuyá — a very ancient female devotee, white-haired, shaking — receives Sita. She gives Sita an extended teaching on wifely virtue: the woman who follows her husband through hardship is elevated; even a wicked or faithless husband is the wife's proper deity; Sita who follows Rama is the highest type.

**Canto CXVIII (Anasuyá's gifts; Sita tells her own story):** Sita recounts to Anasuyá how she was found in the ritual furrow, how Janak raised her, how the bow trial worked. Anasuyá rewards Sita's virtue with three celestial gifts: a divine robe of heavenly fabric, ornamental gems, and a balsam that will make her limbs shine with permanent beauty — "as Lakshmi's beauty lends grace to Vishnu's face." Sita puts the robe on; she returns to Rama glowing.

**Canto CXIX / Book III, Canto I (Dandaka Forest; the hermit settlement):** They enter the great Dandaka Forest. A hermit settlement blazes with ascetic radiance. The hermits formally acknowledge Rama as their king — citing the principle that the king receives one-quarter of a subject's penance-merit in exchange for protection. They ask him to guard them.

Then he speaks of Bharat at Nandigram: sleeping on cold earth, eating little, living in hermit dress, holding the kingdom only for Rama. Rama is moved. Each Vanar names his maximum range — 10, 20, 30, 40 leagues, in sequence. Jambavan names his own age — in his prime he circled the earth; now he can do 90 leagues but cannot land safely. Angad announces he can do 100 leagues, will do it himself. The party argues: a commander cannot take a risk that might leave them leaderless. Who then?

**Cantos LXII–LXIII (Jambavan rouses Hanuman):** Jambavan approaches Hanuman, who has been sitting silent at the group's edge. He delivers a formal speech of praise — recounting Hanuman's origins, birth-power, early exploits, and the reason he has been holding his strength back. He says: "Why do you sit here like an ordinary person? Don't you know what you are?" Hanuman begins to grow. He rises from the shore, grows to the size of a mountain, tail lashing. He says: he will leap to Lanka. He will land there as a cat.

**Cantos LXIV–LXV (Hanuman's origin):** Hanuman gives his own account. His mother Anjana was an apsara cursed into Vanar form. The Wind God passed her in a forest grove and Hanuman was born. As an infant he saw the rising sun and, thinking it a red fruit, leaped for it — 30,000 leagues in one morning jump. He grabbed Rahu (the eclipsing demon) by mistake. Rahu fled to Indra. Indra threw his thunderbolt. Hanuman fell on a mountain, his jaw broken (hanu = jaw; hence the name). The Wind God, furious, withdrew all wind from the world. Brahma, seeing the disaster, restored Hanuman and granted him immunity — no weapon would wound him unless he chose. Indra gave him one further gift: death only when he wills it.

**Book V, Canto I (The leap; Mainaka):** Hanuman compresses himself at the summit of a coastal mountain, then erupts from it — the mountaintop cracks from his launch force. The gods and Gandharvas assemble in the sky to watch. Mainaka — a mountain that was once winged, before Indra clipped all mountains' wings, and which now rests under the ocean — rises at Varuna's command to offer itself as a resting platform. Hanuman touches it briefly out of courtesy and does not land: no time.

**Cantos II–III (Surasa and Sinhika):** Surasa — a she-monster sent by the gods as a test — rises from the sea and declares that nothing may pass her without entering her mouth first. Hanuman grows; she grows to match him. He shrinks to thumb-size, enters her mouth, and exits before she can swallow. She releases him with praise: "Nothing can stop you." Farther south, Sinhika — a demon who feeds by catching shadows — reaches up from below the water's surface and grips Hanuman's shadow, stopping his flight. He senses the drag, dives, shrinks, enters Sinhika's open mouth, tears her vitals, bursts out. She dies. He continues.

**Canto IV (Lanka seen from the air):** He lands on the coastal peak of Lamba and sees Lanka: white walls, gold parapets, moats filled with lotus flowers, terraced gardens, flagged towers, streets paved with crystal. The city blazes like a cloud bank at sunset. Peacocks call from every spire. Hanuman thinks: this city is defended by more than walls. He must enter small.

**Cantos V–VII (Entry as a cat; the guardian goddess Lanka):** He shrinks to cat-size as night falls and approaches the city gate. The goddess Lanka — the city itself embodied — blocks his path: armored, terrible, the size of a thundercloud. Hanuman strikes her with his left fist. She falls. Rising, she speaks: Brahma gave her a prophecy — when she is struck down and falls, Lanka's end is near. She lets him pass.

**Cantos VIII–XII (Search through the palace district):** Moving through Lanka by night, Hanuman maps each quarter: Prahasta's house, Kumbhakarna's enormous courtyard, the generals' residences, the treasury. He enters Ravana's palace — the Pushpaka car is described: a celestial vehicle of living gold, capable of traveling at the speed of thought, shaped like a mountain with terraces and balconies, currently parked. He finds the ladies' bower — thousands of sleeping women in elaborate poetic detail: their jewels, loosened hair, garments, wine-flushed faces, sleeping attitudes.

**Canto XIII (Ravana asleep):** Hanuman finds Ravana's own chamber. Ravana lies asleep — dark as a thundercloud, arms like iron pillars, his chest bearing named battle-scars: the mark of Airavata's tusk from the fight with Indra, the mark of Kubera's mace. He breathes like a mountain. His ten heads are placed at rest. He is surrounded by the most beautiful women in the three worlds.

**Canto XIV (Mandodari; the correction):** Among the sleeping women, Hanuman sees one surpassing all the others in beauty — adorned with the finest ornaments, calm, radiant, at ease — and feels a surge of certainty: this is Sita, found. Then he corrects himself, thinking through the logic: Sita, imprisoned and grieving and refusing Ravana's suit, would not be lying at ease in his own bedchamber dressed in festival ornaments. This woman sleeps here by her own will. This is Mandodari, Ravana's primary queen. Beauty alone had nearly deceived him.

**Cantos XV–XVI (Banquet hall; despair; renewed search):** He searches the banquet hall — musicians, wine vessels, food remains, sleeping women still holding instruments. Still no Sita. He considers: what if she was eaten? He reviews the evidence — Sampati's witness, the dropped ornaments, the months of captivity — and concludes she must be somewhere in Lanka. He begins searching again.

**Cantos XVII–XIX (Ashoka grove; Sita found):** He enters the Ashoka grove — a garden attached to the palace complex, described as the most beautiful enclosed space in the three worlds: golden trees, crystal paths, birds of impossible color. At the base of a Simsapa tree, in shade, a woman — thin, weeping, in rough bark garment, hair braided into a single braid (the braid of a captive), wearing no ornaments except one gem in her hair-knot, surrounded by sleeping she-demons. Her beauty declares itself even in this state. She can only be Sita. Hanuman settles in the tree above her.

**Cantos XX–XXII (Ravana's wooing; Sita's refusals; the ultimatum):** Ravana arrives at the grove with a torch-carrying retinue, repeating his suit — listing Lanka's wealth, his power over gods and demons, the honor she would have as his primary queen. Sita refuses contemptuously, placing a blade of grass between herself and him as a symbolic barrier. Ravana gives his final ultimatum: two months; after that his cooks will prepare her. He leaves. The she-demons circle Sita with threats and entreaties.

**Canto XXIII (Trijata's dream):** An older she-demon named Trijata speaks up. She orders the others to sleep. When they wake she tells her dream: Rama and Lakshmana approaching in white clothes on a white chariot drawn by a white elephant; Ravana shaved, smeared with oil, driven south on a donkey-cart toward Yama's realm; Lanka sinking below the ocean; every demon consumed by fire. She interprets the dream — their race is doomed; Sita should be treated with respect. The she-demons, frightened, comply. Sita thanks Trijata.

**Cantos XXIV–XXVII (Hanuman approaches Sita; gives the ring):** Hanuman deliberates in the Simsapa tree. He must speak without alarming Sita — she will assume any approaching stranger is Ravana in disguise. He decides to use Common Sanskrit, not the High Register; the kind a messenger from a distant kingdom would use. He begins speaking from the tree, recounting Rama's story in order from the beginning. Sita hears and looks up, investigates, sees a small monkey. She is suspicious — Ravana can take any form. Hanuman descends and shows her Rama's ring. She weeps with joy, then immediately suspects illusion. He urges her to accept; she holds the ring against her heart.

**Canto XXVIII (The crow test):** Sita recounts the story of the crow at Chitrakuta — the crow that pecked her feet, the magic arrow, the crow's offered eye — as a secret test. Only someone genuinely from Rama would know this. Hanuman confirms every detail. She is satisfied.

**Canto XXIX (Sita refuses to be carried; the gem):** Hanuman grows to mountain size and offers to carry Sita — one night's travel and she would be with Rama. She refuses: she will not touch any male being but Rama; it is her vow; she will not break it even for rescue. She also fears the sea crossing at speed. She does not doubt Hanuman's strength. She gives him instead a gem — taken from the jeweled plait in her hair, one of Indra's gifts originally given through Anasuyá — as proof for Rama that Hanuman found her.

**Cantos XXX–XXXII (Destruction of the Ashoka grove):** Hanuman decides to cause damage before leaving — to gather intelligence about Lanka's defenses and to demonstrate that the city can be entered by an enemy. He destroys the Ashoka grove entirely: tears trees, breaks fountains, kills the peacocks, shatters every pavilion with his hands and tail. The grove is demolished. She-demons report to Ravana. Ravana sends Jambumali, son of his general Prahasta. Hanuman kills him with a Sal tree used as a club. Ravana sends seven sons of his generals. Part 8 ends mid-battle at Canto XLV as the seven generals attack together.

Ravana's general Prahasta questions Hanuman. Hanuman gives his full account: he came from Rama, he found Sita, he asks Ravana to return her or face destruction. He spares Vibhishana's house deliberately. Drums and shells sound disaster. Lanka's glory is partially destroyed in one night.

**Canto LV (Hanuman's remorse; heavenly confirmation):** Cooling on a rooftop, Hanuman is struck by horror: he set fire to the entire city and Sita was somewhere in it. He considers drowning himself, entering the flames, going south to the ocean. Then voices from the sky — the gods and sages who have been watching — cry out: "Sita lives. No flame harmed a hair of her head." He is consoled and returns to say farewell to Sita.

**Cantos LVI–LVII (Farewell; Hanuman's return; the honey grove):** Hanuman tells Sita he is returning to Rama. She gives him a message — the Chitrakuta crow story again, as confirmation — and blessings. He departs, leaping back across the ocean. He finds the south search party at the honey grove of Sugriva's uncle Dadhimukha; the Vanars, drunk with excitement, have rifled the grove; Dadhimukha goes to Sugriva in complaint; Sugriva infers from the revelry that the search succeeded and bids the party return quickly.

**Canto LXV (The gem; Hanuman's report; Rama's lament on the gem):** Hanuman gives Sita's gem to Rama. Rama presses it to his chest and weeps — it is Janaka's wedding gift, given through Indra, worn on her brow. He gives Hanuman's full report: Sita lives, weeps, gives Rama one month before she can no longer sustain herself. Rama's grief speech: he wants Hanuman to describe every word she said, because her words alone can give him the will to live.

**Book VI, Cantos I–IV (Book VI begins; Sugriva's speech; Hanuman's description of Lanka; the march):** Rama's immediate question: how to cross the sea. Sugriva argues that grief is the only obstacle — the Vánars can destroy any army, and the enemy's location is known; they must act. Hanuman describes Lanka's defenses in detail: golden walls, four fortified gates, each gate held by thousands, moats full of monsters, internal walls of coral and ivory, and the Pushpaka car. The army moves south, filling the plain "like a second sea." They reach the ocean.

Ravana gives his own assessment: he is secure from gods, demons, and serpents by Brahma's boon; men and Vanars were not specified; he is worried. Kumbhakarna says Ravana was a fool to steal the wife first and ask counsel after — but now that it's done, he'll fight.

**Cantos IX–X, XIV–XVI (Vibhishana's three counsels; the debate):** Vibhishana gives Ravana three formal speeches over two separate council sessions, each urging Sita's return. He lists the omens — cattle without milk, sacred fires won't kindle, snakes in the altar, trembling elephants. He argues that Rama cannot be defeated, Kumbhakarna cannot stop him, Indrajit cannot stop him. Prahasta and Indrajit ridicule Vibhishana. Ravana calls him a traitor to his race. **Cantos XVII–XIX (Vibhishana's flight; the Vanar debate; Rama accepts the suppliant):** Vibhishana flies from Lanka with four attendants and lands in the air over the Vanar army on the shore. The Vanar chieftains debate: Angad says don't trust him; Jambavan says suspect any ally of Ravana; Hanuman alone argues for trust. Rama quotes the story of the dove who fed the fowler who killed his mate, offering its own flesh as hospitality — the ancient law of shelter for suppliants. "Even if this were Ravana himself, I could not turn him away." He welcomes Vibhishana and, at Vibhishana's suggestion, has Lakshmana pour ocean water over his head — Vibhishana is crowned king of Lanka in anticipation.

**Cantos XX–XXII (Ocean threatened; Nala builds the bridge):** Rama lies on the shore for three days fasting, asking Ocean to grant passage. Ocean doesn't come. Rama assigns positions on Lanka's perimeter: Nila at the east gate (Prahasta's post), Angad at the south gate (Mahodar's), Hanuman at the west gate (Indrajit's), Rama and Lakshmana at the north (Ravana's post). ### Part 10 Detailed Content

**Cantos LXI–LXVII (Kumbhakarna's sally and death):** The Rakshasas finally rouse Kumbhakarna from his six-month sleep with enormous amounts of food and drink. He is a giant who darkens the sky — his boast is that he will eat the Vanar army and drink the sea. He sallies out; the monkey army flees in terror. Sugriva alone stands, fights Kumbhakarna directly — is captured and squeezed under the giant's arm; Sugriva bites off his nose and ear and escapes. Lakshmana wounds Kumbhakarna with many shafts. Then Rama kills him with a systematic sequence of specifically named divine arrows: the Wind-God's dart (severing the right arm with its mace), Indra's dart (severing the left arm), two crescent-headed arrows (severing both legs), and finally the Brahma-given arrow (decapitation). The severed head, enormous as a mountain, crashes through a city gate as it falls. Ravana laments his brother.

**Cantos LXVIII–LXXII (Ravana's sons fall):** Ravana sends his sons: Narantaka sallies on horseback and is killed by Angad (who tears off the horse's head and strikes him with it). Trisiras and Devantak and Mahaparsva sally — Hanuman and Nila kill them. Atikaya, enormous and near-invincible, is killed by Lakshmana with a Brahma-given wind weapon after his body proves impervious to ordinary arrows. Ravana's lamentations deepen; he now has no sons.

**Indrajit's second binding (editorial summary):** Griffith's editorial note acknowledges that at this point in the text he has omitted Canto LXXIII (Indrajit's false Sita effigy) in full because of its length and the difficulty of translating it faithfully. The full canto in Valmiki describes Indrajit bringing a magical illusory Sita in his chariot to the battlefield and cutting her down before Hanuman's eyes. Hanuman, who had met the real Sita days before, is devastated — he and the entire Vanar army believe she is dead. Rama and Lakshmana collapse in grief. It is illusion: the real Sita is still alive and weeping in the Ashoka grove. Griffith summarizes the episode in a bracketed note and proceeds.

**Cantos LXXIV–LXXVIII (Indrajit's fire sacrifice and death — partly editorial):** Indrajit retreats to perform a fire sacrifice that will give him invincibility for the coming day's battle. Lakshmana, on Vibhishana's guidance, interrupts the rite before it is completed — Griffith notes the full account is again summarized, compressed from many cantos. In Griffith's version, Lakshmana and Indrajit fight at length; Lakshmana finally uses a Brahma-given arrow and kills Indrajit. Ravana's lament for Indrajit is the most anguished of all his grief-speeches — Indrajit was his greatest general, and Ravana provided for Indrajit's education in sorcery and warfare from childhood.

**Kumbha, Nikumbha, Makarakshe (editorial summaries):** Griffith notes in brackets the omission of cantos on Kumbha and Nikumbha (Kumbhakarna's sons) — Sugriva kills Kumbha; Hanuman kills Nikumbha. Makarakshe is killed by Rama (editorial summary of a canto omitted). Night attack on Lanka follows.

**Cantos LXXIX–LXXXV (Ravana's final sally; Lakshmana falls; Rama and the Sun Hymn):** Ravana himself sallies in his chariot for the last great battle. He wounds Lakshmana with Maya's magical lance — Lakshmana falls senseless. Hanuman is ordered to the Himalayas for life-restoring herbs; the mountain peak defeats Hanuman's ability to identify the specific plants, so he lifts the entire mountain and carries it back. The physician Sushena revives Lakshmana with the herbs. Indra sends his divine chariot with Matali as charioteer to Rama's side. The sage Agastya appears (descends from heaven) and recites the Aditya Hridayam — the Sun Hymn — to Rama before the final battle begins, instructing him that reciting it three times will grant him victory.

**Cantos LXXXVI–LXXXIX (The death of Ravana):** Rama and Ravana fight at enormous length — an equal battle at first; every time Rama cuts off one of Ravana's heads, it regenerates. Matali whispers that Rama must use the Brahma-given arrow. Rama fires it — feathered with wind, its point made of fire and sun, its weight drawn from Meru and Mandar, given to Agastya by Brahma, then given to Rama. It passes through Ravana's chest and enters the earth. Ravana dies. The gods praise Rama from the sky.

**Cantos XC–XCII (Funeral rites; Vibhishana crowned):** Vibhishana at first refuses to perform Ravana's funeral rites, citing his brother's sins. Rama rebukes him: enmity ends with death; Ravana was a great king and warrior; the rites must be done. Mandodari's lament is extended and profound — she alone among the mourners seems to understand that Rama was Vishnu incarnate; she says she guesses that is why Ravana was doomed from the start, not because he was weak but because a god had decided to be born as a man. Vibhishana is then crowned king of Lanka by Rama.

**Cantos XCIII–XCVII (Sita's test and vindication):** Sita is brought before Rama. Rama delivers a long speech publicly rejecting her — he says he fought for his name and the fame of his house, not for her; that a woman who has dwelt in another man's house cannot be taken back; that she is free to go where she will. Sita's reply is dignified and controlled; she notes she was taken by force, not by will; she compares herself to gold that can be tested by fire. She asks Lakshmana to build a pyre. She enters the fire, praying to Fire God (Agni) directly. Fire God carries her out unharmed and certifies her purity before the assembled gods and sages. Brahma and the other Gods descend and confirm that Rama is Vishnu incarnate, that he had to deny Sita publicly so the world's eyes would see the proof. Dasharatha descends from heaven to bless his sons. Indra grants a great boon: all the Vanars who died in the war are restored to life.

**Cantos XCVIII–CII (The return; Rama's consecration):** The party travels home on the Pushpaka sky-car — Sita narrates the geography below (pointing out Lanka, the bridge, the sea, Adam's Bridge, Kishkindha) and Rama describes each place they pass. Hanuman races ahead to announce the arrival to Bharat; Bharat faints with joy. Grand procession; the entire kingdom comes out. Rama is consecrated king of Ayodhya. The closing hymn describes ten thousand years of Rama's reign — no child dies before a parent, no disease, no sin, great crops and rain.

**Appendix — Ravana Doomed (Section XIII):** Griffith includes in four languages (Carey+Marshman English prose, Schlegel Latin, Gorresio Italian, Fauche French) the council of the gods where Brahma goes before Vishnu to beg him to incarnate as a mortal to kill Ravana. Ravana had earned a boon that made him immune from being killed by any god, demon, serpent, or divine creature — he had omitted to ask protection from men, thinking men too weak to threaten him. The four translations show the same scene through four different scholarly lenses.

**Uttarakanda summary (Additional Notes):** Griffith appends a summary of the seventh book, which is considered by some scholars to be a later addition. It contains: Ravana's genealogy (grandson of Brahma through Pulastya; his race's claim to Lanka preceded Kubera's); the original Rakshasa kings and how Lanka was given to them; Kumbhakarna's curse explained (he asked for eternal sleep as a boon, accidentally, because Saraswati confused his tongue at the crucial moment). Mandodari's origin. Ravana conquering Indra. The Vedavati story — a woman of great piety whom Ravana abducted; she cursed him and burned herself; she was reborn as Sita, the cause of his death. Sita's second exile — Rama, knowing she is pure, sends her away from Ayodhya because the people of the city talk and doubt. She gives birth to Kusa and Lava in Valmiki's hermitage. At the Ashvamedha (horse sacrifice), Kusa and Lava recite the full Ramayana before the assembled court including their unknowing father. Sita, invited to return to Ayodhya and prove her innocence before the assembly, instead calls on the Earth goddess to receive her — and the earth opens and swallows her. Rama enters the Sarayu river and ascends to heaven. Additional dictionary entries and a lengthy essay by Gorresio on the theological differences between Hindu avatar doctrine and Christian Incarnation close the volume.

## Pass 3 — Quest Seeds

### Source Hook

In the Ramayana's second canto, the sage Valmiki walks to the river Tamasa to bathe. He sees two curlews (krauncha birds) mating by the water. A hunter shoots the male. The female cries out. The sage is seized by sudden grief and, involuntarily, four lines of verse form in his mouth — the first sloka ever spoken. He is amazed: the word sloka (verse) and the word shoka (grief) share a root. Brahma confirms this was intentional: the first poem in the world was born from witnessed suffering.

A medieval Persian scholar, reading a summary of this story, writes a commentary arguing that the same impulse underlies the origin myths of Persian ghazal (which the poet Rudaki describes as emerging from loss), Arabic elegy (marthiya), and the Psalms of David. The commentary is a philosophical treatise, not a religious claim. He wants to deliver it to a poet in Tabriz who is compiling exactly this kind of cross-tradition study.

---

### Logistics

**Year:** 1367
**Territory:** Jalayirid Sultanate (northwest Persia / Azeri highlands)
**Commission city:** Sultaniya (a former Ilkhanid capital, still a center of Persian intellectual life in 1367; about 200 km southwest of Tabriz)
**Delivery city:** Tabriz (the Jalayirid capital; home of Persian poets, astronomers, and manuscript scholars)
**Road distance:** ~200 km, 5–6 days by horse; passes through Qazvin-area hill country, then into the Azeri plain
**Dangers:** Jalayirid bandit activity on the Qazvin road; Chagatai raider incursion from the northeast; competing scholarly factions (another scholar wants the text discredited before it reaches Tabriz)

---

### Cast

**Commissioning party — Scholar Musa of Sultaniya:** A Persian-Armenian scholar, aged 60, who has spent 30 years studying Indian manuscripts that reached Persia through trade routes. He does not travel; he sends. He is known and respected in Sultaniya but has a rival in Tabriz (see below). He uses a reed-pen seal. He is precise, slightly anxious, very proud of the work.

**Delivery recipient — Poet Kamal of Tabriz:** A Persian master poet, 45 years old, compiling a multi-tradition treatise on grief as the source of lyric poetry. He has been waiting for exactly this kind of cross-cultural argument. He is well-connected at the Jalayirid court. He will authenticate the delivery by returning the TOKEN.

**Obstacle 1 — Qazvin tollman Farhad:** Controls a mountain road junction. He has been bribed by Musa's rival to delay all messengers from Sultaniya by 3 days (claiming customs inspection). The delay would cause the manuscript to arrive after a court session where Musa's rival can establish prior authorship of the same argument.

**Obstacle 2 — Scholar Rumi of Tabriz (the rival):** Not named in game text, referred to only as "the other man." He has his own partial version of the cross-tradition argument and will reach Kamal before the manuscript does if Farhad succeeds in causing the delay. He has agents watching the road.

**Obstacle 3 (Act 4) — Chagatai raider outrider:** A lone scout from a raiding party that hit a village east of Tabriz two days ago. He is wounded, disoriented, dangerous. He interprets the carrier's approach as a threat.

**Obstacle 4 (Act 5) — Kamal's gate-keeper at the poet's house in Tabriz:** Kamal is at a court recitation and not home. The gate-keeper has orders to receive no documents without the poet's personal presence. The delay is genuine — the gate-keeper is not corrupt — but the rival's agent is now also at the gate, watching. The carrier must wait, act, or find Kamal at the court.

**Pass:** The carrier leaves with the sealed case, the delivery phrase, and a clear road description. Musa gives a small advance on the fee.
**Fail:** The carrier leaves without the delivery phrase. When they reach Tabriz, Kamal's gate-keeper will not accept the package from an unknown messenger with no authentication. The carrier must improvise in Act 5.

**TOKEN granted:** The cedarwood case is placed in the carrier's hands.

---

#### Act 2 — The Qazvin Junction (hill road, day 2)

**Scene:** A narrow road junction in the foothills. Farhad the tollman has a painted wooden post and a canvas shelter. He is professionally friendly, regretful, produces documents. A three-day customs inspection is required for "manuscripts of foreign origin." He cannot be bribed directly — he has already been paid and is afraid of who paid him.

- Deception: The manuscript is not a "manuscript of foreign origin" — it is a Persian text written by a Persian scholar who was born in Sultaniya. It has no foreign words in it. (True. The commentary is in Persian.) If the carrier argues this persuasively, Farhad has no grounds.
- Persuasion: Ask to see the customs regulation that defines "foreign origin manuscript." Farhad does not have one — he has a bribe and a story. Patient insistence breaks it.

**Fail:** Three-day delay begins. The carrier must find another route (goat trail — harder travel but only adds 6 hours) or accept the delay and know the rival will arrive first.

---

#### Act 3 — The Rival's Outrider (plain below Tabriz, day 3)

He is the rival scholar's messenger — sent to carry a rumor, not a document: a rumor that Musa of Sultaniya plagiarized the cross-tradition argument from an older manuscript (false, but damaging if it reaches Kamal's ears first).

**Fail:** The outrider reaches Kamal's house first and delivers the rumor. In Act 5, Kamal will have heard it and will be colder at reception. The authentication phrase (from Act 1) now matters more — it proves Musa's text is original.

**Scene:** A wounded man in partial riding gear, sitting against a wall at the edge of a village east of Tabriz that was hit by a raiding party two days ago. **Skill check (DC 12 — Medicine or Animal Handling or Persuasion):**
- Medicine: The wound is infected; he will die in 2 days without treatment. If the carrier acknowledges this calmly and offers to help (bandaging, water), the situation de-escalates.
- Animal Handling: His horse is tethered 20 meters away; the horse is calm. Approaching the horse first, slowly, signals non-aggression.
- Persuasion: The carrier does not speak Chagatai, but the man speaks some Persian. Simple words, open hands.

**Pass:** The scout stands down. He asks the carrier which road avoids the garrison (he wants to get out of Jalayirid territory). The carrier points east. The man limps to his horse and goes.
**Fail:** Combat or flight. The TOKEN is not damaged — the case is sealed and sturdy — but the carrier arrives in Tabriz shaken and late. Act 5 begins after dark rather than at the afternoon court session.

---

#### Act 5 — Delivery (Tabriz, Kamal's house or the court)

**Scene:** Kamal is not home. His gate-keeper is polite, firm, and has standing orders: no documents accepted without the poet's personal presence. The rival's outrider (whether or not the carrier succeeded in Act 3) is sitting across the street.

**Approach A — Find Kamal at the court recitation:** Kamal is performing at a Jalayirid court session in the palace district. The carrier must get through the outer court gate (a guard who wants a name on the access list, which the carrier is not on) and into the recitation hall.

**Skill check (DC 12 — Persuasion or Deception):**
- Persuasion: State clearly that the carrier is a private messenger from Scholar Musa of Sultaniya with a time-critical scholarly document for the poet Kamal, and that it concerns the treatise on poetic origins that Kamal has been expecting. This is all true. The guard has seen couriers before.
- Deception: Present as a servant of a court official (Musa has one connection at court, mentioned in passing in Act 1).

**Approach B — Wait at the house:** If the carrier waits, Kamal returns by evening. But the rival's outrider has now also been waiting and will attempt to speak to Kamal first.

**Pass (either approach):** Kamal receives the case, breaks the seal, reads the opening paragraph, hears the authentication phrase if the carrier uses it. He nods. He dismisses the outrider with a single word. He asks the carrier to wait while he writes a receipt for Musa.

**Fail:** Kamal hears the plagiarism rumor from the outrider and is cautious. The carrier must produce the authentication phrase (if obtained in Act 1) or describe the specific argument of the manuscript (requiring the carrier to have learned it in Act 1). Without these, Kamal accepts the manuscript but files it provisionally, pending his own investigation. Musa gets paid, but with a delay.

**TOKEN taken:** Kamal breaks the seal in the carrier's presence. He does not return the case — he keeps it as the authenticated container. The carrier is paid.

## §ADA-02 — Vignette Cycle 2: "Sita Into the Earth"

### Cycle vs. Cycle — What This Angle Reveals

Cycle 1 ("The Curlew's Grief") took the angle of love becoming form: Valmiki witnessed suffering and verse burst from his mouth — the first sloka, the mother-word of all Indian poetry; grief and verse share a root; the shape of grief becomes the shape of art.

Cycle 2 takes the inverse: love that refuses to take any more forms. Sita proved herself before the Fire God and was certified pure. Rama exiled her again because the people talked. She bore his sons in the forest. When asked to prove herself before the assembly a second time, she refused — not with a speech, not with fire, not with any further performance. She called on the Earth goddess and descended. Cycle 1 was the word that burst from grief. Cycle 2 is the return to silence that refuses to speak again to the same court.

The letter argues the distinction between justice (the verdict already given by a court with authority) and vindication (performing purity again for an audience that will not be satisfied by any court but their own).
