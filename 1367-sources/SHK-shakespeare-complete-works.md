## Pass 1 — Full Telling

Shakespeare's Complete Works arrived in the game's source library as a 5,318 KB text spanning 28 split parts — the largest single source in the index. It contains everything: 154 sonnets, 37 plays, 5 longer poems. The historical moment of its writing spans roughly 1590 to 1613, across the reign of Elizabeth I and the first decade of James I. Shakespeare was working through a London that had just survived plague, the Spanish Armada's aftermath, the Essex rebellion, and the question of succession. His theatre — the Globe, the Blackfriars — was the most popular public entertainment in England, and it was constantly threatened: by the city authorities who hated crowds, by the Puritans who hated plays, by censors who read political allegory into everything. He wrote under pressure, for money, for audiences who ranged from groundlings paying a penny to nobles sitting on stage. That pressure produced work that is simultaneously popular entertainment and the sharpest philosophical writing in English.

The Sonnets, which open Part 1, are the emotional autobiography of the collection — though Shakespeare never published them himself; they appeared in 1609, probably without his consent. They address two figures: the Fair Youth (Sonnets 1–126), a beautiful young man whom the speaker loves with an intensity that ranges from adoration to jealousy to shame; and the Dark Lady (Sonnets 127–152), a woman of dark complexion whom he desires with a mixture of lust, self-loathing, and bitter wit. A third figure enters in the middle — the Rival Poet (Sonnets 78–86), a writer of greater reputation who threatens to displace the speaker in the Fair Youth's affections. The whole sequence is a love triangle written from the least powerful corner of the triangle: the speaker who loves too much, writes too plainly, and knows it.

The central argument of the Sonnets: time destroys everything — beauty, youth, reputation, love — and only verse can preserve what time eats. But Shakespeare is also honest enough to know that verse is a weak remedy. "Not marble, nor the gilded monuments / Of princes shall outlive this powerful rhyme" (Sonnet 55) — and yet he closes the Dark Lady sequence with "For I have sworn thee fair, and thought thee bright / Who art as black as hell, as dark as night" (Sonnet 147). The confidence of the preservation claim collapses into the self-knowledge that he has been lying all along.

The compelling stories inside the Sonnets:
- **The Rival Poet** (Sonnets 78–86): Another, more famous writer has begun addressing poems to the Fair Youth. The speaker watches helplessly. Sonnet 86 is the best one: "Was it the proud full sail of his great verse / Bound for the prize of (all too precious) you / That did my ripe thoughts in my brain inhearse" — my thoughts are coffined before they can be said. The Rival wins. The speaker falls silent. Then, later, finds his way back.
- **Sonnet 116**: "Let me not to the marriage of true minds / Admit impediments" — the most famous statement of constancy in English. It is also, in context, a desperate declaration: the speaker is trying to convince himself, not the Youth.
- **Sonnet 129**: "The expense of spirit in a waste of shame / Is lust in action" — the most furious sonnet in the sequence. Lust dissected with the precision of a surgeon, the venom of someone who knows he is describing himself.
- **Sonnet 18**: "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" — the most famous opening line in the sequence. Simple, confident, certain of its own power. It is Sonnet 1 of the emotional argument, even if it is numbered 18.

The plays — across the 28 parts — divide into four groups:

**Comedies** (As You Like It, Much Ado About Nothing, A Midsummer Night's Dream, Twelfth Night, The Merchant of Venice, The Merry Wives of Windsor, Love's Labour's Lost, The Comedy of Errors, The Taming of the Shrew, The Two Gentlemen of Verona): These end in marriages and resolutions. They are not light — Shylock's bond, Malvolio's humiliation, Caliban's dispossession — but they are architecturally optimistic. Disguise is the central mechanism: characters hide who they are, and what they hide is always the truth they need.

**Tragedies** (Hamlet, Macbeth, King Lear, Othello, Romeo and Juliet, Julius Caesar, Antony and Cleopatra, Coriolanus, Timon of Athens, Titus Andronicus, Troilus and Cressida): These end in death. The tragic mechanism is always a character of genuine greatness who has one interior flaw — pride, credulity, ambition, jealousy — that the world exploits. The world doesn't cause the tragedy; it provides the occasion.

**Histories** (Richard II, Henry IV Parts 1 and 2, Henry V, Henry VI Parts 1–3, Richard III, King John, Henry VIII): The history plays are about the price of political power. Richard II is the finest: a king with a poet's soul and a politician's incompetence loses his crown through passivity; the man who takes it, Bolingbroke, is an efficient administrator with no inner life. The question the histories pose: which is worse — the beautiful failure or the hollow success?

**Romances / Late Plays** (The Tempest, The Winter's Tale, Cymbeline, Pericles, The Two Noble Kinsmen): The late plays are the strangest. They return to the themes of the tragedies — loss, jealousy, exile — but route them through fantasy and end in redemption rather than death. Prospero forgives. Leontes' dead wife is restored. There is a quality of dream-logic in these plays, as if Shakespeare was working out what happens after the tragedies end.

**Part 1 specific — All's Well That Ends Well** opens after the Sonnets. A young woman, Helena, loves Bertram, a count who is above her station. Her father was a great physician; she has inherited his skill. The King of France is dying of a fistula; Helena cures him; the King offers her any husband she wants. She chooses Bertram. Bertram refuses her, leaves for war, and sends her a letter: "When thou canst get the ring upon my finger, which never shall come off, and show me a child begotten of thy body that I am father to, then call me husband: but in such a 'then' I write a 'never.'" Helena gets the ring and the child through a bed-trick — substituting herself for the woman Bertram is pursuing. The play is deeply uncomfortable. Bertram is selfish and entitled; Helena is obsessively determined; the happy ending is purchased through deception. It is a comedy that makes you wonder if comedy is the right category.

**Chapter Notes (by major section/play):**

*The Sonnets* (Part 1): 154 poems addressed to the Fair Youth and Dark Lady. The opening 17 urge procreation ("breed another thee"). The middle section (18–126) develops the love, the rivalry, and the fear of time. The Dark Lady sequence (127–152) introduces erotic obsession, mutual deceit, and self-reproach. The coda (153–154) is a cold-water epigram about desire that never quenches.

*All's Well That Ends Well* (Parts 1–2): Opens at the Countess's court in Rossillon. Helena loves Bertram, who is leaving for Paris. The central mechanism — a bed-trick — is uncomfortable in the modern reading; in the period, it was a stock comic resolution. Helena is the play's moral center: she is the one who actually does anything.

## Pass 2 — Slower

**Key named figures (Sonnets):**

- **The Speaker** (the "I" of the Sonnets): A professional poet. Older than the Youth. Self-aware about his limitations — his verse is plain, his age is showing, his rival writes with more flash. What distinguishes him is persistence and honesty. He says what he knows, including the unflattering things.
- **The Fair Youth**: Never named. Beautiful, young, beloved, unreliable. He takes the Rival Poet's attentions. He may have an affair with the Dark Lady. He is never reproached past a certain point — the speaker always finds a way to forgive. He is the center the poem orbits around; we barely know him as a person.
- **The Rival Poet**: Probably modeled on Christopher Marlowe or George Chapman. He has "the proud full sail of his great verse." He wins — temporarily. The speaker's response is silence (Sonnet 86), then return.
- **The Dark Lady**: Dark-eyed, dark-haired, musical, unfaithful, witty, self-possessed. She and the speaker are equally dishonest with each other. She may be the woman who corrupts the Fair Youth. The speaker knows he is lying when he calls her fair (Sonnet 152), and says so.

**Pivotal choices (Sonnets):**
- The speaker's choice to keep writing after the Rival appears (Sonnets 78–86 → 87 onward)
- The speaker's choice to return to the Youth after the Dark Lady episode — forgiveness without justification
- Sonnet 116's choice of constancy as the definition of love: "Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds"

**Tone of the world:** The Sonnets' world is London's literary culture — taverns, patrons, private performances. Everything is mediated by status: the Speaker is below the Youth, who has money and rank. The Rival is above the Speaker in reputation. The Dark Lady is outside the hierarchy entirely — she is defined by desire, not class. The world is polite, competitive, and quietly brutal.

## Pass 3 — One Scene Elaborated: Sonnet 116

*The moment: The speaker writes Sonnet 116. He is addressing the Fair Youth, possibly after the Rival Poet episode has passed, possibly during it. The date doesn't matter. What matters is that he is making a claim he knows he needs to make — not because he is certain of it, but because he needs to say it in order to believe it.*

The room where he writes this is not described — Shakespeare never describes his speaker's physical space. But from the period: a hired room in Southwark, near the theatre. A candle. A desk with a writing board. The pen is a goose quill. Ink is iron gall — it will last four hundred years. He is writing a letter, or a poem for private circulation, or both.

"Let me not to the marriage of true minds / Admit impediments." He is borrowing from the Book of Common Prayer — the priest's question at the wedding, "if any of you know cause or just impediment." He is staging a marriage between minds, not bodies. He is asserting that the love between him and the Youth is as binding as a sacrament, and as publicly recognized.

Then: "love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds." This is where the argument turns from assertion to definition. He is saying: the love I have for you doesn't change because you change. Which means: the Youth has changed. Has perhaps gone to the Rival. Has perhaps gone to the Dark Lady. And the speaker is still here. Still writing. Still naming this constancy as love rather than as something more complicated.

"O no, it is an ever-fixed mark / That looks on tempests and is never shaken." A mark is a navigational beacon — a fixed point sailors use to find their position. His love is what the Youth uses to locate himself when he is lost. The speaker is not the lover here; he is the landmark.

The closing couplet: "If this be error and upon me proved, / I never writ, nor no man ever loved." This is a legal oath — prove me wrong and I deny the whole record. But of course it cannot be proved. The argument is unfalsifiable. The speaker knows this. He is using the logic of the oath to protect himself from having to admit that he might be wrong, that the Youth might not come back, that constancy might be its own kind of delusion. The quill lifts from the paper. The ink is already drying. He folds it and addresses it. He needs someone to carry it.

6. **The King's Mirror** — Richard II, Act IV. Richard II is made to surrender his crown in words. He asks for a mirror. The mirror arrives. He smashes it. The Fighter carries the shards.

## 5-Act Vignette Play — Cycle 1

**Title:** *The Rival's Door*
**TOKEN:** *Sonnet 116 — "The Marriage of True Minds"* — a sealed letter containing the poem, written in iron gall ink on paper folded twice and closed with a plain wax seal. It carries no name on the outside.

**Theme check:** All seven ideas reviewed. Idea 1 (the sealed sonnet) introduces *the poem that cannot reach its reader while a rival controls access* — not present in any existing thread. The Rival Poet's interception of the speaker's access to the Fair Youth is a social mechanism distinct from all active threads. Proceeding.

**Existing themes avoided:** Love and fate intertwined (this is about delivery, not love itself). Grief carried in an object (the object here is not grief — it is a claim). Truth delivered posthumously (the speaker is alive).

**ACT I**
*The Sonnet · sealed, ink still faintly warm · in the Poet's hand · the Birka dockside, late evening, first snow on the piers*

The Poet is a middle-aged man in a hired room above the Birka wool merchants' hall. He has been in the city three weeks, angling for access to the young northern factor — a Fair Youth who controls four trading accounts and is being courted by every writer and wit in the port. The Rival has gotten there first. He has a letter of introduction from a lord; he has better clothes; he performs at suppers. The Poet has only what he has written. He holds the sealed sonnet out to you across the table. His hands are ink-stained. He does not explain what is in it. He says only: *get this to him tonight, before the Rival reads at the evening supper. After the supper, it will mean nothing. Before the supper, it might mean everything.*

*The Fighter's internal reaction: The letter weighs nothing. That is what makes it difficult.*

**SCENE**
You stand at the pier end of the Birka wool merchants' hall as the first snow comes in off the water. The hall is loud with traders and their hangers-on, the smell of wool and tallow and salt. Somewhere inside, a man with good clothes and a fine voice is preparing to perform. The Poet's sealed letter is inside your coat. You need to cross the hall, find the young factor's table, and place the letter in his hands before the Rival begins — but the Rival's associate is at the door, checking every face that enters.

**PROMPT:** Persuasion DC 13. The associate at the door knows most of the port's faces. Convince him you are a wool assessor with a delivery for the young factor — a routine bill of lading, nothing worth holding up.

**FAIL TEXT:** He looks at you too long and says the factor is not receiving deliveries tonight. You wait in the cold. The Rival's voice starts inside — rich, confident, the opening of something long. When the door opens again for a servant, you slip in at the margin. You have missed the introduction. The crowd's attention is fully on the Rival. Try again from inside.

**SUCCESS TEXT:** He waves you through. The hall opens around you — warm, loud, the smell of beeswax and roasting meat. The young factor is at the far end at a center table, watching the Rival with the pleasant attention of someone who is enjoying himself. You have perhaps four minutes before the Rival speaks directly to him. You receive the *Wool Factor's Token* — a stamped lead tag the associate gave you as a pass, which now gets you to the factor's table without challenge.

**ACT II**
*The Sonnet · sealed, inside the Fighter's coat · the Fighter's hands · the center of the Birka hall, mid-supper, the Rival at full performance*

The Rival is extraordinary. You can see why the Poet is afraid. He has the room — every face turned toward him, the factor's included. His poem is addressed to the factor directly, naming him by a quality ("the tenth Muse"), offering him immortality through verse. It is flattering, skilled, and slightly too long. The factor is smiling. The window is the moment between the Rival's applause and his second poem. You are three seats away from the factor's table. The Rival's second associate has appeared at your shoulder.

*The Fighter's internal reaction: The Rival's poem is better than nothing. That is all I can grant it.*

**SCENE**
The hall rings with applause for the Rival's first poem. The young factor turns to say something to his neighbor, and for a moment his face is unguarded — pleased, but slightly too pleased, the look of someone being performed at rather than spoken to. The Rival's associate steps close behind you and asks, quietly, what you are doing at the factor's end of the hall with a lead pass that belongs to door duty. The sealed letter is in your coat. You have thirty seconds before the Rival calls for quiet for his second poem.

**PROMPT:** Deception DC 14. The associate is sharp. Tell him you are carrying a private accounting document that the factor's steward asked to be delivered during supper — the kind of thing that cannot wait until morning because the merchant ships leave before dawn.

**FAIL TEXT:** He does not believe you but he is not certain enough to cause a scene. He steps between you and the factor's table and says the factor is receiving no documents until the performance is done. The second poem begins. You wait at the edge of the room, watching the factor's face grow more certain that the Rival is the better choice. The poem ends. The factor rises. Try again in the corridor.

**SUCCESS TEXT:** He considers you for one beat too long, then steps aside — not because he believes you, but because making a scene would embarrass the Rival's supper. You move to the factor's table. He looks up. You place the sealed letter beside his wine cup and step back. He glances at it, glances at you, and does not open it — not yet. But he picks it up and puts it in his own coat. The Rival's second poem begins. You receive the *Harbor Manifest* — a real accounting document the associate was himself carrying, which he dropped in the confusion of stepping aside.

**ACT III**
*The Sonnet · sealed and unread, in the factor's coat · the factor's hands · the Birka corridor between the hall and the counting rooms*

The supper ends. The Rival is surrounded by admirers. The factor has slipped out early — you follow. He is in the corridor that connects the hall to the counting rooms, reading something by the window-light. Not the sealed letter. A note from the Rival's associate, delivered at some point you missed. He has not opened the sealed letter. He is deciding.

*The Fighter's internal reaction: He is holding both choices at once. He does not know this is what he is doing.*

**SCENE**
The corridor is cold, the window open a crack to let out the candle smoke. The young factor stands with a note in one hand and the sealed letter in the other. He looks at you — you are the one who placed the letter beside his wine cup — and says, simply: *who sent this?* He means the sealed letter. He has not opened it. He is giving you one chance to answer. If you name the Poet, the factor may set the letter down unopened, because the Rival's associate has already primed him to see the Poet as the lesser choice.

**PROMPT:** Persuasion DC 15. Do not name the Poet. Tell the factor that the letter was sent by someone who knows the difference between a poem that flatters and a poem that sees — and that the only way to know which this is, is to read it.

**FAIL TEXT:** He is not convinced by abstraction. He sets the sealed letter on the window ledge and says he will read it in the morning. You watch him walk back toward the hall. The letter is on the ledge. Unopened. You take it back — it cannot be left unattended. Try once more when he reaches his lodgings.

**SUCCESS TEXT:** He pauses. Then breaks the seal himself, right there in the corridor. He reads it standing. He reads it twice. His face does not change — but he folds it carefully and puts it in the inside pocket, closer to himself. He says nothing to you. He walks back toward the hall. You receive the *Broken Seal* — the wax, still warm, that fell when he opened the letter. This is the proof it was read.

**grantItem:** Broken Seal
**checkPassFlag:** letterRead

**ACT IV**
*The Sonnet · read, folded, in the factor's inside pocket · the Dark Lady's hands — briefly · the Birka counting room, late, one candle*

The Dark Lady arrives at the counting room an hour later. She is the Rival's patron and silent partner — she finances his performances, collects the letters of recommendation he generates, and controls which writers get which doors opened. She knows the sealed letter was delivered and read. She says, directly, that the letter belongs to her — that the factor's attention belongs to a larger economy than one plain-writing poet can offer. She is entirely right about the economy. She may even be right about the poet. She reaches for the factor's inside pocket.

*The Fighter's internal reaction: She is not wrong. That is the problem.*

**SCENE**
The counting room smells of cedar and cold wax. The factor is at his ledger. The Dark Lady stands across the table — composed, unhurried, certain. She has produced a folded document from her own coat: a contract, signed, committing the factor's trading accounts to the Rival's literary patronage network for three years. She places it on the ledger and says the sealed poem is a private document that arrived without authorization, and she would like it returned to her for disposal. She is looking at you, not the factor. She knows you are the one who delivered it.

**PROMPT:** Courage DC 14. The Dark Lady is connected, wealthy, and technically correct that the poet had no standing to send an unsolicited letter to the factor. Stand your ground. Tell her the letter was not sent to her and is not her property to dispose of.

**FAIL TEXT:** She nods, as if confirming a hypothesis. She does not threaten you. She simply tells the factor that his answer is expected by morning, and leaves. The contract is still on the ledger. The factor looks at you with the expression of someone who is calculating. Try again at the door before she leaves the building.

**SUCCESS TEXT:** You step between her and the factor and say, clearly: *the letter arrived. It was read. What he chooses to do with it is his, not yours.* She considers you for one full moment — measuring something. Then she picks up her contract and leaves without the poem. The factor exhales. He takes the sealed letter from his inside pocket and places it on the ledger — this time not to dispose of it but to keep it visible. You receive the *Dark Lady's Receipt* — the corner she tore from her contract to note your face for future reference. She left it deliberately.

**grantItem:** Dark Lady's Receipt
**checkPassFlag:** darkLadyTurned

**ACT V**
*The Sonnet · read, kept, now surrendered · the factor's hands → the flame · the Birka wharf, dawn, ships preparing to leave*

The factor has made his choice. He is leaving on the morning tide — one of his four trading accounts is in Visby, and the political situation means he cannot stay in Birka. He will not take the poem with him. He stands at the wharf with the sealed letter — re-folded, re-sealed with new wax, as if the first reading did not happen. He holds it out to you. He says: *return it to whoever sent it. Tell him it arrived. Tell him I read it twice. Tell him I would rather carry it than not — but I can't carry anything that can be found.*

He does not burn it himself. He gives that to you.

*The Fighter's internal reaction: He read it twice. That is not nothing. That is the most the poem could ask.*

**SCENE**
The wharf is loud with loading crews and rope-handlers. The factor's ship is the third from the end. He has two minutes before the gangplank is pulled. He stands with the folded sonnet extended toward you, and behind him the ship is already moving its lines. You take it. He turns and walks up the gangplank without looking back. You are holding a poem that was read twice, sealed again, and is now yours to carry. The Poet is waiting at the top of the pier with the expression of someone who has prepared for either answer.

**PROMPT:** Athletics DC 12. The gangplank is being pulled. The factor has one thing he forgot — a counter-seal receipt that proves delivery, for the Poet's records. Reach the gangplank before it clears and press it into the factor's hand.

**FAIL TEXT:** The gangplank clears before you reach it. The factor is already on deck. You can see him watching from the rail as the ship moves away. The receipt stays in your pocket. You walk back to the Poet with the returned sonnet and the counter-seal undelivered. He takes the poem, reads the new wax seal, and says nothing for a long time. Then: *did he read it?* You tell him: twice. That is enough. You receive *The Returned Sonnet* — which you hand to the Poet. The quest is done.

**SUCCESS TEXT:** You press the receipt into his hand as the gangplank lifts — he takes it, looks at it, and puts it in his coat without speaking. The ship moves out. You walk back to the Poet and place the returned sonnet in his hands. He breaks the new seal and reads it himself, standing in the snow on the pier. His hands shake slightly — not from cold. You receive *The Returned Sonnet*, which you place in his hands. The quest is complete.
