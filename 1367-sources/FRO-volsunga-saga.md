# FRO — Volsunga Saga (The Story of the Volsungs and Niblungs)
## Cycle 1 — Angle: "The Shards Before the Sword"

**Source:** The Story of the Volsungs (Volsunga Saga), with Excerpts from the Poetic Edda.
Translated by William Morris and Eiríkr Magnússon (Walter Scott Press, London, 1888).
Project Gutenberg eBook #1152. 343 KB. Read directly and completely.

## Pass 1 — Full Telling

There is no more complete expression of what the Norse world thought fate meant than this saga. It is not a tragedy in the Greek sense — where a flaw brings a hero down. It is something bleaker and stranger: a story in which everyone acts honorably, or tries to, and everyone is destroyed anyway. The gold is cursed before Sigurd is born. Brynhild loves Sigurd before she meets him. The forgetfulness potion is given before Sigurd can refuse it. The sword is broken before Sigurd can wield it. Doom moves through the world like a river, and the heroes are carried on it.

The saga begins before Sigurd, in the founding generations of the Volsung line. **Sigi**, Odin's son, murders a thrall who outshot him in a deer-hunt, hides the body in a snowdrift, is found out, exiled, and made king of the Huns. His son **Rerir** prays for a child; Odin hears it and sends a goddess-crow with an apple, which Rerir's queen eats. She carries the child six years and cannot deliver him. She orders him cut from her womb. He is born already kissing her; she dies. This is **Volsung**, the founding king, born of sacrifice. He builds a great hall around a living oak-tree called Branstock and fathers ten sons and one daughter. The most important are the twins **Sigmund** and **Signy**.

King Siggeir of Gothland comes to the Volsung court and asks for Signy's hand. She does not want him — she has the foresight of her line and knows this ends in death — but her father says a king's troth must not be broken. At the wedding feast an old one-eyed man (Odin) walks in and drives a sword into the Branstock so deep only the hilt shows. He says it belongs to whoever can draw it out. Every man at the feast tries. Only Sigmund draws it free. Siggeir offers three times its weight in gold. Sigmund refuses: "If it was your lot to bear it, it would have come to you there." Siggeir goes home with his bride holding that humiliation in his chest.

He invites the Volsungs to visit. Signy warns them the night they arrive: go home, raise an army, come back stronger. Volsung refuses. "I swore I would never flee from fire or sword, and I am too old to start. All men die once." He fights Siggeir's army eight times through. He falls. His ten sons are taken and put in stocks in the forest. Each night a she-wolf comes and eats one. The she-wolf is Siggeir's own mother, transformed by witchcraft. By the tenth night only Sigmund is left. Signy sends honey; the wolf licks his face and he catches her tongue in his teeth and rips it out by the roots. The wolf dies.

**Signy** is a figure of cold, patient genius. She knows from the start what she needs: not her sons by Siggeir (who prove to lack Volsung blood-hardness when tested), but a son of Sigmund himself. She disguises herself as a witch-wife, sleeps three nights in her brother's forest hut, and conceives. The child is **Sinfjotli**. When he is old enough, she tests him by sewing gloves through his flesh — he doesn't flinch, says "Volsung would have felt less." He is the one.

Sigmund and Sinfjotli spend years in the forest, becoming warriors and also wolf-skinchangers, running as wolves until they can return to human form. Sinfjotli kills eleven men alone before asking for help. They burn Siggeir's hall. Signy comes to the door and says: I knew what I was doing. I let my other children die. I slept with my brother to make this boy. Now the deed is done. And she walks back into the fire.

Sigmund's two sons from his first marriage are Helgi (who wins the war-maiden Sigrun) and **Sinfjotli** (who is poisoned at a feast by his step-mother Borghild, because Sinfjotli killed her brother). Sigmund carries Sinfjotli to a firth and a mysterious old man ferries him away — it is Odin.

For his second wife Sigmund chooses **Hjordis**, the wisest woman alive, even though he is old and a rival king Lyngi also wants her. She chooses Sigmund over Lyngi. This is his death warrant. Lyngi returns with an army. In the battle an old one-eyed man appears and breaks Sigmund's sword with a spear-shaft. It is Odin taking back what he gave. Sigmund falls.

Hjordis finds Sigmund dying among the slain. He tells her: "Odin does not will me to be healed. I have fought while it was his will. But you carry a son who will be the greatest of our kin. Keep the shards of the sword — of them a good sword shall be made, and it shall be called Gram, and our son shall bear it, and work many a great work." Then he dies at dawn.

Hjordis saves the shards. She disguises herself as a bondmaid to survive. Danish ships arrive — King Alf's fleet — and she and the real bondmaid are taken aboard. Alf's mother guesses the truth: the "bondmaid" knows what time it is because her gold ring goes cold at dawn. Alf treats Hjordis with honor. Their son is **Sigurd**, raised at the court of King Hjalprek with Alf as his stepfather.

Sigurd's foster-father is **Regin**, son of Hreidmar — a dwarf-smith of ancient cunning. Regin has a long story to tell: his father Hreidmar was paid the gold of Andvari the Dwarf as weregild when Loki killed Regin's brother Otter. Andvari, stripped of his hoard, kept only one ring — which Loki also took. Andvari cursed it: all that gold and all who hold it shall be bane to one another. The ring is **Andvari's-loom**. Regin's brother Fafnir killed their father for the gold and now lies on it in dragon-form on Gnita-heath. Regin wants Sigurd to kill Fafnir, recover the gold, avenge the family. He does not say he also wants the gold.

Regin makes Sigurd two swords; Sigurd breaks both on an anvil. He goes to his mother and asks for the shards of his father's sword. She gives them. Regin reforges them into Gram. Sigurd tests it: the sword cuts an anvil clean. He drops a wool-lock in a stream; it drifts against the blade and falls in two pieces. Gram is ready.

Before fighting Fafnir, Sigurd first avenges his father: he raises a fleet, destroys the sons of Hunding in a naval battle, kills the king Lyngi himself. Then he turns to the dragon.

On Gnita-heath, Odin-disguised counsels Sigurd to dig not one but many pits, letting Fafnir's blood drain away from him. Sigurd kills Fafnir from a pit, driving Gram up under the left shoulder. Fafnir speaks at length in his dying — warning Sigurd of the curse, asking questions about fate. Sigurd is unimpressed: "Every brave man wants wealth. If I feared death I would never fight." Fafnir dies.

Sigurd tastes the dragon's blood while roasting his heart for Regin. He touches his burned finger to his tongue and suddenly hears the birds speak. The birds tell him: Regin plans to kill him now and take the gold. Sigurd kills Regin and takes everything: two chests of gold, the sword Rotti, the Helm of Awe, the Gold Byrny. He loads Grani and rides.

He rides to Hindfell, a mountain ringed in fire. He passes through the fire into a castle with a golden roof, finds a warrior asleep in a byrny so tight it seems grown to the skin. He cuts it off. It is **Brynhild**: a Valkyrie put to sleep by Odin for choosing wrong in a battle (she gave victory to Agnar over Helm Gunnar, whom Odin had promised the win). She teaches Sigurd rune-wisdom for an afternoon. He declares himself: no other woman but her. She gives him a drink, binds him in knowledge of all the runes of fate, and they plight troth. He gives her Andvari's ring from Fafnir's hoard as betrothal.

Sigurd rides to the court of King Giuki. Queen Grimhild — Giuki's witch-wife — wants Sigurd for her daughter **Gudrun**. She gives him a drink of forgetfulness. All memory of Brynhild leaves him. He marries Gudrun and is adopted as a brother by Gunnar and Hogni, Giuki's sons. They feast five years.

Grimhild then decides Gunnar should marry Brynhild. Sigurd rides with Gunnar to woo her. But Brynhild has set a fire-wall: only the man who rides through it may have her. Gunnar's horse refuses. Grani (Sigurd's horse, Sleipnir's kin) refuses under Gunnar too. So Sigurd and Gunnar use a shape-swap spell Grimhild taught them: Sigurd wears Gunnar's face, rides Grani through the fire. In Brynhild's hall he tells her he is Gunnar. She accepts, according to her oath. He lies three nights beside her with Gram laid between them. He takes back Andvari's ring — it is on her finger from when he gave it on Hindfell — and gives her another ring from Fafnir's treasure. Then he rides back, resumes his own shape, and Gunnar marries Brynhild.

When Sigurd comes back to himself, he has memory of his oaths to Brynhild. He lets everything rest.

The catastrophe is triggered at the river. The two queens, Brynhild and Gudrun, wade separately: Brynhild wades farther out, claiming precedence. They argue — whose husband is the greater king? Gudrun, furious, reveals the truth: Sigurd rode that fire, not Gunnar. She shows Andvari's ring as proof. Brynhild sees it. She recognizes it. She goes white and says nothing all evening.

Then she sets herself against Sigurd's life. She tells Gunnar: kill Sigurd, or I leave you. She insists the night Sigurd rode the fire and lay beside her means Sigurd took her maidenhead. Gunnar knows this is wrong — Gram was between them — but Brynhild is his wife and his love. He uses Guttorm, his youngest brother who swore no oaths to Sigurd, feeding him a wolf-and-serpent potion until he is wild enough to do the murder.

The first two attempts, Guttorm enters Sigurd's chamber and retreats — Sigurd's waking eyes are too bright to meet. The third time Sigurd is asleep. Guttorm drives a sword through him. Sigurd's dying throw of Gram cuts Guttorm in half at the door. Gudrun wakes swimming in her husband's blood. Brynhild hears her grief-cry and laughs once — with something in it that sounds like satisfaction and something that sounds like a woman who has just destroyed the only thing she wanted.

Gudrun's grief-scene is the emotional peak of the saga. She cannot weep. Her sister-in-law Gullrond pulls the cover off Sigurd's face and places his head in Gudrun's lap. One look — the great eyes glazed, the bloodied locks — and the dam breaks. She weeps so hard the geese in the yard begin screaming. She compares him: "Such was my Sigurd among the sons of Giuki as the king-leek over the low grass." Her tears fall through the pillow.

Brynhild stabs herself and arranges her own funeral: burn her on Sigurd's pyre, sword between them, five bondwomen and eight bondmen, two hawks, two at the head, two at the feet. In her dying prophecies she tells the whole future: Gudrun will be drugged and given to Atli; Gunnar will be put in a worm-close; Gudrun will avenge everyone. Brynhild drives to Hell in her funeral chariot and argues with a giantess at the gate.

**Gudrun** is given to Atli — Brynhild's brother, Lord of the Huns — by Grimhild's third potion, against her will. She is never happy with him. She has children by him but no love.

Atli wants the Nibelung gold, which Gunnar and Hogni now hold. He invites them to a feast with promises of wealth. Gudrun sends a warning: a ring wrapped in wolf's hair, runes on it that say "beware." The messenger Vingi alters the runes to read "come." Their wives dream terrible dreams; they shrug them off. Gunnar and Hogni ride to Atli's hall.

It is a trap. Battle in the mead-hall. Gudrun fights at her brothers' side. They kill nineteen of Atli's best warriors but are finally taken.

Atli wants the gold. He says: tell me where it is and live. Gunnar says: show me Hogni's heart first. Atli's men cut out the heart of the thrall Hjalli instead and bring it to Gunnar on a plate. It trembles. "That is Hjalli's cowardly heart," Gunnar says. "Little like my brother's." So they cut out Hogni's heart. Hogni laughs as they do it. When they bring it to Gunnar, it does not tremble. "That is Hogni's heart," says Gunnar. "Now I know where the gold is, and no one else does. The Rhine shall rule over it rather than the Huns." He is cast into a worm-close. He plays the harp with his toes — Gudrun smuggled it to him — and his playing puts every serpent to sleep except one: Atli's mother in serpent form, who stings him in the heart.

Gudrun kills Atli in the night with her own hand (and Hogni's surviving son helps). She has already served Atli the flesh of his own sons at table. She burns the hall with all inside. Then she walks into the sea to end her life. The sea does not take her. It carries her to King Jonakr, who marries her. She bears sons Hamdir, Sorli, Erp. Her daughter **Swanhild** — Sigurd's child, the most beautiful woman in the world — is given to King Jormunrek and trampled to death by horses on the word of the slanderer Bikki.

Gudrun wakes her sons. They go to avenge Swanhild. On the road they meet and kill their half-brother Erp in a fit of pride. They reach Jormunrek, cut off his hands and feet, but cannot kill him — they realize too late that Erp would have cut off the head. Odin (one-eyed, ancient, appearing at the end as at the beginning) advises Jormunrek's men to use stones. Iron will not bite on the brothers. They are stoned to death. All the kin of the Giukings is gone.

The last words of the saga are a blessing: "Now may all earls be bettered in mind, may the grief of all maidens ever be minished, for this tale of sorrow so told to its ending."

### Historical Context

The Volsunga Saga was written down in Iceland in the 13th century, probably between 1200 and 1270 AD, drawing on older Eddaic poetry some of which dates to the 9th–10th centuries. The underlying historical kernel — Attila (Atli), the destruction of the Burgundians by the Huns — is real history from 437 AD, filtered through four centuries of Germanic and Norse tradition. The German parallel version, the Nibelungenlied (c.1200), strips away the mythological framework and turns Sigurd/Siegfried into a courtly romance hero; the Norse version keeps the gods, the curses, the doom-structure. Richard Wagner's Ring cycle (1848–1874) is the modern descendant; William Morris's Sigurd the Volsung (1876) is the Victorian one. The saga was translated into English for the first time by Morris and Magnússon in 1888 — this text. Before that it was essentially unknown in English.

The age in which the saga was composed — post-conversion Iceland, 13th century — was an age of retrospection. The old religion was gone. The Viking age was gone. The sagas were the way Iceland kept the past alive, and the Volsunga Saga is the most ambitious of them: a mythic epic that tries to hold the entire cosmological imagination of the Norse world.

### Chapter Notes

**Chs. I–II** (Sigi/Rerir/Volsung): Foundation chapters. Blood-debt, divine paternity, and the birth of the line. Establishes that every Volsung is born of sacrifice.

**Ch. III** (The Branstock sword): Odin sets the test. Sigmund is chosen by the weapon. Siggeir is marked by his response to refusal — he waits, noting everything.

**Ch. IV** (Siggeir takes Signy): Signy's foresight is introduced. She predicts disaster; Volsung overrides her with his code of honor. The price of that code is paid over the next ten chapters.

**Ch. V** (Slaying of Volsung): Signy's second warning, refused again. Volsung's speech on facing death without flight is the saga's first great set piece. He falls, but standing.

**Chs. V–VI** (She-wolf / Sigmund survives): The wolf is Siggeir's mother transformed. Nine brothers die one each night. Sigmund survives through wit: bites the wolf's tongue off.

**Ch. VII** (Birth of Sinfjotli): The most disturbing event in the saga is delivered without commentary. Signy has her incestuous son by Sigmund because she needs Volsung blood in all veins. Her calculation is correct.

**Ch. VIII** (Burning of Siggeir's hall): The revenge achieved. Signy's revelation and death. The drama is in what she doesn't explain until the last moment.

**Ch. IX** (Helgi and Sigrun): Interlude episode. Helgi is a great hero; he wins Sigrun against her betrothed. Structurally this establishes that Volsung sons are always for the greatest women.

**Ch. X** (Death of Sinfjotli): Borghild's poison at the feast, three times offered. Sigmund drinks for his son twice; the third time Sinfjotli accepts it himself. The mysterious ferryman (Odin) takes the body.

**Ch. XI** (Sigmund's last battle): Odin breaks his own sword. The gift is recalled. Sigmund doesn't resist — he says the moment is right. His dying instruction to save the shards is the hinge of the whole saga.

**Ch. XII** (Hjordis/Alf): The gold ring that goes cold at dawn reveals the disguise. A quiet, intelligent episode — the war is over; now the pieces must be gathered.

**Ch. XIII** (Sigurd's birth and youth): Sigurd's extraordinary eyes. Regin introduced as a double agent — tutor, manipulator, elder schemer with a long personal grievance.

**Ch. XIV** (Regin tells of Andvari's gold): The curse first named. This chapter is the essay in how curses work in the Norse world — through object, through inheritance, through nobody's fault. The gold is already traveling.

**Ch. XV** (Gram reforged): The sword that breaks Regin's two smithings, reforged from shards into something that cuts wool on a stream. The proof-tests are satisfying like a ritual.

**Ch. XVI** (Grifir's prophecy): Sigurd is told his whole fate before he acts. He goes anyway.

**Ch. XVII** (Revenge for Sigmund): Sigurd's war at sea. He asks Odin-disguised (as Hnikar) for battle-omens; gets a poem. Then victories. Business completed before the dragon.

**Ch. XVIII** (Slaying of Fafnir): The famous scene. Odin counsels the multiple pits. The dragon's dialogue is extraordinary — he knows everything and it changes nothing.

**Ch. XIX** (Slaying of Regin): The birds in the wood tell Sigurd what Regin plans. Sigurd kills him with Gram and takes the hoard. Eats Fafnir's heart (partially); keeps the rest.

**Ch. XX** (Brynhild on Hindfell): Sigurd cuts through the sleep-byrny. She wakes. The rune-teaching is one of the saga's most beautiful passages — it is the fullest expression of Brynhild's power before that power is locked into the mechanism of her doom.

**Ch. XXI** (More wisdom): Brynhild's practical counsels — honesty, care for the dead, wariness of dangerous women, don't trust the enemy's young kin. Everything Sigurd will fail to do.

**Chs. XXII–XXIV** (Sigurd's appearance / Hlymdale): Sigurd's physical description. The hawk that lands at Brynhild's tower window — he follows the hawk and sees her embroidering his own deeds. Love and grief are already running in parallel.

**Ch. XXV** (Gudrun's dream / Brynhild's interpretation): Gudrun dreams of a golden hawk; Brynhild interprets: Sigurd is coming, he will be yours, and you will quickly lose him. She already knows. She says it without flinching.

**Ch. XXVI** (Sigurd wed to Gudrun): Grimhild's forgetfulness potion. Five years pass in good fellowship. Sigurd remembers nothing.

**Ch. XXVII** (Wooing of Brynhild): Shape-swap through fire. Sword between them. The ring taken back. The most elaborate deception in the saga — and everyone involved will die for it.

**Ch. XXVIII** (The queens' quarrel at the river): The ring revealed. The scene where everything breaks. Brynhild goes white as a corpse. Structurally this is the point of no return.

**Ch. XXIX** (Brynhild's grief / Sigurd's last visit): The most devastating scene. Two people who love each other, who know they love each other, who cannot have each other, and who both know what is coming. Sigurd's byrny-rings burst from the pressure of his grief.

**Ch. XXX** (Slaying of Sigurd): Guttorm's three attempts. The dying throw of Gram. Gudrun waking in blood. Brynhild laughing.

**Ch. XXXI** (Gudrun's lament — First Lay of Gudrun): The grief poem. One of the finest things in Norse literature. Gudrun cannot weep until she sees his face.

**Ch. XXXII** (Death of Brynhild): Her dying prophecy covers the whole remaining saga. She arranges her pyre in technical detail.

**Ch. XXXIII** (Gudrun wed to Atli): The second forgetfulness potion. Gudrun resists; is overridden; is given.

**Chs. XXXIV–XXXVI** (Atli's trap / dreams): Dream prophecies ignored. Vingi's altered runes. The scene of the wives predicting exactly what will happen to their husbands. The husbands go anyway.

**Chs. XXXVII–XXXVIII** (Battle in Atli's hall): Gudrun fights with her brothers. The hearts brought on a platter. Gunnar's final refusal.

**Ch. XXXIX** (End of Atli): Gudrun feeds Atli his sons. Kills him in bed. Burns the hall.

**Chs. XL–XLIII** (Gudrun's last sorrow): The sea won't take her. Jonakr's sons. Swanhild trampled. The sons avenge her but kill Erp first and cannot complete the act. Stoned to death. All gone.

## Pass 2 — Characters

**Sigurd** is perhaps the most remarkable hero in the saga tradition precisely because he has no flaw in the tragic sense. He is brave, generous, loyal, beautiful, bird-speech-knowing, wise. He slays the dragon, reforges (via Regin) his father's sword, fulfills every prophecy. His catastrophe comes entirely from outside: a potion erases his memory of the woman he loves. He doesn't fail — the mechanism of the world fails him. This makes his death more terrible, not less. He dies knowing he did not deserve it. His last words are defense and prophecy: "None of what happens will profit them." His byrny bursting at the grief of his last conversation with Brynhild is the saga's single most physical image of interior agony.

**Brynhild** is the saga's true engine. She is a Valkyrie — she chose who lived and died in battle — punished by Odin for choosing the wrong man's victory and put to sleep until a hero fearless enough to cross fire can wake her. Sigurd is that hero. She loves him from the first. When she discovers through the ring that it was Sigurd who rode the fire and not Gunnar — that she was deceived into marrying a lesser man — her rage and grief are inseparable from each other. Her manipulation of Gunnar into killing Sigurd is not petty jealousy; it is the action of someone who cannot live in the wrong life and will unmake it if she must. She dies choosing: the sword between them on the pyre, the arrangement of the guard and bondmaids, the careful distribution of her gold. She chooses her death with the same authority she chose her battles. The final image — driving to Hell in a funeral chariot, arguing with a giantess at the gate — is exactly right.

**Gudrun** carries the grief of the whole saga. She loves Sigurd, loses him, is forced into a second marriage she despises, fights with her brothers at Atli's feast (she puts on a mailcoat and goes to the front), takes terrible revenge, tries to drown herself, washes up alive on another coast, has more children, loses her daughter Swanhild to the horses, sends her last sons to avenge Swanhild, and they are killed by stones. What makes Gudrun remarkable is that she never stops acting. She is the one who warns her brothers with the wolf-hair ring. She is the one who fights beside them. She is the one who kills Atli. She is also the one who kills Atli's sons by her own hand to serve them at table. She is the darkest kind of mourner: someone for whom grief converts directly into action, all of it eventual, much of it terrible.

**Signy** is the great forgotten figure of the saga's first half. She is everything Brynhild is, concentrated into a smaller space. She has the foresight to see the disaster coming. She is forced into it anyway by her father's honor-code. She then spends years as a patient double agent inside her enemy's household, sending her sons to be tested and found wanting, sleeping with her own brother to get the right blood into the right child. When the revenge is complete she walks into the fire she set, without hesitation or self-pity. The saga barely pauses. Sigmund's "great grief at heart" is mentioned in one line. The saga has enormous respect for Signy — it shows her everything she sees and does, without commentary — but it does not coddle her.

**Regin** is the master manipulator whose scheme destroys him. He is the smith who forges the world's greatest sword, using shards he did not gather. His claim to sympathy is real: his father was killed, his brother became a monster, his heritage was stolen. But his method — grooming a hero from childhood, withholding what he knows, angling for Sigurd to do his killing and then planning to do Sigurd's — is pure instrumentalism. When the birds tell Sigurd what Regin intends, you believe them instantly because Regin's behavior throughout has prepared it.

**Gunnar** is more interesting in defeat than in triumph. He is not a villain. He loves Brynhild. He wants Sigurd to live. He makes the decision to use Guttorm slowly, painfully, against his own better judgment, because he cannot face losing Brynhild and because his oath to Sigurd is actually balanced against his love. In the worm-pit he achieves something like heroic grandeur: playing the harp with his toes, keeping the gold's location to himself even knowing there is no longer any reason to do so except refusal. "The Rhine shall rule it rather than the Huns." He is still that man.

**Hogni** is the clearer head. He always says what is true. He knows the journey to Atli is a trap. He argues against killing Sigurd. He laughs when they cut his heart out — the saga says "such was the might of his manhood" — because he has never been afraid of anything and he is not going to start now. The heart brought on the plate to Gunnar — "little like Hjalli's faint heart" — is one of the saga's most horrible and wonderful images.

**Andvari's Ring** (Andvari's-loom / the cursed gold): The non-human character at the center of everything. Loki stripped it from the dwarf Andvari as the last piece of a weregild; Andvari cursed it before giving it up. The ring passes from Odin to Hreidmar to Fafnir's hoard to Sigurd to Brynhild (first betrothal, on Hindfell) to Sigurd again (taken back at the false wooing) to Gudrun's hand (implied) to the scene at the river where it is shown to Brynhild and breaks everything open. The ring is not magical in itself — it doesn't make people fall in love or control their wills. It simply marks who is entangled. When Brynhild sees it on someone else's hand, she knows exactly what it means: the real betrothal, and who betrayed it.

## Pass 3 — The Scene at the River (Chapter XXVIII)

It begins as a quarrel about protocol.

The two queens have gone to bathe in the river. This is a known custom — women of rank bathing together was a social ritual. Brynhild wades farther into the river than Gudrun, claiming by her position that she has precedence — her father is mightier, her husband has done greater deeds.

Gudrun has been trying not to say this for years. She says it.

"A marvellous thing truly," she says. "My husband slew Fafnir and Regin, and all the sons of Hunding. Siggeir he avenged. When he was fifteen he won his wars. And thy husband, Gunnar — thou wouldst be wise if thou shouldst hold thy peace rather than revile him." She is building up to it. She still hasn't said the thing.

Brynhild presses. She says her father is mightier. She says Gunnar rode through the flaming fire when Sigurd was but a thrall of King Hjalprek. This is the insult that breaks Gudrun open.

"Thou wouldst be wise if thou shouldst hold thy peace. Sigurd rode that fire. Not Gunnar. Sigurd wore Gunnar's face by a shape-spell Grimhild taught them. It was Sigurd who came into thy hall."

And she holds out Andvari's ring.

Brynhild sees it. She knows it. She gave it to him on Hindfell. It was on her finger when Sigurd (wearing Gunnar's face) took it back during the false wooing. She has been without it since that night. She has known, in some part of herself, that the man who rode through her fire was the man she pledged herself to on the mountain, and she has been living in that knowledge for years, in her gilded rooms in Gunnar's hall, embroidering Sigurd's deeds in gold thread, and now the ring is in front of her face and there is nothing left to not-know.

The saga says: *"Then Brynhild saw the ring and knew it, and waxed as wan as a dead woman, and she went home and spake no word the evening long."*

She does not argue. She does not accuse Gudrun of lying. She does not rage. She goes home and says nothing.

What follows — the seven days of lying in bed, the summoning of Gunnar, the manipulation of the oath-bound brothers into killing the one man she ever loved — all of it flows directly from that moment of silence. The silence is the worst thing in the saga. It is the moment when Brynhild looks at the ring in Gudrun's hand and sees, whole and entire, what her life has been and what she is going to do about it. The decision is made in that silence. By the time she speaks again — when Sigurd comes to her chamber — the conclusion is already settled. She tells him: "the sorest sorrow to me is that the bitter sword is not reddened in thy blood." She has already decided.

Sigurd's response, when he finally admits what he knows, is the saga's most heartbreaking speech: "Ah, yea. And where in all the world was she when she said she had the noblest of all men, and the dearest to her heart?" He says it to Gudrun; Gudrun hears in his voice what he still feels for Brynhild. She does not ask him about it. She goes to sleep beside him.

The ring returns to Gudrun's hand. The ring that was Andvari's last possession, that Loki stripped from him, that Andvari cursed before giving up. It has done exactly what cursed gold does: it has bound everyone who touched it to one another, across years, across a forgetfulness potion, across a false wedding, and now the binding tightens.

When Sigurd comes to Brynhild's chamber — his byrny-rings burst from the swell of his grief, the saga says, the rings of iron snapping under the pressure — he offers to divorce Gudrun and marry her. She refuses. She says she will not have two kings in one hall. She says she has sworn an oath and will hold it or die. ## 5-Act Vignette Play: "The Shards of Gram"

**TOKEN:** The Shards of Gram — two pieces of iron wrapped in linen, tied with a braid cut from Hjordis's own hair. They are a broken sword. They look like nothing. They are not nothing.

---

### ACT I — *The Shards · Cold on the Earth · Hjordis Searches for Them · Sigmund's Battlefield, an Hour Before Dawn*

The battlefield is still. The dead are everywhere. Dogs and ravens are beginning, at the edges.

The woman who moves through the bodies wears a bondmaid's rough cloth, though the bearing is not a bondmaid's. Behind her, the real bondmaid wears a princess's gold — the decoy. Behind the bondmaid, the Fighter moves without sound, watching the tree-line, where torchlight flickers from Lyngi's scouts.

She finds Sigmund. He is not yet dead. He speaks to her in pieces: *Odin does not will my healing. This wound is his gift. Keep the shards of the sword — of them a goodly sword shall be made for our son — and it shall be called Gram.*

She does not weep. She takes one piece of iron from his open hand and searches the mud for the other, eight feet from where he fell. She wraps both in the cloth she carried folded at her breast and ties the bundle with a braid she cuts from her own hair.

*The Fighter thinks: she has already decided what these things are.*

---

### ACT II — *The Shards · Wrapped in Linen · Hjordis Carries Them Inside Her Arms · The Wood's Edge, the Women Changed*

The decoy is in place: the bondmaid wears the fine dress, Hjordis wears the rough, arms crossed at the chest over the bundle. When Lyngi's scouts sweep the field with torches, they will look at the one in gold.

The torches come through the bodies.

One scout stops near the two women. He stares at the one in rough cloth. He takes a step.

The Fighter moves: a dead man's helm kicked clattering down a slope behind the patrol. Torches turn. The women walk past in the dark.

*The Fighter thinks: I have just made someone else's future possible without knowing whose.*

---

### ACT III — *The Shards · Hidden Against Hjordis's Chest · The Coastal Road, a Timber Gate*

A thorn-hedge runs to the shore. In its middle, a gate: two of Lyngi's men with torches, checking travelers. Beyond the gate, sand, sea, ships at anchor with lanterns.

The Fighter talks. Loud, offhand, his own pack open for inspection. The guards study him. Hjordis keeps her arms crossed, her face down.

One guard steps toward her. His hand moves toward the bundle.

The fight is short. Not silent enough. But the gate is open and the bodies are on the far side of it and there is distance between them and the shore.

*The Fighter thinks: every gate I open tonight will be forgotten by morning. The iron in that cloth will not.*

---

### ACT IV — *The Shards · At Hjordis's Chest · The Shore, Grey Pre-Dawn*

Alf's ships ride at anchor. A skiff is offshore. The sailor in it calls out: who comes? What women?

Hjordis does not answer. The Fighter does not answer for her. He calls back with enough weight — not authority exactly, but something that carries across water — and the skiff moves.

Hjordis steps forward to the prow-rope. She does not ask. She claims. The sailor looks at her hands.

He rows.

*The Fighter thinks: she knew the gesture before I did.*

---

### ACT V — *The Shards · Unwrapped · Given Away · Alf's Longship, at Dawn*

Alf stands at the ship's stem, young and iron-clad, looking between the two women.

Hjordis holds out her left hand. The gold ring on her finger is cold — dawn has come and the ring has gone cold, as it always does, as her father told her it would. Her fingertip is pale at the root.

Alf looks at it. He knows the sign.

She unwraps the linen from her breast. Two pieces of iron, dark and dull. He takes them from her hands and holds them for a long moment, turning them over. He rewraps them more carefully than she did — tighter, the knot set close — and places them in the chest at the ship's bow.

"For his son," he says.

She nods.

The bundle is in the chest. The linen tied. The braid still knotted. The shards are not a sword. But they have a destination now.

*The Fighter thinks: I carried something that doesn't exist yet. I don't know its name.*

### 5-Act Vignette Play: *The Pyre-Arrangements*

*Storyline 7 — Execution of Last Instructions. Brynhild has arranged her own death and dictated how it will be completed. #### ACT I — *Brynhild's Chamber*
*The sword: on the wall, still hers. Location: GNH — Gunnar's hall, Brynhild's chamber.*

The hall is wrong in the way that halls become wrong when someone has decided to die. Brynhild is lying on her bed. She has already stabbed herself — not deeply, not yet fatal, enough. Her gold is distributed in neat piles on the table: each pile labeled for a specific person with a specific instruction. She has been doing this for hours. She is precise.

She looks at the Fighter when they enter and does not waste time.

**Brynhild:** "Take the sword from the wall."

The sword is old. It has been on the wall for years — since the night in this hall when she was given to Gunnar and Sigurd slept beside her in Gunnar's face with this sword between them, so that she would be kept for Gunnar correctly and not for Sigurd incorrectly, and the sword did its job and the keeping was correct in the law and wrong in everything else.

She watches the Fighter take it from the wall.

**Brynhild:** "It goes between us on the pyre. Edge up. Horizontal. As it was in the hall. Not at his feet. Beside him." A pause. "Gunnar will try to prevent this. He does not want people to remember what he did. You will not allow it to be prevented. Can you do that?"

*Insight check DC 12: understand what "beside him" means and why it matters — not as ceremony but as statement; this placement corrects the story publicly in the only form still available to her.*

**Failure:** You understand the instruction mechanically — where to place the sword, how to hold it. You miss the grammar of it: why it is this sword, why it goes between them, why beside and not at his feet. You carry it out of the chamber with the bearing of someone completing a task. She watches you go. "Do it exactly," she says — not a correction, but an instruction given to someone who has not fully understood.

**Success:** You take the sword from the wall in both hands and hold it for a moment — the weight of it, the specific history in the cross-guard, the mark on the scabbard's edge from the night it lay on a cold floor between two people who could not have each other. You say nothing. Brynhild closes her eyes. You receive the Sword Between.

---

#### ACT II — *The Hall*
*The sword: carried through Gunnar's hall. Location: GNH.*

Gunnar meets you in the hall. He has been expecting this. He is not a villain in the simple sense — he is a man who did a terrible thing and cannot stop the accounting from arriving, and he would very much prefer the accounting to arrive quietly.

**Gunnar:** "The sword stays in the hall. A funeral pyre does not require weapons. I am her husband and I say it stays."

He has legal standing. He is her husband. He is also the man whose false swearing and manipulation brought every event in this story to its current point. He knows both of these things.

*Persuasion DC 12: name Brynhild's authority over her own death — not his authority over her funeral, which is real; but the older authority, the one that supersedes it: the authority of the dying person over the form of their death, which he cannot legally override unless he is willing to say in public that he is doing so.*

**Failure:** He takes the sword. You argue. He is not convinced but he is temporarily embarrassed by the legal question you've raised, and his chamberlain — who is more worried about the household's reputation than about the sword — tells him to let you proceed. He does, grudgingly, and with a warning that the sword returns to the hall after.

**Success:** "You can prevent the sword from going to the pyre. You would have to say, in the hall, in front of your people, that you are overriding Brynhild's last instruction — that her dying wishes are subject to your amendment." Gunnar is quiet for a long time. He stands aside. He does not agree. He does not prevent.

#### ACT III — *The Pyre Site*
*The sword: in the Fighter's hands, approaching the pyre. Location: GNP — funeral pyre ground, Gunnar's hall.*

The pyre has been built. Sigurd's body has been laid on it. Gunnar's men are now preparing to position Brynhild's body — and the position they have chosen is at Sigurd's feet. At his feet means servant. At his feet means the story Gunnar is telling, which is the story of a woman who was faithfully married to Gunnar and caught a fever of grief at her husband's kinsman's death.

The instruction is beside him, sword between.

*Composure check DC 12: hold the instruction publicly, in front of Gunnar's men, against the arrangement already in progress — without anger, without accusation; simply stating Brynhild's instruction in the form of a fact that cannot be disputed because she said it in the presence of witnesses.*

**Failure:** Gunnar's men argue back. The situation escalates. Gunnar himself arrives. The body's position becomes a debate. You win the debate eventually but the arrangement has taken so long that the pyre-master has moved to other preparations and you must complete the placement yourself, which draws attention.

**Success:** "Beside him. Her instruction." The men look at each other and then at the instruction. Brynhild spoke it in front of witnesses — several of them were there. They move the bier to the correct position. The space between the bodies is now open, waiting for the sword.

#### ACT IV — *The Sword, Contested*
*The sword: in the Fighter's hand, at the pyre's edge. Location: GNP.*

Gunnar has made a decision. He cannot stop the sword from going to the pyre — you have made the legal situation clear — but he has sent a warrior to take it from the Fighter by force on the grounds that it is a piece of hall property being disposed of without his explicit authorization. The warrior has a letter of authorization from Gunnar's chamberlain.

The chamberlain's letter is not invalid. The authorization is real. The only counter-argument is the sword in the Fighter's hand, and the sword's commission, and the pyre waiting twenty feet away.

*Combat: Gunnar's Hall-Warrior, AC 14 HP 26. He has a letter. He also has a sword.*

**Failure:** The hall-warrior takes the sword. He is a professional — he uses minimum force, disables rather than kills, and walks back to Gunnar's hall with the sword. The pyre is lit without it. Brynhild's last instruction is not carried out. The story Gunnar wanted is the story that gets told.

**Success:** The hall-warrior learns that a letter of chamberlain authorization is not, in practical terms, the same as the physical argument it was supposed to replace. He retreats. The sword is in your hands. The pyre is twenty feet away. One act remains.

---

#### ACT V — *The Placement*
*The sword: ready to be placed. Location: GNP.*

Sigurd's body is on the pyre. Brynhild's body is beside him. The space between them is open.

The instruction was: edge up, horizontal, between them. As it was in the hall.

*Precision check DC 11: place the sword exactly as specified — edge up, horizontal between them, the cross-guard even, the blade lying flat — correctly enough that anyone looking will see that this is not a burial weapon but a specific gesture, and will understand what it means.*

**Failure:** You place the sword. It is between them. But the angle is wrong by a fraction — it is not quite horizontal, not quite as she specified. The pyre is lit. The instruction is completed. But the placement is not perfect, and perfection was the point: the specific form of this was the statement.
