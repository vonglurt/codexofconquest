# Paul of Tarsus: A Game Designer's Node Map Reference

## Primary Source Methodology

All citations draw from: Acts of the Apostles (chs. 7–28), Galatians 1–2, Romans 15, 2 Corinthians 11, Philippians 3–4, Philemon, and relevant cross-references from 1–2 Thessalonians, 1–2 Corinthians, Colossians, Ephesians. Where Paul's letters conflict with Acts chronologically, both versions are noted with the discrepancy flagged. The "Western text" variants of Acts (found in Codex Bezae) occasionally add details; these are flagged where relevant.

---

## NODE 00 — BACKGROUND: Saul's Origin

**Location:** Tarsus, Cilicia (modern Mersin province, southern Turkey)

Saul was born in Tarsus, a Hellenized Roman city and the capital of the province of Cilicia. He was a Roman citizen by birth (Acts 22:28), which means his family held citizenship before him — likely granted during the period of Pompey or Julius Caesar, or through some civic service. This citizen status is a major mechanical asset throughout the narrative.

He identifies himself in three registers simultaneously: "a Hebrew of Hebrews" (Phil 3:5), a Pharisee of the tribe of Benjamin, AND a Roman citizen with the Greek-speaking education of a Diaspora Jew. He was sent to Jerusalem for advanced rabbinic training under Gamaliel I (Acts 22:3), one of the most prestigious legal scholars of the era.

**Physical description (historical/apocryphal):** The canonical texts never describe Paul's appearance except obliquely. In 2 Corinthians 10:10, his opponents mock him: "his letters are weighty and powerful, but his bodily presence is weak and his speech contemptible." In Galatians 4:13–15, Paul references "a bodily illness" that prompted his initial visit to Galatia — something about his physical condition was evidently noticeable. The Acts of Paul and Thecla (2nd century apocrypha, non-canonical but widely circulated) describes him as "a man small of stature, with a bald head and crooked legs, in a good state of body, with eyebrows meeting and nose somewhat hooked, full of friendliness." Many scholars consider this description to draw on authentic early tradition, since unflattering physical traits would be unlikely inventions. The "thorn in the flesh" (2 Cor 12:7, see below) has generated centuries of speculation: recurring malaria, epilepsy, severe eye disease (Gal 4:15 suggests an eye condition), and migraine headaches are among the leading proposals. None are confirmed in the text.

**Occupation:** Tentmaking (σκηνοποιός, skenopoios). Acts 18:3. This term may mean leather-worker or weaver of goat-hair cloth (cilicium, named after his home province Cilicia), not exclusively tent manufacture. The skill travels with him and provides economic independence wherever he goes.

## NODE 01 — JERUSALEM: Saul the Persecutor

**Location:** Jerusalem, Judea (modern East Jerusalem, Israel/Palestinian Authority)

**How he got there:** Arrived as a young student for Pharisaic education under Gamaliel (Acts 22:3). His family evidently had connections: his sister's son (nephew) lives in Jerusalem and later saves his life (Acts 23:16), suggesting the family may have maintained a household there.

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified for this period.

**Who he met:**
- **Gamaliel** (Acts 22:3) — rabbinic teacher, Pharisee, member of the Sanhedrin. Friendly (as teacher).
- **The Jerusalem Sanhedrin leadership** — he acts as their agent, indicating high trust.
- **Stephen** (Acts 7:58, 8:1) — Hellenistic Jewish-Christian deacon. Hostile relationship; Stephen is Saul's first documented victim.

**Key event — Stoning of Stephen (Acts 7:54–8:1):**
The stoning is the narrative hinge. Stephen delivers his long speech (Acts 7:2–53), a retelling of Israelite history as a pattern of rejection. The crowd responds with fury (Acts 7:54: "they gnashed on him with their teeth"). Stephen, described as seeing a vision of "the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God" (7:56), is dragged outside the city and stoned.

**Saul's specific role:** Acts 7:58 says "the witnesses laid down their clothes at a young man's feet, whose name was Saul." He is the garment-watcher — not the thrower. But Acts 8:1 immediately follows: "And Saul was consenting unto his death." His guilt is explicit. He "made havoc of the church, entering into every house, and haling men and women committed them to prison" (Acts 8:3).

**What happened:**
Following Stephen's death, a "great persecution" scatters the Jerusalem church (Acts 8:1). The apostles stay; everyone else disperses. Saul is the primary enforcement mechanism.

**Departure from this phase:** Saul requests and receives letters from the high priest authorizing him to travel to Damascus to arrest Christians there (Acts 9:1–2, 22:5). This is approximately 240 km north-northeast.

## NODE 02 — THE DAMASCUS ROAD: Conversion

**Location:** Road between Jerusalem and Damascus, Syria (modern Syria). The road passes through the Jordan Valley, around the Sea of Galilee, and north. The encounter is commonly placed near Damascus itself.

**How he got there:** Overland with a group of companions (Acts 9:7 — "the men which journeyed with him stood speechless"). The high priest's letters were his authorization documents. Travel companions are unnamed.

**Where he stayed:** N/A (in transit)

**Who he met:**
- **The Risen Christ** — Acts 9:3–6, 22:6–11, 26:12–18 (three separate tellings, with slight variations, which is a fascinating design note: each retelling emphasizes different details for different audiences).

**Key conversation — The Damascus Road (Acts 9:4–6):**
> "Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?" / "Who art thou, Lord?" / "I am Jesus whom thou persecutest: it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks." / "Lord, what wilt thou have me to do?" / "Arise, and go into the city, and it shall be told thee what thou must do."

In the Acts 26 retelling (before Agrippa), the voice adds: "I have appeared unto thee for this purpose, to make thee a minister and a witness both of these things which thou hast seen, and of those things in the which I will appear unto thee; Delivering thee from the people, and from the Gentiles, unto whom now I send thee" (26:16–17). This version adds the commission that Acts 9 omits.

**What happened:**
- Midday (Acts 22:6 — "about noon"). A light "above the brightness of the sun" (Acts 26:13).
- Saul falls to the ground (Acts 9:4).
- He is blinded: "And when his eyes were opened, he saw no man: and they led him by the hand, and brought him into Damascus" (Acts 9:8).
- His companions hear a sound but see no one (Acts 9:7), or in the Acts 22:9 variant, "see the light" but hear no voice — a textual inconsistency in Acts itself.
- Three days without sight, eating nothing, drinking nothing (Acts 9:9).

**Departure conditions:** Led blind into Damascus.

## NODE 03 — DAMASCUS, EPISODE ONE: Ananias, Baptism, and the Escape

**Location:** Damascus, Syria (modern Damascus, Syria — one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world)

**How he got there:** Led by hand (Acts 9:8).

**Where he stayed:** The house of Judas on the Street called Straight (Acts 9:11 — "the street which is called Straight"). This is a named, locatable address. The Via Recta was a real colonnaded main street of Roman Damascus, still partially visible. Saul is there for three days, blind, fasting.

**Who he met:**
- **Ananias** (Acts 9:10–17) — a Damascus disciple. He has a vision instructing him to go to Saul. He protests: he has heard about Saul's persecutions. He is told "he is a chosen vessel unto me, to bear my name before the Gentiles, and kings, and the children of Israel" (Acts 9:15). Ananias is friendly despite fear. He lays hands on Saul, restores his sight, and baptizes him.
- **The Damascus disciples** (Acts 9:19) — Saul "was certain days with the disciples which were at Damascus." Friendly.
- **The Jews in the Damascus synagogues** (Acts 9:20–22) — Saul immediately begins preaching that Jesus is the Son of God. Initial reaction: "amazement" and confusion. It escalates to murderous hostility: "And after that many days were fulfilled, the Jews took counsel to kill him" (Acts 9:23).

**Key events:**
- Saul begins preaching immediately in the synagogues (Acts 9:20).
- His argument: proving that Jesus is the Christ (Acts 9:22 — "proving that this is very Christ").
- A conspiracy forms. The conspirators watch the city gates day and night (Acts 9:24).

**Meals:** During his initial three-day blindness, no food or drink (Acts 9:9). After receiving his sight and being baptized, he "received meat" — the first recorded meal in Paul's life (Acts 9:19).

**KEY DESIGN NOTE — The Arabian Interlude (Galatians 1:17):**
Here Acts and Galatians diverge significantly. Galatians 1:15–17 says that after his conversion, Paul "immediately conferred not with flesh and blood: Neither went I up to Jerusalem to them which were apostles before me; but I went into Arabia, and returned again unto Damascus." Acts contains no mention of this Arabian retreat whatsoever.

**Arabia** in this context likely means the Nabataean kingdom (capital Petra, modern Jordan) or its fringes — desert territory east and south of Damascus. Duration is unspecified but the full gap between conversion and first Jerusalem visit is "three years" (Gal 1:18). The Arabian withdrawal is presumably for prayer, study, and whatever Paul calls "receiving the gospel... by revelation of Jesus Christ" (Gal 1:12). The Nabataean king Aretas IV is relevant: in 2 Corinthians 11:32, Paul mentions "the governor under Aretas the king kept the city of the Damascenes with a garrison" to arrest him. This suggests Paul may have irritated Nabataean political structures during his time in Arabia, creating a two-front threat (Jewish conspirators from within, Nabataean officials from without).

**Departure — The Basket Escape:**
Acts 9:24–25: "And they watched the gates day and night to kill him. Then the disciples took him by night, and let him down by the wall in a basket."

2 Corinthians 11:32–33 is Paul's own account, with a slight variation: "In Damascus the governor under Aretas the king kept the city of the Damascenes with a garrison, desirous to apprehend me: And through a window in a basket was I let down by the wall, and escaped his hands."

Paul lists this humiliating escape in his "boasting of weaknesses" passage. The basket (σπυρίς, spuris) is a large wicker or rope container. The city walls of Damascus had domestic buildings built into them; the window was likely in such a structure.

## NODE 04 — JERUSALEM VISIT #1: Fifteen Days with Peter

**Location:** Jerusalem, Judea (modern East Jerusalem)

**How he got there:** Overland from Damascus. Three years after conversion (Gal 1:18 — "Then after three years I went up to Jerusalem").

**Where he stayed:** Unspecified initially; significantly with Peter. Galatians 1:18 says he "abode with him fifteen days."

**Who he met:**
- **Barnabas** (Acts 9:27) — A Levite from Cyprus, a senior Jerusalem disciple. His role here is pivotal: he is the one who "took him, and brought him to the apostles, and declared unto them how he had seen the Lord in the way, and that he had spoken to him, and how he had preached boldly at Damascus." Without Barnabas vouching, Saul had no entry. Barnabas is friendly — his name means "Son of Encouragement" (Acts 4:36).
- **Peter (Cephas)** (Acts 9:27, Gal 1:18) — Saul specifically went "to see Peter" (Gal 1:18, ἱστορῆσαι Κηφᾶν — "to get to know Cephas"). Fifteen days of direct conversation. These two are the two most important figures in early Christianity; their interaction here is the only substantial personal meeting Paul confirms.
- **James, brother of Jesus** (Gal 1:19) — "But other of the apostles saw I none, save James the Lord's brother." James has emerged as a Jerusalem church leader. Paul categorizes him as an "apostle" here, though technically he was not one of the Twelve.
- **The Hellenistic Jews (Hellenists)** (Acts 9:29) — Paul "spake boldly in the name of the Lord Jesus, and disputed against the Grecians [Hellenists]." They "went about to slay him." Same community that opposed Stephen.

**What happened:**
- Initial fear from the disciples: "they were all afraid of him, and believed not that he was a disciple" (Acts 9:26). Barnabas's intervention resolves this.
- Paul's preaching in Jerusalem provokes the same murderous reaction as in Damascus.
- "The brethren" (Acts 9:30) learn of the plot and intervene.

**Departure conditions:** "They brought him down to Caesarea, and sent him forth to Tarsus" (Acts 9:30). This is a protective escort — he is escorted to Caesarea Maritima (the Roman port city, ~90 km northwest of Jerusalem) and shipped home to Cilicia.

## NODE 05 — TARSUS: The Silent Years

**Location:** Tarsus, Cilicia (modern Tarsus, Mersin province, Turkey)

**Duration:** Approximately 8–10 years. Acts 9:30 sends Saul to Tarsus; Acts 11:25–26 has Barnabas going to Tarsus to retrieve him "to bring him unto Antioch." The intervening material in Acts covers: Peter's Joppa/Lydda ministry, the conversion of Cornelius (Acts 10), and the founding of the Antioch church by scattered Jerusalem disciples (Acts 11:19–21). This gap is typically dated ~37–46 CE.

**What happened (inferential):** Galatians 1:21 confirms: "Afterwards I came into the regions of Syria and Cilicia." Paul's letters occasionally reference experiences that must fall here: visions, suffering, being "in deaths oft" before his missionary journeys begin. 2 Corinthians 11:23–27 lists "stripes above measure, in prisons more frequent, in deaths oft" — some of these must predate the journeys documented in Acts, suggesting active ministry during the Tarsus years that left no narrative record.

The "man in Christ... caught up to the third heaven" vision (2 Cor 12:2–4) — "fourteen years ago," counting back from ~57 CE when 2 Corinthians is written — places this vision at approximately 43 CE, squarely in the Tarsus period.

**The Thorn in the Flesh (2 Cor 12:7):** "And lest I should be exalted above measure through the abundance of the revelations, there was given to me a thorn in the flesh, the messenger of Satan to buffet me, lest I should be exalted above measure." This arrives as a direct consequence of the third-heaven vision. Paul asked three times for its removal; the answer was "My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Cor 12:9). The Greek (σκόλοψ τῇ σαρκί, skolops tē sarki) means literally "a stake in the flesh" — sharp, lodged, recurring. Proposals: chronic ophthalmia/eye disease (supported by Gal 4:15: "if it had been possible, ye would have plucked out your own eyes, and have given them to me"), malaria contracted in lowland Pamphylia (the timing of the Galatian visit in Acts 13 fits), epilepsy, a speech impediment, or a recurring spiritual/psychological assault. The game designer note: this is a mechanical debuff that cannot be removed — three prayer attempts failed. It defines Paul's ministry posture: "when I am weak, then am I strong" (2 Cor 12:10).

**Where he stayed:** Presumably with family. Lodging unspecified in sources.

## NODE 06 — ANTIOCH ON THE ORONTES: The Base of Operations

**Location:** Antioch on the Orontes, Syria (modern Antakya, Hatay province, southern Turkey, near the Syrian border)

**How Saul got there:** Barnabas, already established in Antioch, travels to Tarsus specifically to find Saul (Acts 11:25 — "Then departed Barnabas to Tarsus, for to seek Saul"). This is approximately 160 km overland. Barnabas's deliberate retrieval suggests Saul had a reputation for effectiveness that Barnabas believed was needed.

**Duration of first stay:** One full year of teaching (Acts 11:26).

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified.

**Who he met:**
- **Barnabas** — established leader here, functioning as Saul's senior partner at this stage.
- **The Antioch church** — a mixed Jewish-Gentile community. Acts 11:20 notes that some Jerusalem refugees "spake unto the Grecians [Gentiles], preaching the Lord Jesus" — Antioch is the first formally mixed church.
- **Agabus** (Acts 11:27–28) — a prophet from Jerusalem who visits Antioch and predicts a great famine "throughout all the world: which came to pass in the days of Claudius Caesar." This is datable: the famine in Judea is documented by Josephus as occurring under the procurator Tiberius Julius Alexander (~46–48 CE). Agabus will appear again later.
- **Simeon called Niger, Lucius of Cyrene, Manaen (foster brother of Herod Antipas), and others** (Acts 13:1) — the prophets and teachers at Antioch. A cosmopolitan group.

**Key event:**
Acts 11:26 — "And the disciples were called Christians first in Antioch." This is the origin of the term. It is likely a Roman administrative label (the -ianus suffix being typically Roman, designating followers of a named leader: Christianus = follower of Christus).

**The Famine Relief Mission (Acts 11:29–30; 12:25):**
The Antioch church collects relief funds for Jerusalem. "Then the disciples, every man according to his ability, determined to send relief unto the brethren which dwelt in Judaea: Which also they did, and sent it to the elders by the hands of Barnabas and Saul." This is Paul's Jerusalem visit #2 — the "famine visit." Galatians doesn't mention it, which has generated scholarly debate (some argue Gal 2:1–10 describes this visit, not the Jerusalem Council).

**John Mark joins:** Acts 12:25 — "And Barnabas and Saul returned from Jerusalem, when they had fulfilled their ministry, and took with them John, whose surname was Mark." John Mark is Barnabas's cousin (Col 4:10) and the probable author of the Gospel of Mark. He is a significant NPC throughout the first journey and a source of later conflict.

## FIRST MISSIONARY JOURNEY (~46–48 CE)

### NODE 07 — ANTIOCH COMMISSIONING

**Key event:** Acts 13:1–3 — "As they ministered to the Lord, and fasted, the Holy Ghost said, Separate me Barnabas and Saul for the work whereunto I have called them. And when they had fasted and prayed, and laid their hands on them, they sent them away."

**Travel party:** Barnabas, Saul, John Mark (as "minister"/assistant — Acts 13:5).

### NODE 08 — SELEUCIA PIERIA / CYPRUS: Salamis

**Location:** Seleucia was Antioch's port, ~25 km west. They sailed to Cyprus — Barnabas's homeland (Acts 4:36), approximately 100 km across open water.

**First stop on Cyprus:** Salamis (Acts 13:5) — the main eastern port of Cyprus (modern Famagusta area, Cyprus).

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified.

**What happened:** "They preached the word of God in the synagogues of the Jews" (Acts 13:5). No named individuals, no recorded events. Moving through.

---

### NODE 09 — PAPHOS, CYPRUS: Bar-Jesus and the Proconsul

**Location:** Paphos, western Cyprus — the Roman administrative capital of the island. Modern Paphos, Cyprus.

**How he got there:** Traveled through the island (Acts 13:6 — "when they had gone through the isle unto Paphos").

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified.

**Who he met:**
- **Sergius Paulus** (Acts 13:7) — the Roman proconsul (anthupatos — correct technical term for a senatorial province governor). He is described as "a prudent [intelligent] man" who "desired to hear the word of God." Friendly to neutral.
- **Bar-Jesus / Elymas** (Acts 13:6–8) — "a certain sorcerer, a false prophet, a Jew, whose name was Bar-jesus." He serves in Sergius Paulus's circle and "withstood them, seeking to turn away the deputy from the faith." Hostile.

**Key event — First use of Paul's Roman name and first miracle:**
Acts 13:9: "Then Saul, (who also is called Paul)." From this point forward, Acts uses "Paul." The name change is not supernatural — Saul/Shaul was his Hebrew name, Paulus his Roman cognomen. The shift signals a deliberate Gentile orientation.

Paul confronts Bar-Jesus directly: "O full of all subtilty and all mischief, thou child of the devil, thou enemy of all righteousness" (Acts 13:10) and pronounces temporary blindness on him: "thou shalt be blind, not seeing the sun for a season" (Acts 13:11). The mirror is striking — Paul himself was blinded at conversion.

Sergius Paulus "believed, when he saw what was done" (Acts 13:12). This is the first Gentile convert of the journey.

**Power shift:** At Paphos, Paul begins to take the lead narrative role. Prior to Cyprus, Acts consistently said "Barnabas and Saul"; after Paphos, it becomes "Paul and Barnabas" (Acts 13:13). Barnabas reclaims primacy briefly at Lystra but the balance has shifted.

**Departure:** "Paul and his company loosed from Paphos" (Acts 13:13) and sailed north.

---

### NODE 10 — PERGA, PAMPHYLIA

**Location:** Perga, Pamphylia, Asia Minor (modern Murtina near Antalya, Turkey).

**How he got there:** Sailed from Cyprus across approximately 250 km of open sea to the southern coast of Asia Minor. The port of landing was likely Attalia (modern Antalya), then upriver/overland ~15 km to Perga.

**Key event — John Mark deserts:**
Acts 13:13 — "John departing from them returned to Jerusalem." No explanation given. This desertion is the source of the Paul-Barnabas split in Acts 15:36–39. Paul later judges it sufficient grounds to refuse Mark on the second journey. The text is silent on Mark's reasons: illness, homesickness, theological disagreement about Gentile inclusion, or fear of the dangerous inland journey to Pisidian Antioch (the mountain pass through the Taurus range was bandit country).

Paul's illness reference: Galatians 4:13 — "Ye know how through infirmity of the flesh I preached the gospel unto you at the first." Many scholars place this illness at Perga, suggesting malaria from the coastal lowlands drove Paul inland to the higher, healthier elevation of Pisidian Antioch. If correct, the Galatian churches were founded during Paul's illness-driven retreat to altitude — a remarkable design irony.

**What happened at Perga itself:** No preaching recorded. They appear to pass through. On the return journey (Acts 14:25) they do preach here.

**Departure:** North, through the Taurus mountains, approximately 160 km to Pisidian Antioch.

---

### NODE 11 — PISIDIAN ANTIOCH: The Synagogue Sermon

**Location:** Antioch of Pisidia (modern Yalvaç, Isparta province, Turkey). A Roman colony in the Galatian region, seated on a plateau at ~1,200m elevation. Not to be confused with Antioch on the Orontes.

**How he got there:** Overland through Taurus mountain passes. The road was part of the Via Sebaste, a Roman military road.

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified.

**Who he met:**
- **Synagogue rulers** (Acts 13:15) — initially friendly, invite Paul and Barnabas to speak.
- **Jewish audience + God-fearers** (Acts 13:16 — "Men of Israel, and ye that fear God") — God-fearers are Gentiles who attend synagogue and respect Jewish religion without full conversion. They become a key demographic.
- **Hostile Jews** (Acts 13:45) — "when the Jews saw the multitudes, they were filled with envy, and spake against those things which were spoken by Paul, contradicting and blaspheming."
- **Gentile audiences** (Acts 13:48) — respond enthusiastically.

**Key speech — Pisidian Antioch synagogue (Acts 13:16–41):**
This is Paul's most complete synagogue sermon. Its structure:
1. God's choice of Israel and exodus narrative (13:17–19)
2. Judges and kings — Saul, David (13:20–22)
3. John the Baptist as forerunner (13:24–25)
4. Passion and resurrection of Jesus (13:26–31)
5. Fulfillment of Psalm 2:7, Isaiah 55:3, Psalm 16:10 (13:33–37)
6. Announcement of forgiveness of sins through Jesus — "by him all that believe are justified from all things, from which ye could not be justified by the law of Moses" (13:38–39)
7. Warning from Habakkuk 1:5 (13:40–41)

**What happened:**
- Response: "Many of the Jews and religious proselytes followed Paul and Barnabas" (13:43).
- The following Sabbath, "almost the whole city" comes to hear. This scale triggers jealousy.
- Jewish leaders' opposition: Paul and Barnabas issue their programmatic statement: "It was necessary that the word of God should first have been spoken to you: but seeing ye put it from you, and judge yourselves unworthy of everlasting life, lo, we turn to the Gentiles" (13:46). This pattern — synagogue, rejection, pivot to Gentiles — repeats throughout Paul's journeys.
- "The devout and honourable women, and the chief men of the city" are stirred up against Paul (13:50). These are prominent Gentile women associated with the synagogue — their social influence is weaponized.
- Paul and Barnabas are expelled from the district ("expulsion from their coasts" — Acts 13:50).

**Departure:** Expelled. "They shook off the dust of their feet against them, and came unto Iconium" (Acts 13:51) — approximately 90 km east. They depart "filled with joy, and with the Holy Ghost" (13:52).

---

### NODE 12 — ICONIUM

**Location:** Iconium, Phrygia/Galatia (modern Konya, Turkey).

**How he got there:** Overland ~90 km from Pisidian Antioch along the Via Sebaste.

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified.

**Who he met:**
- **"A great multitude both of the Jews and also of the Greeks believed"** (Acts 14:1) — the largest recorded single response on the first journey.
- **Unbelieving Jews** (Acts 14:2) — "stirred up the Gentiles, and made their minds evil affected against the brethren."
- **Mixed city population** — divided: some with Jews, some with apostles (Acts 14:4).
- **Gentile and Jewish rulers who plot stoning** (Acts 14:5).

**What happened:**
- Extended stay (Acts 14:3 — "Long time therefore abode they").
- Signs and wonders performed (Acts 14:3).
- Plot discovered: "an assault made both of the Gentiles, and also of the Jews with their rulers, to use them despitefully, and to stone them" (Acts 14:5).

**Departure:** Fled. "They were ware of it, and fled unto Lystra and Derbe, cities of Lycaonia" (Acts 14:6) — approximately 30 km south to Lystra.

---

### NODE 13 — LYSTRA: Healing, Deification, and Stoning

**Location:** Lystra, Lycaonia (modern Hatunsaray, Konya province, Turkey). A Roman colony.

**How he got there:** Fled overland from Iconium, ~30 km.

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified.

**Who he met:**
- **A lame man** (Acts 14:8–10) — "impotent in his feet, being a cripple from his mother's womb, who never had walked." Paul looks at him "stedfastly" and perceives faith, then heals him.
- **Barnabas = "Jupiter" (Zeus), Paul = "Mercury" (Hermes)** (Acts 14:12) — the crowd interprets the miracle through their local Greek religion. "They called Barnabas, Jupiter; and Paul, Mercurius, because he was the chief speaker." The priest of Zeus comes with garlands and oxen to sacrifice.
- **Timothy's family** — though Timothy is not formally introduced until Acts 16:1, he was almost certainly at Lystra for this visit (Acts 16:1 says he was already "a disciple"). His mother Eunice and grandmother Lois (2 Tim 1:5) are described as women of faith. Timothy is Paul's most lasting companion.
- **Jews from Antioch and Iconium** (Acts 14:19) — pursue Paul to Lystra specifically. This is impressive hostile commitment.

**Key speech — Against sacrifice (Acts 14:15–17):**
Paul and Barnabas tear their clothes (a Jewish sign of horror at blasphemy) and cry out: "Sirs, why do ye these things? We also are men of like passions with you, and preach unto you that ye should turn from these vanities unto the living God, which made heaven, and earth, and the sea, and all things that are therein: Who in times past suffered all nations to walk in their own ways. Nevertheless he left not himself without witness, in that he did good, and gave us rain from heaven, and fruitful seasons, filling our hearts with food and gladness." This speech appeals to natural theology rather than Jewish scripture — calibrated for a fully pagan audience, in contrast to the synagogue approach at Pisidian Antioch.

**Meals:** "Filling our hearts with food and gladness" — the reference to rain, seasons, and food is notable as an agricultural metaphor. No specific meal described.

**What happened:**
- Healing of the lame man (Acts 14:8–10).
- Crowd attempts deification; Paul and Barnabas frantically refuse.
- Jewish agitators arrive from Pisidian Antioch and Iconium — their persistence is notable. "Who persuaded the people, and having stoned Paul, drew him out of the city, supposing he had been dead" (Acts 14:19). Paul is stoned and left for dead.
- "Howbeit, as the disciples stood round about him, he rose up, and came into the city: and the next day he departed with Barnabas to Derbe" (Acts 14:20). 

**Design note:** Paul later refers to this in Galatians 6:17 — "I bear in my body the marks of the Lord Jesus" (the stigmata, plural). He also lists in 2 Cor 11:25 "once was I stoned."

**Departure:** Next day, with Barnabas, to Derbe (~90 km southeast).

---

### NODE 14 — DERBE: The Turnaround

**Location:** Derbe, Lycaonia (modern Kerti Höyük near Karaman, Turkey).

**How he got there:** Overland ~90 km from Lystra.

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified.

**What happened:** "When they had preached the gospel to that city, and had taught many, they returned again to Lystra, and to Iconium, and Antioch" (Acts 14:21). Derbe is the easternmost point. From here they could have taken the easy route home through the Cilician Gates (mountain pass) to Tarsus and down to Antioch on the Orontes — but they deliberately reverse through hostile territory.

**NPC gained here:** Gaius of Derbe (Acts 20:4), later a travel companion on the third journey.

**Departure:** Retraced route west.

---

### NODE 15 — RETURN ROUTE: Lystra, Iconium, Pisidian Antioch, Perga, Attalia

**What happened on the return:**
- Acts 14:21–23: "confirming the souls of the disciples, and exhorting them to continue in the faith, and that we must through much tribulation enter into the kingdom of God."
- They appoint elders in every church (Acts 14:23) — the organizational infrastructure established.
- At Perga, they "preached the word" (Acts 14:25) — making up for the initial pass-through.
- At Attalia (the port, modern Antalya), they sail.

**Departure:** Sailed from Attalia back to Antioch on the Orontes.

---

### NODE 16 — ANTIOCH: Report and Rest

**Location:** Antioch on the Orontes (modern Antakya, Turkey).

**What happened:**
- Acts 14:27 — "And when they were come, and had gathered the church together, they rehearsed all that God had done with them, and how he had opened the door of faith unto the Gentiles."
- "And there they abode long time with the disciples" (Acts 14:28).

**The Antioch Incident (Galatians 2:11–14):**
Galatians records an event Acts omits: Paul confronting Peter (Cephas) publicly in Antioch. Peter had been eating with Gentile believers, but when "certain came from James" (from the Jerusalem church leadership), Peter "withdrew and separated himself, fearing them which were of the circumcision." Barnabas was also "carried away with their dissimulation." Paul "withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed" (Gal 2:11–14). This is the only recorded direct conflict between Paul and Peter. It is the doctrinal core of Galatians' argument about grace vs. law.

---

## NODE 17 — JERUSALEM COUNCIL (~49 CE)

**Location:** Jerusalem (Acts 15:1–29; the parallel Galatians account Gal 2:1–10, though some scholars dispute whether Gal 2 describes this event or the famine visit).

**How he got there:** Acts 15:2 — "Paul and Barnabas, and certain other of them" went up. Galatians 2:1 says Paul went "with Barnabas" and "took Titus also with me." The journey was apparently instigated by a dispute in Antioch: "certain men which came down from Judaea taught the brethren, and said, Except ye be circumcised after the manner of Moses, ye cannot be saved" (Acts 15:1).

**Who he met:**
- **The apostles and elders** — the Jerusalem leadership.
- **James (the Lord's brother)** — delivers the decisive ruling.
- **Peter** — speaks in favor of Gentile inclusion (Acts 15:7–11), citing the Cornelius episode.
- **The Pharisee party within the church** (Acts 15:5) — demands Gentile circumcision.
- **Titus** (Gal 2:3) — Paul brings a Gentile (uncircumcised) companion as a living test case. "But neither Titus, who was with me, being a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised."
- **"False brethren unawares brought in" (Gal 2:4)** — a hostile faction who "came in privily to spy out our liberty... that they might bring us into bondage."

**Key speeches:**
- Peter (Acts 15:7–11): "God made choice among us, that the Gentiles by my mouth should hear the word of the gospel... Now therefore why tempt ye God, to put a yoke upon the neck of the disciples, which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear?"
- James (Acts 15:13–21): The decisive ruling cites Amos 9:11–12, concludes Gentiles should not be troubled with circumcision but should "abstain from pollutions of idols, and from fornication, and from things strangled, and from blood" — the Apostolic Decree.

**The "right hands of fellowship" (Gal 2:9):** James, Peter, and John "gave to me and Barnabas the right hands of fellowship; that we should go unto the heathen, and they unto the circumcision." A formal territorial/demographic division of missionary responsibility.

**Departure conditions:** Positive. A letter is sent with Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:22–29), and two Jerusalem delegates — Judas Barsabas and Silas — accompany them to Antioch to confirm the ruling.

---

## SECOND MISSIONARY JOURNEY (~49–52 CE)

### NODE 18 — ANTIOCH: The Paul-Barnabas Split

**What happened:**
Acts 15:36–41 — Paul proposes revisiting the churches from the first journey. Barnabas wants to bring John Mark. "Paul thought not good to take him with them, who departed from them from Pamphylia, and went not with them to the work" (Acts 15:38). The disagreement is described as "sharp contention" (παροξυσμός, paroxysmos — a medical term for a sharp, acute crisis). Result:
- Barnabas takes Mark and sails to Cyprus.
- Paul chooses Silas (also called Silvanus) as new partner and goes overland through Syria and Cilicia.

This is a permanent partnership breakup. Barnabas disappears from Acts narrative. Paul writes warmly of Barnabas in 1 Corinthians 9:6 but never describes reconciliation. Mark is eventually rehabilitated in Paul's eyes (Col 4:10; 2 Tim 4:11 — "Take Mark, and bring him with thee: for he is profitable to me for the ministry").

**Travel party for 2nd Journey:** Paul + Silas (later adding Timothy at Lystra, and Luke from Troas).

---

### NODE 19 — LYSTRA: Timothy Joins

**Location:** Lystra (same city as NODE 13).

**How he got there:** Overland through Syria, Cilicia, Derbe, then Lystra.

**Who he met:**
- **Timothy** (Acts 16:1–3) — "a certain disciple... the son of a certain woman, which was a Jewess, and believed; but his father was a Greek." Well-regarded by the churches at Lystra and Iconium. Paul wants to take him along.

**Key detail — Circumcision:** Acts 16:3 — "Him would Paul have to go forth with him; and took and circumcised him because of the Jews which were in those quarters: for they all knew that his father was a Greek." This is remarkable given Paul's recent battle over circumcision at Jerusalem. The justification is pragmatic: Timothy's Jewish mother made him technically Jewish, but his uncircumcised status would undermine credibility in synagogues. This is circumcision as social strategy, not theological requirement.

**Departure:** Northwest, attempting various routes.

---

### NODE 20 — THE PHRYGIO-GALATIAN WANDERING

**Location:** Through Phrygia and the region of Galatia (Acts 16:6).

**What happened:** Paul and companions are "forbidden of the Holy Ghost to preach the word in Asia" (the Roman province of Asia, western Turkey). They attempt to go north into Bithynia — "the Spirit suffered them not" (Acts 16:7). They end up at Troas by a process of elimination.

This navigation-by-exclusion is significant. It places them at Troas with no obvious plan — until the vision.

---

### NODE 21 — TROAS: The Macedonian Vision

**Location:** Alexandria Troas, Mysia (modern Dalyan near Çanakkale, Turkey). The port of northwest Asia Minor, near ancient Troy.

**What happened:**
Acts 16:9–10 — "And a vision appeared to Paul in the night; There stood a man of Macedonia, and prayed him, saying, Come over into Macedonia, and help us. And after he had seen the vision, immediately we endeavoured to go into Macedonia, assuredly gathering that the Lord had called us for to help them."

**KEY NARRATIVE SHIFT — The "we" passages begin here:** Acts 16:10 switches from "they" to "we" — the narrator has joined the party. This is almost universally taken as a signal that Luke (the author-physician) joined the group at Troas. Luke will appear and disappear throughout Acts based on these "we" passages.

**Departure:** Sailed from Troas to Samothrace (an island, overnight anchorage), then to Neapolis (the Macedonian port, modern Kavala, Greece), then overland to Philippi.

---

### NODE 22 — PHILIPPI: Lydia, Prison, and Earthquake

**Location:** Philippi, Macedonia (modern Filippoi, Eastern Macedonia and Thrace, Greece). A Roman colony, the "first city" of the Macedonian district (Acts 16:12).

**How he got there:** Sailed Troas → Samothrace → Neapolis (port), then ~15 km inland to Philippi.

**Where he stayed:**
- Initial prayer meeting at a riverside (Acts 16:13) — a "place of prayer" (προσευχή, proseuche), likely a Jewish prayer site outside the city gate.
- **Lydia's house** (Acts 16:14–15, 40) — "And when she was baptized, and her household, she besought us, saying, If ye have judged me to be faithful to the Lord, come into my house, and abide there. And she constrained us." After the prison incident, they return to Lydia's house.

**Who he met:**
- **Lydia of Thyatira** (Acts 16:14–15) — "a seller of purple, of the city of Thyatira." Thyatira (modern Akhisar, Turkey) was famous for purple dye production. She is a businesswoman, evidently wealthy (can host a missionary team), a "worshipper of God" (God-fearer). "Whose heart the Lord opened, that she attended unto the things which were spoken of Paul." First European convert. Friendly.
- **A slave girl with a spirit of divination** (Acts 16:16–18) — she has a πνεῦμα πύθωνα (spirit of a Python, i.e., oracular spirit), which she uses to earn money for her owners. She follows Paul for "many days" crying "These men are the servants of the most high God, which shew unto us the way of salvation." Paul exorcises the spirit.
- **The slave girl's owners** (Acts 16:19) — economically damaged by the exorcism. They drag Paul and Silas to the magistrates.
- **The Philippian magistrates (duumviri)** — condemn Paul and Silas without trial to be beaten and imprisoned.
- **The jailer** (Acts 16:27–33) — wakes to find prison doors open, assumes escape, draws sword to kill himself (standard Roman penalty for losing prisoners). Paul stops him. He is converted and baptizes his household. He "washed their stripes" and "brought them up into his house, and set meat before them" (16:34). Friendly.

**Key events:**
- Lydia's conversion (16:14–15).
- Exorcism of slave girl (16:18).
- Arrest: Paul and Silas "torn off their clothes" (16:22, implying public humiliation) and beaten with rods (the Roman fustigatio). This is the first documented instance of this punishment — 2 Cor 11:25 says "thrice was I beaten with rods."
- Imprisonment in the "inner prison" with feet in stocks (16:24).
- At midnight, Paul and Silas sing hymns (16:25). Earthquake opens all doors and loosens all chains (16:26).
- Jailer's conversion (16:27–34).

**KEY LEGAL MOMENT:** Acts 16:37 — "They have beaten us openly uncondemned, being Romans, and have cast us into prison; and now do they thrust us out privily? nay verily; but let them come themselves and fetch us out." Paul invokes Roman citizenship only after the fact — deliberately, to establish legal protection for the Philippian church after he leaves. The magistrates are alarmed and come personally to release them.

**Meals:** The jailer "set meat before them" (Acts 16:34) — a meal shared at midnight after the earthquake and conversion. One of the few specific meal references.

**Departure:** Released by magistrates, visited Lydia's house, then departed.

**Letters written from/to Philippi:** Philippians — written during imprisonment, most likely Rome (~60–62 CE). Philippians 4:15–16 references Philippi's support: "Now ye Philippians know also, that in the beginning of the gospel, when I departed from Macedonia, no church communicated with me as concerning giving and receiving, but ye only."

---

### NODE 23 — THESSALONICA

**Location:** Thessalonica, Macedonia (modern Thessaloniki, Greece). The largest city in Macedonia, the Roman provincial capital.

**How he got there:** Via the Via Egnatia, the main Roman road westward. Through Amphipolis and Apollonia (Acts 17:1) — approximately 160 km from Philippi.

**Where he stayed:**
- **Jason's house** (Acts 17:5–7) — "And they drew Jason out of his house, and sought to bring them out to the people." Jason is identified as Saul's host; the mob comes to his house looking for Paul. He is dragged before city authorities and forced to post bond.

**Duration:** Three Sabbaths in the synagogue (Acts 17:2). However, Philippians 4:16 says "even in Thessalonica ye sent once and again unto my necessity" — suggesting a longer stay than three weeks, since support reached him multiple times.

**Who he met:**
- **Synagogue members** (Acts 17:2–4) — "some of them believed, and consorted with Paul and Silas; and of the devout Greeks a great multitude, and of the chief women not a few."
- **Jason** — host, dragged before authorities. Friendly.
- **Hostile Jews** (Acts 17:5) — "moved with envy, took unto them certain lewd fellows of the baser sort, and gathered a company, and set all the city on an uproar." The charge before city authorities is political: "These that have turned the world upside down are come hither also... saying that there is another king, one Jesus" (17:6–7). This is the Roman political charge — treason against Caesar's claim to be the universal king.

**What happened:**
- Paul's synagogue approach: "three sabbath days reasoned with them out of the scriptures, opening and alleging, that Christ must needs have suffered, and risen again from the dead" (Acts 17:3).
- Riot organized by hostile Jews.
- Jason arraigned before the politarchs (Acts 17:6 uses πολιτάρχης — a title confirmed by inscriptions found at Thessalonica, validating Luke's accuracy).
- Jason posts bond for Paul's departure.

**Letters written from Thessalonica:** None — but 1 and 2 Thessalonians are written TO Thessalonica, from Corinth (~50–51 CE), shortly after Paul's departure. They address the community's questions about the Second Coming and deceased members.

**Departure:** Night departure (Acts 17:10). Sent away "by night" to Berea.

---

### NODE 24 — BEREA

**Location:** Berea, Macedonia (modern Veria, Greece). ~70 km southwest of Thessalonica.

**How he got there:** Night travel from Thessalonica.

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified.

**Who he met:**
- **The Berean Jews** (Acts 17:11) — described in notably positive terms: "These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so." Many believed, including "honourable women which were Greeks, and of men, not a few."
- **Sopater son of Pyrrhus** (Acts 20:4) — from Berea, later a travel companion.
- **Hostile Thessalonian Jews** (Acts 17:13) — follow Paul to Berea and "stirred up the people."

**Departure:** The Berean Christians "immediately sent away Paul to go as it were to the sea" (Acts 17:14) — Silas and Timothy stay at Berea while Paul goes ahead, escorted by Berean believers, to Athens.

---

### NODE 25 — ATHENS: The Areopagus

**Location:** Athens, Achaia (modern Athens, Greece). The cultural and intellectual capital of the ancient world.

**How he got there:** By sea, escorted by Berean brothers from Berea to Athens. Silas and Timothy are left behind with instructions to "come to him with all speed" (Acts 17:15).

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified.

**Who he met:**
- **Epicurean and Stoic philosophers** (Acts 17:18) — they debate Paul in the agora. They call him a σπερμολόγος (spermologos — "babbler," literally "seed-picker," a term for someone who cobbles together scraps of ideas).
- **The Areopagus council** (Acts 17:19) — not necessarily a formal judicial proceeding; the Areopagus was a philosophical and cultural oversight body. They invite Paul to explain his teaching.
- **Dionysius the Areopagite** (Acts 17:34) — a member of the Areopagus council who converts. Friendly.
- **Damaris** (Acts 17:34) — a woman who converts. No further detail given.
- **"Others with them"** — a small group of converts. Athens yields fewer converts than almost anywhere else in Acts.

**What happened:**
Acts 17:16 — "Now while Paul waited for them at Athens, his spirit was stirred in him, when he saw the city wholly given to idolatry." He reasons in the synagogue AND "in the market daily with them that met with him" — a new venue for Paul.

**Key Speech — The Areopagus Address (Acts 17:22–31):**
The most sophisticated speech in Acts, calibrated entirely for Greek philosophical audiences:

1. Opening: "Ye men of Athens, I perceive that in all things ye are too superstitious [very religious]" (17:22).
2. The altar to the Unknown God: "Whom therefore ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you" (17:23).
3. Monotheism in philosophical terms: God as creator who "dwelleth not in temples made with hands; Neither is worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed any thing" (17:24–25).
4. Human unity and searching: "He hath made of one blood all nations of men... that they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him" (17:26–27).
5. Greek citations: "For in him we live, and move, and have our being; as certain also of your own poets have said, For we are also his offspring" (17:28) — quoting Aratus's Phaenomena and/or Epimenides' Cretica. This is Paul quoting pagan Greek poets approvingly to make his argument.
6. Call to repentance and judgment through "a man whom he hath ordained; whereof he hath given assurance unto all men, in that he hath raised him from the dead" (17:31).

**Response to the Resurrection:** "Some mocked: and others said, We will hear thee again of this matter" (17:32). The resurrection was the conceptual obstacle for Greek philosophical audiences — it contradicted the Greek ideal of escaping the body.

**Design note:** Paul does NOT quote Hebrew scripture once in this speech. He uses Greek literature and natural theology instead. This is his most culturally adaptive performance.

**Departure:** Left for Corinth. Silas and Timothy eventually join him there (Acts 18:5, though 1 Thess 3:1–2 suggests Timothy came briefly to Athens and was sent from there to Thessalonica).

---

### NODE 26 — CORINTH: Eighteen Months

**Location:** Corinth, Achaia (modern Corinth, Greece). A major Roman colony, newly rebuilt by Julius Caesar in 44 BCE, the commercial capital of Greece.

**How he got there:** From Athens, approximately 80 km west overland or by coastal boat.

**Duration:** 18 months (Acts 18:11 — "he continued there a year and six months").

**Where he stayed:**
- **Aquila and Priscilla's house** (Acts 18:2–3) — "And found a certain Jew named Aquila, born in Pontus, lately come from Italy, with his wife Priscilla; (because that Claudius had commanded all Jews to depart from Rome:) and came unto them." This expulsion of Jews from Rome is referenced by the Roman historian Suetonius (Life of Claudius 25.4 — "since the Jews constantly made disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus"), typically dated to 49 CE.

**Occupation:** Acts 18:3 — "because he was of the same craft, he abode with them, and wrought: for by their occupation they were tentmakers." The most explicit statement of Paul's trade. He works with Aquila and Priscilla (both tentmakers/leather workers). This is Paul's deliberate choice to be financially self-supporting rather than dependent on his converts — a policy he defends in 1 Corinthians 9 and 2 Corinthians 11:7–9.

Priscilla and Aquila become his most long-term collaborators. They travel with him to Ephesus, remain there, return eventually to Rome (Romans 16:3). They are named in three Pauline letters (Rom 16:3, 1 Cor 16:19, 2 Tim 4:19) and in Acts three more times. Notably, Priscilla is named first in four of these six references — an unusual convention suggesting she held higher social or ecclesiastical status than her husband.

**Who he met:**
- **Aquila and Priscilla** — hosts, work colleagues, fellow missionaries. Friendly.
- **Crispus** (Acts 18:8) — the ruler of the synagogue who "believed on the Lord with all his house." Paul personally baptized him (1 Cor 1:14). Friendly.
- **Gaius** (1 Cor 1:14) — personally baptized by Paul; later hosts the entire Roman church (Rom 16:23). Friendly.
- **Sosthenes** (Acts 18:17; 1 Cor 1:1) — initially the synagogue ruler after Crispus; is beaten before the judgment seat. Later appears as co-author of 1 Corinthians, suggesting eventual conversion.
- **Gallio** (Acts 18:12–17) — Lucius Junius Gallio Annaeanus, proconsul of Achaia, brother of Seneca the philosopher. An inscription at Delphi dates his proconsulship to ~51–52 CE, which is the single most important chronological anchor point for all of Pauline chronology.

**What happened:**
- Standard opening: synagogue reasoning (Acts 18:4).
- Silas and Timothy arrive from Macedonia (Acts 18:5) — "Paul was pressed in the spirit, and testified to the Jews that Jesus was Christ."
- Jewish opposition: Paul "shook his raiment, and said unto them, Your blood be upon your own heads; I am clean: from henceforth I will go unto the Gentiles" (Acts 18:6).
- Paul moves next door to the synagogue — to the house of one Justus (Acts 18:7), a God-fearer.
- Vision: "Be not afraid, but speak, and hold not thy peace: For I am with thee, and no man shall set on thee to hurt thee: for I have much people in this city" (Acts 18:9–10).
- Crispus the synagogue ruler converts (Acts 18:8).
- Jewish prosecution before Gallio: "This fellow persuadeth men to worship God contrary to the law" (Acts 18:13). Gallio dismisses it: "If it were a matter of wrong or wicked lewdness, O ye Jews, reason would that I should bear with you: But if it be a question of words and names, and of your law, look ye to it; for I will be no judge of such matters" (Acts 18:14–15). This refusal to adjudicate is legally significant — it establishes a de facto precedent that Christianity is a Jewish internal matter, not a Roman criminal concern.
- Sosthenes beaten before the judgment seat by the crowd; Gallio ignores it (Acts 18:17).

**Letters written from Corinth:** 1 Thessalonians (the earliest Pauline letter, ~50–51 CE) and 2 Thessalonians (if authentic, also ~51 CE). Also possibly Galatians (though many place Galatians earlier, from Antioch, or later from Ephesus — disputed).

**Departure:** After 18 months, Paul sailed for Syria with Priscilla and Aquila. He had cut his hair at Cenchreae (Acts 18:18 — "he had a vow") — the Nazirite vow, completed at Jerusalem.

---

### NODE 27 — EPHESUS (BRIEF STOP), CAESAREA, ANTIOCH

**Ephesus (first visit):** Acts 18:19–21 — Brief stop. Paul enters the synagogue and reasons with the Jews. They ask him to stay longer; he declines, saying "I must by all means keep this feast that cometh in Jerusalem: but I will return again unto you, if God will." He leaves Priscilla and Aquila at Ephesus — planting them as agents.

**Caesarea:** Acts 18:22 — "when he had landed at Caesarea, and gone up, and saluted the church." "Gone up" likely means going up to Jerusalem briefly. Ambiguous.

**Antioch:** Acts 18:22 — "he went down to Antioch." A period of time there before the third journey begins (Acts 18:23).

---

## THIRD MISSIONARY JOURNEY (~53–57 CE)

### NODE 28 — EPHESUS: Three Years (The Headquarters)

**Location:** Ephesus, Asia Minor (modern Selçuk/Efes, İzmir province, Turkey). The Roman capital of the province of Asia, one of the largest cities in the empire, home to the Temple of Artemis (one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World).

**How he got there:** Acts 18:23–19:1 — after Antioch, he traveled through Galatia and Phrygia (second pass) and arrived at Ephesus overland.

**Duration:** Acts 19:8 — three months in the synagogue; Acts 19:10 — "two years" in the Hall of Tyrannus; Acts 20:31 — "three years" total ("night and day... with tears").

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified. He worked (tentmaking) while in Ephesus — 1 Cor 4:12, Acts 20:34 ("these hands have ministered unto my necessities").

**Who he met:**
- **Apollos** (Acts 18:24–28 — predates Paul's Ephesus stay, but relevant) — "a certain Jew named Apollos, born at Alexandria, an eloquent man, and mighty in the scriptures: This man was instructed in the way of the Lord; and being fervent in the spirit, he spake and taught diligently the things of the Lord, knowing only the baptism of John." Priscilla and Aquila "expounded unto him the way of God more perfectly" and sent him to Achaia with letters. He became a major teacher at Corinth (1 Cor 1:12, 3:4–6). Friendly to Paul but independently significant.
- **"Twelve disciples"** (Acts 19:1–7) — men who had received only John's baptism. Paul questions them, rebaptizes them, lays hands on them, they speak in tongues and prophesy.
- **Sceva's seven sons** (Acts 19:14–16) — Jewish exorcists who attempt to use Jesus's and Paul's name. The evil spirit answers: "Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye?" The demonized man attacks all seven, strips them naked, and they flee. Memorable scene.
- **Demetrius the silversmith** (Acts 19:24) — craftsman who makes silver shrines of Artemis. Ringleader of the riot. Economically motivated hostility: "This Paul hath persuaded and turned away much people, saying that they be no gods, which are made with hands: So that not only this our craft is in danger... but also that the temple of the great goddess Diana should be despised" (19:26–27). Hostile.
- **Gaius and Aristarchus** (Acts 19:29) — Paul's travel companions, seized and dragged into the theater.
- **Alexander** (Acts 19:33) — put forward by the Jews to address the crowd; the crowd shouts him down, "for about the space of two hours" chanting "Great is Diana of the Ephesians."
- **The town clerk (grammateus)** (Acts 19:35–40) — calms the crowd with careful legal argument, disperses them. The unsung hero of the Ephesian riot.
- **Timothy** (Acts 19:22) — sent ahead to Macedonia.
- **Erastus** (Acts 19:22) — sent to Macedonia with Timothy. Named again in Romans 16:23 as "the chamberlain [city treasurer] of the city" — an inscription found at Corinth mentions an Erastus who paved a street at his own expense, possibly the same man.

**Key events:**
- Hall of Tyrannus lectures: Paul teaches daily "in the school of one Tyrannus" (Acts 19:9). The Western text adds "from the fifth to the tenth hour" — i.e., 11 AM to 4 PM, the midday break hours when the hall would be vacant. Five hours of daily teaching, two years: "so that all they which dwelt in Asia heard the word of the Lord Jesus, both Jews and Greeks" (19:10). This is the intellectual center of Paul's ministry.
- Extraordinary miracles: "handkerchiefs or aprons" from Paul's body carried to the sick (Acts 19:12).
- Sceva incident: mass fear; "the name of the Lord Jesus was magnified" (Acts 19:17).
- Book burning: "Many of them also which used curious arts brought their books together, and burned them before all men: and they counted the price of them, and found it fifty thousand pieces of silver" (Acts 19:19). 50,000 drachmas — approximately 50,000 days of wages for a laborer. One of the most specific financial details in Acts.
- Riot of Artemis silversmiths (Acts 19:23–41).

**The "Thorn in the Flesh" context (2 Cor 12:7):** As noted, the thorn likely predates Ephesus (it follows the third-heaven vision dated ~43 CE). But Paul's sufferings at Ephesus may explain his language in 2 Corinthians 1:8–9: "we were pressed out of measure, above strength, insomuch that we despaired even of life... we had the sentence of death in ourselves." Some scholars identify an Ephesian imprisonment (not in Acts) based on Philippians 1:13 and the timing, since Philippians is often dated to an Ephesian imprisonment rather than Rome.

**Letters written from Ephesus:**
- **1 Corinthians** — written from Ephesus (~55 CE, Acts 19:8–10 period). 1 Cor 16:8: "I will tarry at Ephesus until Pentecost."
- **The "Previous Letter"** — referenced in 1 Cor 5:9 ("I wrote unto you in an epistle not to company with fornicators"), a lost letter to Corinth written before 1 Corinthians.
- **Galatians** — possibly written from Ephesus (if Galatia = ethnic Galatia, north, second visit).
- **Philippians** — possibly written from Ephesus if the "Ephesian imprisonment" hypothesis is accepted.
- **Philemon** — if written during an Ephesian imprisonment.

**Departure:** After the riot subsides, Paul departs for Macedonia (Acts 20:1).

---

### NODE 29 — TROAS: Eutychus Falls from the Window

**Location:** Alexandria Troas (same as NODE 21, modern Dalyan, Turkey).

**How he got there:** Via Macedonia and Greece; returned to Asia via this route. Acts 20:6 — "we sailed away from Philippi after the days of unleavened bread, and came unto them to Troas in five days; where we abode seven days."

**Where he stayed:** Lodging unspecified. The meeting is in an upper room (ὑπερῷον, hyperoon) — Acts 20:8 — lit by "many lights."

**Who he met:**
- **Eutychus** (Acts 20:9) — "a certain young man named Eutychus, being fallen into a deep sleep: and as Paul was long preaching, he sunk down with sleep, and fell down from the third loft, and was taken up dead."

**What happened:**
Acts 20:7–12:
- "Upon the first day of the week, when the disciples came together to break bread, Paul preached unto them" — breaking of bread (Eucharist or communal meal) mentioned explicitly.
- Paul preaches "till midnight" (Acts 20:7). Then "ready to depart on the morrow" he "continued his speech until midnight" — the occasion of Eutychus's fatal sleep.
- Eutychus falls from the third-story window. Paul goes down, "fell on him, and embracing him said, Trouble not yourselves; for his life is in him" (Acts 20:10). Eutychus is restored.
- "And they brought the young man alive, and were not a little comforted" (Acts 20:12).
- "When he therefore was come up again, and had broken bread, and eaten, and talked a long while, even till break of day" — Paul continues through the night. Meal after the miracle.

**Meals:** The "breaking of bread" before and after the Eutychus incident — one of the clearest meal references.

**Departure:** The "we" party sailed; Paul went by foot to Assos (Acts 20:13 — a notable detail; Paul traveled alone overland about 30 km while the ship went around the cape).

### NODE 30 — THE FAREWELL CIRCUIT: Assos, Mitylene, Chios, Samos, Miletus

Acts 20:13–15 — a careful itinerary:
- **Assos** — Paul boards the ship (Acts 20:14)
- **Mitylene** (island of Lesbos, Greece) — overnight stop
- **Chios** (island) — anchored offshore
- **Samos** (island, Greece) — brief stop at Trogyllium (Western text)
- **Miletus** — main stop; Paul "had determined to sail by Ephesus, because he would not spend the time in Asia: for he hasted, if it were possible for him, to be at Jerusalem the day of Pentecost" (Acts 20:16).

### NODE 31 — MILETUS: Farewell to the Ephesian Elders

**Location:** Miletus, Asia Minor (modern Balat, Aydın province, Turkey). Ancient port city ~50 km south of Ephesus.

**What happened:**
Paul sends to Ephesus for the elders (πρεσβύτεροι) of the church to come to Miletus — a deliberate decision not to return to Ephesus, both for time reasons and perhaps because of the riot's aftermath.

**Key Speech — The Miletus Farewell (Acts 20:18–35):**
This is the only speech in Acts addressed to a Christian audience (all others are evangelistic). It is Paul's most personal and emotional recorded speech.

Key themes and direct quotes:
- "Ye know, from the first day that I came into Asia, after what manner I have been with you at all seasons, Serving the Lord with all humility of mind, and with many tears, and temptations" (20:18–19).
- "I kept back nothing that was profitable unto you, but have shewed you, and have taught you publickly, and from house to house" (20:20).
- "And now, behold, I go bound in the spirit unto Jerusalem, not knowing the things that shall befall me there: Save that the Holy Ghost witnesseth in every city, saying that bonds and afflictions abide me" (20:22–23).
- "But none of these things move me, neither count I my life dear unto myself" (20:24).
- "Take heed therefore unto yourselves, and to all the flock... For I know this, that after my departing shall grievous wolves enter in among you, not sparing the flock" (20:28–29). Warning of coming false teachers.
- "I have coveted no man's silver, or gold, or apparel. Yea, ye yourselves know, that these hands have ministered unto my necessities, and to them that were with me" (20:33–34). Explicitly references tent-making.
- Unique citation: "It is more blessed to give than to receive" (20:35) — attributed to Jesus, but not found in any gospel. This is the one place in Acts where Paul quotes a Jesus saying not in the gospels.

**Response:** Acts 20:37–38 — "And they all wept sore, and fell on Paul's neck, and kissed him, Sorrowing most of all for the words which he spake, that they should see his face no more."

**Departure:** They accompany him to the ship.

### NODE 32 — THE JOURNEY TO JERUSALEM: Cos, Rhodes, Patara, Tyre, Ptolemais, Caesarea

**Itinerary (Acts 21:1–8):**
- **Cos** (island, Greece) — overnight stop
- **Rhodes** (island, Greece) — overnight stop
- **Patara** (and possibly Myra — Western text) — Lycia, Turkey; they change to a larger, direct ship
- **Cyprus** — "sailed under Cyprus, because the winds were contrary, and sailed into Syria" (Acts 21:3) — passing Cyprus to the south
- **Tyre** — "landed at Tyre: for there the ship was to unlade her burden" (Acts 21:3). Seven days with disciples. The disciples "through the Spirit" urge Paul not to go to Jerusalem (21:4). Farewell on the beach: "they all brought us on our way, with wives and children, till we were out of the city: and we kneeled down on the shore, and prayed" (21:5). Memorable scene.
- **Ptolemais** — one day with brothers
- **Caesarea Maritima** — significant stop

**Caesarea stop:**
- **Where he stayed: Philip the Evangelist's house** (Acts 21:8) — "one of the seven" (original deacons), who had four daughters "which did prophesy" (21:9). "Many days" stay.
- **Agabus appears again** (Acts 21:10–11) — the prophet from Jerusalem (first seen predicting the famine, Acts 11:28). He "took Paul's girdle, and bound his own hands and feet" and prophesied: "So shall the Jews at Jerusalem bind the man that owneth this girdle, and shall deliver him into the hands of the Gentiles." 
- Paul's response (Acts 21:13): "What mean ye to weep and to break mine heart? for I am ready not to be bound only, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus."

**Jerusalem via Caesarea:** Acts 21:15–16 — "And after those days we took up our carriages, and went up to Jerusalem. There went with us also certain of the disciples of Caesarea, and brought with them one **Mnason of Cyprus**, an old disciple, with whom we should lodge."

**Where he stayed in Jerusalem: Mnason's house** — Acts 21:16. Mnason is specifically named as host. He is described as ἀρχαίῳ μαθητῇ ("an old [original/early] disciple") — suggesting he may have known Jesus or been a very early convert.

## NODE 33 — JERUSALEM: ARREST

**Location:** Jerusalem, Judea (modern East Jerusalem).

**What happened:**
- Paul meets James and the Jerusalem elders (Acts 21:18–25). They report that Jewish Christians have been told Paul teaches Jews to abandon Moses. They ask Paul to sponsor four men taking a Nazirite vow — public demonstration of Torah observance (Acts 21:23–24).
- Paul agrees and goes to the Temple (Acts 21:26).
- On the seventh day: "the Jews which were of Asia, when they saw him in the temple, stirred up all the people, and laid hands on him, Crying out, Men of Israel, help: This is the man, that teacheth all men every where against the people, and the law, and this place: and further brought Greeks also into the temple, and hath polluted this holy place" (Acts 21:27–28). The charge of bringing Gentiles past the Court of Gentiles (the soreg barrier, punishable by death even for Romans) was specifically prompted by seeing Trophimus the Ephesian in the city (Acts 21:29) — mistakenly assumed to have entered.
- Riot. Paul is dragged from the Temple, beaten. The gates are shut.
- The Roman tribune (chiliarchos) Claudius Lysias arrives with soldiers (Acts 21:31–33). Paul is arrested and put in chains.
- Paul requests to speak to the crowd (Acts 21:37–39). He addresses the tribune in Greek — "Canst thou speak Greek?" (21:37). Paul identifies himself: "I am a man which am a Jew of Tarsus, a city in Cilicia, a citizen of no mean city" (21:39).

---

### ADDRESS FROM THE STEPS (Acts 22:1–21):
Paul addresses the crowd in Hebrew/Aramaic — a calculated move that "kept the more silence" (22:2). He recounts his conversion narrative (second version). The crowd listens until he reaches: "I will send thee far hence unto the Gentiles" (22:21). At "Gentiles" — riot resumes.

**Roman citizenship invoked (Acts 22:25–29):** The tribune prepares to flog him. Paul: "Is it lawful for you to scourge a man that is a Roman, and uncondemned?" The centurion reports to the tribune; the tribune comes to Paul: "Tell me, art thou a Roman?" The tribune reveals he bought his citizenship; Paul: "But I was free born" (22:28). The flogging stops. The tribune is "afraid, after he knew that he was a Roman, and because he had bound him" — arresting a Roman citizen in chains was itself potentially illegal.

---

### BEFORE THE SANHEDRIN (Acts 22:30–23:10):
Paul's appearance before the Sanhedrin. He begins: "Men and brethren, I have lived in all good conscience before God until this day." He is immediately struck on the mouth — Paul's explosive response: "God shall smite thee, thou whited wall: for sittest thou to judge me after the law, and commandest me to be smitten contrary to the law?" Told he is speaking to the High Priest, Paul backs down with a scripture citation (23:5).

**Strategic split:** Paul cries out: "I am a Pharisee, the son of a Pharisee: of the hope and resurrection of the dead I am called in question" (23:6). This fractures the Sanhedrin — Pharisees and Sadducees into factional conflict over resurrection theology. Paul is escorted out before he can be torn apart.

**The nephew:** Acts 23:16 — "And when Paul's sister's son heard of their lying in wait, he came and entered into the castle, and told Paul." The nephew reports a plot: 40 men have "bound themselves under a curse, saying that they will neither eat nor drink till they have killed Paul" (23:12). Paul sends his nephew to the tribune.

**Transfer to Caesarea:** Lysias sends Paul to Felix the governor in Caesarea with an escort of 470 military personnel (200 soldiers, 200 spearmen, 70 horsemen — Acts 23:23). A letter from Lysias to Felix is reproduced in Acts 23:26–30. Paul departs at night.

## NODE 34 — CAESAREA: TWO YEARS BEFORE FELIX

**Location:** Caesarea Maritima, Judea (modern Caesarea, Israel).

**Duration:** Two years (Acts 24:27).

**Where he stayed:** Herod's Praetorium — the Roman administrative palace/prison (Acts 23:35).

**Who he met:**
- **Felix (Marcus Antonius Felix)** (Acts 24:1–27) — procurator of Judea, ~52–60 CE. Former slave, freedman of Empress Antonia, brother of Narcissus (Claudius's powerful secretary). Tacitus describes him as exercising "the power of a king with the mind of a slave" (Annals 12.54). He "had a more exact knowledge of that way" (24:22) — aware of Christianity, possibly through his wife Drusilla. He keeps Paul imprisoned but treats him relatively well — "commanded a centurion to keep Paul, and to let him have liberty, and that he should forbid none of his acquaintance to minister or come unto him" (24:23).
- **Drusilla** (Acts 24:24) — Felix's wife, "which was a Jewess." She is the daughter of Herod Agrippa I, great-granddaughter of Herod the Great. Felix apparently desires to hear Paul directly.
- **Ananias the High Priest + Tertullus the orator** (Acts 24:1) — come from Jerusalem to prosecute Paul. Tertullus is a professional rhetorician. Hostile.
- **Festus (Porcius Festus)** (Acts 24:27; 25:1) — succeeds Felix, ~60 CE. More scrupulous and efficient than Felix.
- **Agrippa II and Bernice** (Acts 25:13) — Herod Agrippa II, king of Chalcis/territories, and his sister Bernice (their relationship was rumored incestuous — Juvenal, Suetonius). They visit Festus. Agrippa is knowledgeable about Jewish affairs and wants to hear Paul.

**Key conversations:**
- Paul's defense before Felix (Acts 24:10–21): Denies accusations, admits following "the Way," emphasizes resurrection as the core issue.
- Paul before Felix and Drusilla (Acts 24:25): "As he reasoned of righteousness, temperance, and judgment to come, Felix trembled, and answered, Go thy way for this time; when I have a convenient season, I will call for thee." Felix then hopes Paul will offer a bribe (Acts 24:26).
- Paul before Festus (Acts 25:6–12): Festus proposes sending Paul to Jerusalem (political appeasement of the Jewish leadership). Paul invokes his right of appeal: "I appeal unto Caesar" (25:11). The Greek is παρακαλῶ Καίσαρα — the formal legal formula. Festus: "Hast thou appealed unto Caesar? unto Caesar shalt thou go" (25:12). Appeal to Caesar was an absolute right of Roman citizens.

**Paul before Agrippa (Acts 26:1–29):**
Festus wants Agrippa to help formulate the charges for the imperial referral — he cannot send Paul to Caesar without specifying the crime. Paul's speech to Agrippa is the third conversion account in Acts and the most rhetorically polished. It includes additional details not in Acts 9 or 22. Agrippa's response (Acts 26:28): "Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian." Paul: "I would to God, that not only thou, but also all that hear me this day, were both almost, and altogether such as I am, except these bonds" (26:29).

Agrippa to Festus afterward (Acts 26:31–32): "This man doeth nothing worthy of death or of bonds. Then said Agrippa unto Festus, This man might have been set at liberty, if he had not appealed unto Caesar." The irony: the appeal that keeps him imprisoned also keeps him alive.

## NODE 35 — SEA VOYAGE AND SHIPWRECK (Acts 27)

**This is the most detailed travel narrative in all of ancient literature.** Acts 27 is remarkable for its nautical precision — scholars use it as a primary source for understanding ancient Mediterranean sailing.

### Embarkation

**Location:** Caesarea Maritima.

**Travel party:**
- Paul
- Aristarchus of Thessalonica (Acts 27:2 — "there was with us one Aristarchus, a Macedonian of Thessalonica")
- Luke (implied by "we")
- Julius the centurion of the "Augustan cohort" (Σπείρης Σεβαστῆς — Acts 27:1)
- Other prisoners (Acts 27:1)
- A crew and other passengers

**Ship #1:** An Adramyttene ship (from Adramyttium, modern Edremit, Turkey) — heading along the coast of Asia (Acts 27:2).

### Sidon

Acts 27:3 — "And the next day we touched at Sidon." Julius "courteously entreated Paul, and gave him liberty to go unto his friends to refresh himself" — Paul visits the Christian community at Sidon. This is a significant note: Paul is an escorted prisoner but granted significant freedom.

### Cyprus and Cilicia

The winds are against them — they sail under the lee (shelter) of Cyprus on its north coast, then "over the sea of Cilicia and Pamphylia" (Acts 27:5). Standard summer sailing routes.

### Myra, Lycia

**Ship #2:** At Myra (modern Demre, Turkey), they find "a ship of Alexandria sailing into Italy" (Acts 27:6) — an Alexandrian grain freighter. These ships were among the largest merchant vessels of the ancient world, carrying grain from Egypt to Rome. They transfer to this ship.

### Cnidus, Crete (Fair Havens)

"And when we had sailed slowly many days, and scarce were come over against Cnidus, the wind not suffering us, we sailed under Crete, over against Salmone; And, hardly passing it, came unto a place which is called The fair havens" (Acts 27:7–8). 

**Fair Havens** (Καλοὶ Λιμένες) is a real harbor on Crete's south coast, still known by this name.

**The Council of War (Acts 27:9–12):**
"Now when much time was spent, and when sailing was now dangerous, because the fast was now also over [the Day of Atonement / Yom Kippur — late September/early October], Paul admonished them, And said unto them, Sirs, I perceive that this voyage will be with hurt and much damage, not only of the lading and ship, but also of our lives" (27:9–10). 

Paul's advice: stay at Fair Havens. The centurion follows the pilot and owner over Paul's counsel. The majority vote to try to reach Phoenix — a better harbor on Crete's southwest coast for wintering.

### The Storm — Euroclydon

Acts 27:14: "But not long after there arose against it a tempestuous wind, called Euroclydon." The Εὐρακύλων — a violent northeastern wind, NE by E according to the Greek term (Eurus = east, aquilo = north). Modern meteorologists identify this as a gregale — a violent northeastern Mediterranean storm.

**Storm mechanics (Acts 27:16–38) — detailed nautical sequence:**
1. Running under the lee of the small island Cauda/Clauda (modern Gavdos, southernmost Greek territory).
2. "We had much work to come by the boat" (27:16) — the dinghy being towed had filled with water; they haul it aboard with difficulty.
3. "Used helps, undergirding the ship" (27:17) — passing cables under the hull (frapping/undergirding) to hold the hull together under wave stress.
4. "Fearing lest they should fall into the quicksands [Syrtis Major/Minor]" — the North African shoals off Libya — they "strake sail" (lowered the mainsail) and ran before the wind.
5. Day two: cargo jettisoned (Acts 27:18).
6. Day three: ship's tackle thrown overboard (Acts 27:19).
7. Days four through ~fourteen: "neither sun nor stars in many days appeared, and no small tempest lay on us: all hope that we should be saved was then taken away" (27:20). Navigating by stars was the primary method; blinded by storm, they are completely lost.
8. Fasting: "long abstinence" — no food for an extended period (27:21).

**Paul's intervention (Acts 27:21–26):**
"Sirs, ye should have hearkened unto me, and not have loosed from Crete, and to have gained this harm and loss. And now I exhort you to be of good cheer: for there shall be no loss of any man's life among you, but of the ship. For there stood by me this night the angel of God, whose I am, and whom I serve, Saying, Fear not, Paul; thou must be brought before Caesar: and, lo, God hath given thee all them that sail with thee" (27:21–24).

9. Night of the fourteenth day: "the shipmen deemed that they drew near to some country; and sounded, and found it twenty fathoms: and when they had gone a little further, they sounded again, and found it fifteen fathoms" (27:28). Shoaling — approaching land.
10. "They cast four anchors out of the stern, and wished for the day" (27:29).
11. Sailors attempt to escape in the dinghy (pretending to lay anchors from the bow); Paul warns the centurion; soldiers cut the ropes (27:30–32).

**The Meal Before the Wreck (Acts 27:33–36):**
"While the day was coming on, Paul besought them all to take meat, saying, This day is the fourteenth day that ye have tarried and continued fasting, having taken nothing. Wherefore I pray you to take some meat: for this is for your health: for there shall not an hair fall from the head of any of you. And when he had thus spoken, he took bread, and gave thanks to God in presence of them all: and when he had broken it, he began to eat. Then were they all of good cheer, and they also took some meat."

This is one of the most explicit meal scenes in Acts — the language ("took bread, gave thanks, broke it") deliberately echoes Eucharistic language. Paul presides over a meal for the entire ship's company (276 persons) in the midst of catastrophe.

**Crew count:** Acts 27:37 — "And we were in all in the ship two hundred threescore and sixteen souls" — 276 people. The largest single gathering mentioned in the entire narrative.

12. At dawn: "they knew not the land; but they discovered a certain creek with a shore, into the which they were minded, if it were possible, to thrust in the ship" (27:39). Malta's bay of St. Paul (now called Mġarr ix-Xini or traditionally St. Paul's Bay).
13. Running the ship aground: "And falling into a place where two seas met, they ran the ship aground; and the forepart stuck fast, and remained unmoveable, but the hinder part was broken with the violence of the waves" (27:41). A sand bar or rocky shoal between two channels.
14. Soldiers plan to kill the prisoners (27:42) — to prevent escape. Julius overrules them "willing to save Paul" (27:43).
15. All 276 reach shore safely — "some on boards, and some on broken pieces of the ship" (27:44).

## NODE 36 — MALTA: THREE MONTHS

**Location:** Melita (modern Malta). The island is 246 km² with a largely Phoenician/Punic-descended population.

**Duration:** Three months — "because the haven was not commodious to winter in" (Acts 28:11 echoes the earlier note; Malta's winter months = approximately November–February, so approximately November 59 – February 60 CE).

**Where he stayed:**
- Initially on the beach/shore.
- **Publius's estate** — "the chief man of the island, whose name was Publius; who received us, and lodged us three days courteously" (Acts 28:7). Publius is described as ὁ πρῶτος τῆς νήσου — "the first man of the island," which corresponds exactly to an inscriptional title found on Malta: "primus Melitensium."

**Who he met:**
- **The "barbarous people"** (Acts 28:2, 4) — βάρβαροι (barbaroi) meaning non-Greek/Latin speakers, not primitive. "The barbarous people shewed us no little kindness: for they kindled a fire, and received us every one, because of the present rain, and because of the cold" (28:2). Friendly.
- **The Viper** (Acts 28:3–6) — "as Paul had gathered a bundle of sticks, and laid them on the fire, there came a viper out of the heat, and fastened on his hand." The islanders' initial reaction: "this man is a murderer, whom, though he hath escaped the sea, yet vengeance suffereth not to live" (28:4). Paul shakes it off into the fire and shows no harm. The crowd reverses: "they changed their minds, and said that he was a god" (28:6).
- **Publius** (Acts 28:7) — chief man, host. Friendly.
- **Publius's father** (Acts 28:8) — "sick of a fever and of a bloody flux [dysentery]." Paul visits him, "prayed, and laid his hands on him, and healed him." This triggers healings of all the sick on the island.

**Meals:** The bonfire on the beach; lodging and hospitality at Publius's estate (three days of meals implied).

**Departure:** Acts 28:10–11 — "who also honoured us with many honours; and when we departed, they laded us with such things as were necessary. And after three months we departed in a ship of Alexandria, which had wintered in the isle, whose sign was Castor and Pollux [Dioscuri]."

**Ship #3:** An Alexandrian grain freighter with the figurehead/emblem of Castor and Pollux (Dioscuri) — the patron gods of sailors.

## NODE 37 — ROME: THE DESTINATION

**Location:** Rome, Italy (modern Rome, Italy).

**Route from Malta:**
- **Syracuse** (Sicily) — three days (Acts 28:12)
- **Rhegium** (modern Reggio Calabria, Italy's toe) — one day
- **Puteoli** (modern Pozzuoli, near Naples) — the main port for Alexandrian grain ships, the principal entry point to Italy. "Where we found brethren, and were desired to tarry with them seven days" (Acts 28:13–14). A Christian community at Puteoli.
- **The Appii Forum and Three Taverns** — Christians from Rome come out to meet Paul on the Appian Way. "Whom when Paul saw, he thanked God, and took courage" (Acts 28:15). These are reception points on the Via Appia, approximately 70–100 km south of Rome.

**How he got there:** Arrived in Rome escorted, approximately early spring 60 CE.

**Where he stayed:** Acts 28:16 — "Paul was suffered to dwell by himself with a soldier that kept him." A private hired lodging (28:30 — "And Paul dwelt two whole years in his own hired house, and received all that came in unto him").

**Duration:** Two years (Acts 28:30).

**Who he met at Rome:**
- **The chief of the Jews in Rome** (Acts 28:17) — Paul calls them together immediately, explains his situation (28:17–20). Initial response: they have received no letters about Paul from Judea, but want to hear his views ("concerning this sect, we know that every where it is spoken against" — 28:22). Friendly-neutral initially.
- **A "great number" of Jews** come to his lodging; he preaches from morning to evening (Acts 28:23).
- **Believers and skeptics** — the pattern repeats (28:24 — "some believed the things which were spoken, and some believed not").
- **Paul's final pronouncement (Acts 28:25–28):** Quoting Isaiah 6:9–10 (the hardening passage), Paul declares: "Be it known therefore unto you, that the salvation of God is sent unto the Gentiles, and that they will hear it." This is the programmatic conclusion of Acts.

**Letters written from Rome (the "Prison Epistles"):**
- **Philemon** — written from Roman imprisonment. "Paul, a prisoner of Jesus Christ" (Phm 1:1). Concerns the slave Onesimus, who had run away from Philemon (a Colossian Christian) and somehow come to Paul in prison. Paul returns him with this letter, asking Philemon to receive him "not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved" (Phm 1:16). The letter is 335 words — the shortest Pauline letter.
- **Philippians** — if dated to Rome (the majority view). Written with "joy" despite imprisonment. Phil 4:22 — "All the saints salute you, chiefly they that are of Caesar's household" — indicates a presence within the imperial administration.
- **Colossians** — possibly written from Rome (though some date it to Ephesus).
- **Ephesians** — if authentic, possibly from Rome.
- **Romans** was written FROM CORINTH (on the third journey, ~57 CE, before the arrest), TO Rome. Romans 15:19–24: Paul writes that he has "fully preached the gospel of Christ" from Jerusalem around to Illyricum (the Dalmatian coast — suggesting journeys Acts doesn't record), and "having no more place in these parts," plans to go to Spain via Rome. This confirms Romans predates the Roman imprisonment and represents Paul's statement of intent, not yet fulfilled.

**Acts ends** (28:30–31) with Paul in his rented house, "preaching the kingdom of God, and teaching those things which concern the Lord Jesus Christ, with all confidence, no man forbidding him." The abrupt ending — before the trial's outcome — has generated centuries of discussion. Theories:
1. Acts was written while Paul was still alive, and Luke chose to end here.
2. Luke intended a third volume.
3. The abrupt ending is intentional — the gospel has reached Rome, the narrative goal is achieved.

## FLAGGED DETAILS: Game Designer's Special Index

### MEALS AND FOOD
1. **Damascus, post-baptism:** "He received meat" — first meal after 3-day fast (Acts 9:19).
2. **Lystra:** Natural theology speech references "food and gladness" (Acts 14:17).
3. **Philippi, jailer's house:** Midnight meal after earthquake and conversion (Acts 16:34).
4. **Troas:** Breaking of bread before and after Eutychus miracle; eating at dawn (Acts 20:7, 11).
5. **The storm:** Fourteen-day fast, then Paul takes bread, gives thanks, breaks it for all 276 persons aboard (Acts 27:33–36). Explicit Eucharistic echo language.
6. **Malta:** Bonfire on beach; Publius's hospitality (Acts 28:2, 7).

### TENTMAKING / OCCUPATION
- Acts 18:3 — Corinth, working with Priscilla and Aquila.
- Acts 20:34 — Miletus speech: "these hands have ministered unto my necessities."
- 1 Corinthians 9:6, 12, 15 — Paul defends his right NOT to use apostolic support.
- 2 Corinthians 11:7–9 — admits "robbing other churches" (taking support from Macedonia, not Corinth) to preach free at Corinth.
- 1 Thessalonians 2:9 — "labour and travail: for labouring night and day, because we would not be chargeable unto any of you."

### THE THORN IN THE FLESH
- 2 Corinthians 12:7–10 — given "lest I should be exalted above measure." "Messenger of Satan." Three prayer requests refused. Answer: "My grace is sufficient for thee."
- Galatians 4:13–15 — initial illness prompts the Galatian visit; they would have given their eyes for him.
- 2 Corinthians 10:10 — opponents mock his weak physical presence.
- Acts of Paul and Thecla (apocryphal): "small of stature, with a bald head and crooked legs... eyebrows meeting and nose somewhat hooked."

### ROMAN CITIZENSHIP INVOCATIONS (key mechanical asset)
1. Philippi — invoked AFTER beating (Acts 16:37) — deliberate strategic delay.
2. Jerusalem — invoked to stop flogging (Acts 22:25).
3. Caesarea — appeal to Caesar (Acts 25:11) — the ultimate invoke, consuming the "asset."

## DESIGN NOTES: STRUCTURAL PATTERNS

**The Rejection Pattern:** In virtually every city, Paul follows the same sequence: synagogue → initial welcome → large response → Jewish opposition → pivot to Gentiles → expulsion or arrest. This is so regular it could be mechanized as a game loop.

**The Legal Escalation Arc:** Each imprisonment involves higher Roman authority: Philippi (local magistrates) → Jerusalem (tribune Claudius Lysias) → Caesarea (Felix, then Festus, then Agrippa) → Rome (Caesar's tribunal). Each level triggers rights and expands Paul's audience.

**The Two-Front Threat:** In Damascus (Jewish conspirators + Nabataean governor), in Jerusalem (Pharisees + Romans), in Ephesus (Jewish community + pagan economic interests) — Paul routinely faces simultaneous hostile forces with incompatible motives.

**Routes:** Paul's journeys largely follow Roman road infrastructure (Via Sebaste in Asia Minor, Via Egnatia in Macedonia, Via Appia into Rome) and established sailing routes (Alexandria→Puteoli grain run, coastal hopping). He moves with and against these systems — sometimes using them, sometimes avoiding main routes for safety.

**The 276:** The full ship's manifest surviving the Malta wreck is the only precise crowd count in Acts (other than the vague "three thousand" at Pentecost). It suggests Luke was counting carefully and had access to the ship's records.
