# Lab Report — Codex of Conquest: The Curse of Knowledge
### Narrative Architecture for the Full Story Arc
**Date:** 2026-05-22  
**Project:** roll2hit.com — story mode narrative layer  
**Framework applied:**
- D&D quest design — 5-step template (Tales Arcane)
- One-shot pacing principles — modular content, single goal
- Steven Pinker — Curse of Knowledge as both writing principle *and* theme
- Epic Battlegrounds — the testing ground for the curse in action

---

## THE CONCEPT

Steven Pinker's Curse of Knowledge: once you know something, you can no longer remember what it was like not to know it. Once you can fix the world, you can no longer remember what it felt like to be helpless in it.

That is Froberger's arc. That is the story.

Froberger gathered the Shards before you did. He knew everything you are learning. He could see the danger with perfect clarity, explain it to anyone, demonstrate exactly why the Void was advancing and exactly what needed to happen to stop it. He tried telling people. They couldn't hear it — not because they were stupid, but because understanding requires the same knowledge you already have, and you can't give knowledge to someone by talking at them. You can only give it to them by the world showing them, slowly, over years.

So Froberger stopped telling and started fixing. He became the person who could do what others couldn't. He became the only one. And that — not the Void, not Commander Auros, not anything with teeth — is what destroyed him.

The Codex of Conquest is about what happens to a person who becomes too capable, too fast, in a world that cannot keep up. The player reads this story in fragments, in the margins of a dead man's journal, and has to decide whether they're going to repeat it.

The curse is not that you become evil. The curse is that you become *alone*. And being alone with the knowledge of what the world needs, while the world continues to not know it, is a particular kind of hell that the world never sees and never thanks you for surviving.

## SECTION I — THE 5-STEP TEMPLATE APPLIED TO THE FULL ARC

The Tales Arcane framework recommends starting from **Step 3** — the main challenge — and building outward. This is correct. The main challenge drives the shape of everything else.

**Step 3 — The Main Challenge:** Gather seven Codex Shards across eight acts, crossing every terrain in a broken world, and seal the Void in the Cosmic Realm. This is the stated quest.

Working *backward* to Step 2:

**Step 2 — Initial Challenge:** The world is not broken only by the Void. It is broken in twenty specific ways, each with a specific person who has been waiting for someone of your exact capability to arrive. They cannot fix it themselves. No one nearby can fix it either. They have tried the local options and the local options have failed. You are what is left. The Epic Battlegrounds are not side content — they are the test of whether you can help people not as a transaction but as a human being who chose to be capable for a reason.

Working backward to Step 1:

**Step 1 — Establish the Objective:** Archivus Sweelinck tells you about the Shards. He is careful. He uses the word "covenant," not "quest." Froberger's Journal, Entry 1: *"The Codex is broken. The Shards are scattered. Someone will come. Someone always does."* He doesn't say it with hope. He says it with the flat exhaustion of someone who has watched this pattern long enough to know its shape.

The player has three pieces of information before the quest begins: what needs to be done (gather seven Shards), why it needs to be done (the Void is a conqueror, not a fog), and what happens if they fail (the world loses the things that make it worth saving — Wren's farm, Rona's loch, Darro's feeling of safety at sea). The "what happens if they fail" is not abstract. It is specific people they have not yet met but will.

Moving forward to Step 4:

**Step 4 — New Information:** Froberger's Journal, in fragments. The player reads five entries at key narrative nodes, and can find twelve more as collectibles. Each entry shows the same person moving through the same world the player is moving through. Early entries: wonder, care, naming things. Middle entries: efficiency, distance, the steady narrowing of attention to what matters for the mission. Late entries: a man who has stopped seeing people and started seeing problems, who helps with flawless competence and zero presence.

The new information is not about the Void. It is about the player. The journal is a mirror. By Entry 31 the player understands that what Froberger is describing is not failure — it is success taken too far, competence without limit applied to a world that needed something else.

**Step 5 — The Final Challenge:** The player defeats Commander Auros. They have all seven Shards. The Codex reforges. And Sweelinck says something in the Cosmic Realm that is not a congratulation.

The final challenge is not the boss fight. The final challenge is what the player was while they were doing all of it. Whether they came back to the NPCs. Whether they read the journal entries or skipped them. Whether they helped the people who were using them, knowing they were being used. The game ends either way. The ending changes.

## SECTION II — THE CURSE OF KNOWLEDGE AS THEME

### What the curse looks like from the outside

A person under the Curse of Knowledge is very good at their job. They move quickly. They solve problems efficiently. They ask the right questions and don't waste time on the wrong ones. From the outside they look like exactly what the world needs.

From the inside they are watching everyone around them fail to understand what they understand, fail to move at the pace they move, fail to see what is obvious. This is not impatience — impatience passes. This is something that calcifies. Every person who couldn't fix their own problem becomes evidence of a category: people who cannot fix things. The curse is the moment you stop noticing which specific person is standing in front of you.

### What Froberger's journal shows

The journal has forty-two entries. Seventeen of them are written or revised for the Curse of Knowledge arc. They form a continuous narrative in three movements.

**Movement 1 — Wonder (Entries 1–14):** Froberger is a person. He has questions about people's lives. He writes about the quality of light at different hours in different terrains. He makes mistakes and writes about those too. He is surprised by things.

**Movement 2 — Efficiency (Entries 15–28):** Froberger is becoming a function. He writes in shorter sentences. He stops using people's names. He notes elapsed time and route conditions. He still helps — he helps everyone, thoroughly — but the helping has changed character. It is now something he does *to* situations, not something he does *with* people.

**Movement 3 — Isolation (Entries 29–41):** Froberger is alone in a specific way. Not lonely — he talks to people constantly, he negotiates, he delivers return beats. But he is describing the world from outside it. The people he helps are categories of need. The terrain is a series of obstacles. The journal becomes a log. Entry 41 is the exception — the only entry in the last phase that sounds like a person again, because he is writing to the person who will come after him.

### What the player learns from the mirror

The journal's function is not to tell the player "don't be like Froberger." It is to show the player, in real time, what Froberger was doing when he was still good — and then show them what happened when he kept doing exactly the same thing, slightly more.

The difference between Movement 1 Froberger and Movement 3 Froberger is not a dramatic break. There is no moment where Froberger becomes cold. There is only a series of moments where he becomes slightly more efficient. The horror of the Curse of Knowledge is that it doesn't feel like decay from the inside. It feels like growth.

## SECTION III — FINN'S JOURNAL: THE FULL EXTENDED ARC

### Read-Aloud Entries (5) — canonical in story.md

These five entries play automatically at story beats. They are read aloud. They are the spine.

---

**Entry 7** *(Node: IN — First Inn, Act I)*

The innkeeper's daughter is learning to read. She showed me the letters — she wrote them on a scrap of flour-sack paper with a stub of charcoal, pressing hard, the way children do when they haven't learned to trust that soft marks still count. She showed me three letters and said their sounds.

I showed her how to write *expedition*. She asked what it meant. I said: going somewhere you haven't been before, on purpose. She wrote it back, larger than I wrote it, because she was still pressing hard.

I am writing this in a room that smells of old hay and soap. The hay is from a bed that has had forty years of travelers in it and the soap is from someone who cares about forty years of travelers. I am not usually aware of soap.

I think this is the last night I will be unaware of soap. The route gets harder from here.

---

**Entry 14** *(Node: DK — Tilbury Docks, Act II)*

Magistra Muffat said: *He was careful. Couriers who are careful last three years on this route. He lasted thirty-eight months.*

She said it matter-of-factly, the way a person says a fact they have already grieved. Thirty-eight months is not an abstraction to her. It is a number she arrived at by subtraction, on a specific day, in a specific office, with a ledger open in front of her.

I have been calculating things since I started this journey. Distances, encounter probabilities, optimal approach vectors for the terrain types I've crossed. I am not sure when I started. I think it was before the inn with the daughter who presses hard.

Muffat gave me the Shard without ceremony. I received it the same way. I don't know if that was correct. I don't know what the correct way to receive a surrender document is.

---

**Entry 23** *(Node: DS — Deep Sea, Act IV)*

The Charybdis is not a monster. It is a consequence. The thing in the trench that causes the water displacement that causes the whirlpool — that is a monster. What I fought today was the evidence of something I haven't reached yet.

I have been thinking about evidence. Muffat's thirty-eight months. The claw marks in the crypt. The satchel at the altar edge, still packed. The evidence is always the same shape: someone who got close, and then stopped, and left a mark that points toward what stopped them.

I am following a trail of absences.

The navigator has been on the deep routes for thirty years. He folded his charts the way you fold something you trust completely. I am going to have to come back to this trench. I can feel it. The True Leviathan is down there. I know how that sentence sounds. I am writing it anyway.

---

**Entry 31** *(Node: DE — Desert, Act VI)*

The caravan master cannot file an insurance claim for "mummified pharaoh destroyed six camels." She told me this with the focused anger of someone who has been told by every institutional structure available that their problem doesn't fit the categories the institution recognizes.

She is right. It doesn't. Three hundred years of scholarship created the pharaoh and the insurance adjusters can't touch either of them.

I fixed it in one afternoon. She paid me. She is already filling in the resolution field on the claim form. The adjuster can assume what he likes.

I wrote "one afternoon" and then sat with it. Three centuries. One afternoon. I am not sure what to do with that ratio. I used to be certain it was a good thing.

---

**Entry 41** *(Node: CO — Cosmic Realm, Act VIII)*

Your sibling will find seven. I know this the way I know everything now — by the shape of the absence. Someone made the covenant before me. I made it after. You are making it now. The pattern is very clear from here.

The curse is not gathering the Shards. The curse is knowing, every time, that you are the only one who will — and being right about that, and still not knowing whether being right about it is the same as it being true.

I tried to give this knowledge away. I wrote it in forty-one entries. I explained the terrain, the routes, the monsters, the people. I explained everything I knew. No one who read this before you could carry it — not because they weren't capable, but because knowledge that cost this much to earn cannot be received for free. It has to be walked.

You walked it. So you know now. I am sorry for that. I am also grateful. The covenant needed someone who knew.

Come back, when it's done. To the people. They need the person who knows, not just the knowledge. That is what I failed to understand. I kept the knowledge and gave them the outcomes. They needed the person who was willing to stay uncertain with them while the outcomes were still unknown.

Come back.

**Entry 40** *(Node: HC — Heavenly Clouds, Act VII)*

Ithiel moves like someone who knows exactly how much strength he has left and is spending it carefully. I used to move that way. Before the route got clear. Before I stopped believing that strength was a finite resource.

The problem with believing that your strength is infinite is not that it makes you reckless. It makes you lonely. Finite strength is something you share with other people — everyone runs out, so everyone understands running out, so there is a kind of fellowship in the exhaustion. If you never run out, you are not part of that fellowship anymore.

Ithiel is part of it. I can tell by the way he walks. I am envious of him in a way I cannot explain to anyone who hasn't felt what I am feeling right now, which is most people, which is the whole problem.

## SECTION IV — CLASSIFYING THE EB NPCS BY CURSE DYNAMIC

The Epic Battleground quests are not all the same test. They fall into three categories based on what they require of the player emotionally. The curse tests the player differently in each category.

### Category A — The Genuinely Broken

These NPCs have been waiting for someone capable. Their pain is old, specific, and not about the player. They are not asking because they want to use power — they're asking because they have exhausted every alternative and the player is what is left.

| NPC | Signal | The wound |
|-----|--------|-----------|
| Shepherd Rona (EH) | *"I buried his coat last week. People thought it was strange. I thought it was practical."* | She waves at her husband through the water every day. She knows exactly what she's lost and refuses to pretend otherwise. |
| Woodcutter Bram (EF) | *"I'm not asking you to be a woodsman. I'm asking you to go where a woodsman can't."* | He walked his crew in. He is sitting on a small rock. He is not moving. |
| First Mate Darro (ED) | *"I'm fifty-three years old and I've never felt safe at sea."* | One request, correctly sized for one person. He has been carrying this image for twenty years because no one else on the ship will talk about it. |
| Farmer Wren (EM) | *"You're the third option. I've exhausted the first two."* | Three years in a rented room. A house she cannot enter. The county folder is very thick. |
| Crone Wane (EW) | *"We are not good. We have never claimed to be good. But there is a difference between what we do and what she does."* | She makes distinctions. The distinction is important and she knows it and knows no one will understand it. |

**The curse test:** These NPCs are easy to dismiss precisely because they're so clearly in need. The curse says: *I am here. Let me fix this quickly so I can move on.* The covenant says: *This person has been waiting. I am the first person capable of helping who has stopped.* The difference is whether you read the return beat as a transaction or a resolution.

### Category B — The Transactional

These NPCs lead with professional distance. They have framed their desperation as a business problem because professional distance is how they have survived the desperation. They are not less broken — they are more armored.

| NPC | The frame | What's underneath |
|-----|-----------|-------------------|
| Harbormaster Tula (EB) | *"The wreck is a business problem."* | She has been telling widows their husbands are at sea. She has been doing it for three years. The ledger is standing in front of her rage so it doesn't burn everything. |
| Caravan Master Zephyrine (EE) | *"I cannot file a claim for 'mummified pharaoh destroyed six camels.'"* | She has been right all along and no institution will acknowledge it. The anger is the correct response to being right and ignored. |
| River Trader Aldous (EL) | *"My accountant told me to stop ordering boats."* | The boats are a coping mechanism. He knows this. He said so. His accountant told him twice. |
| Harbormaster Tula (EB) extra | *"I'm not sentimental about this."* | She says it twice. People who aren't sentimental don't need to say it. |

**The curse test:** The transactional NPCs are the most dangerous for a cursed player because they speak the language of efficiency. They offer the player a clean transaction. The curse says: *Good. A clear exchange. I help, I am paid, I leave.* The covenant says: *This person is using the word 'business' because they cannot afford to use the word 'grief' and still finish the sentence.*

### Category C — The Users

These NPCs genuinely need the player's help and are also using the player for purposes beyond the stated task. They are not dishonest about this — they simply have more interests than they're naming.

| NPC | The ask | The secondary interest |
|-----|---------|------------------------|
| Warlord Mordus (EG) | Territorial integrity — a Void shaman operating in his warrens. | Kazrath's journal mentions Birka twice. Mordus will work that intelligence long after the player is gone. He knows exactly what he's buying for 400gp. |
| Izador al-Rashun (EV) | A contract renegotiated without consent. | He has been building a network for two centuries. The player is an asset in it. The endorsement he gives you is worth more to him than 350gp. |
| Captain Draketide (EA) | Something below the archive floor. | The construction notes. Six years of footnotes, now she has the text. She will use it. Not all of the use will be for the player's benefit. |
| Navigator Cassius (EO) | The shipping lane. His brother's ship. | The two food-insecure islands. They paid before Cassius told them to, because news travels fast when it's good. He is a navigator. He had already mapped the political value of this. |

**The curse test:** Category C is the hardest. The cursed player sees the secondary interest and thinks: *I am being used.* And stops. The covenant says: *They need help. They also have their own interests. These are not in conflict. Every person who asks for help has interests I don't fully know. Help is not conditional on purity of motive.*

Froberger helped all of them. He helped them efficiently and without illusions. He wrote about Mordus: *"He will use what I brought him. Let him."* But notice: he stopped using Mordus's name somewhere around Entry 26. The person disappeared. What remained was the outcome.

That is where the curse takes root. Not in refusing to help. In helping so efficiently that the person you're helping stops being a person and starts being a completed task.

## SECTION V — THE WORLD-BUILDING QUEST

Parallel to the Shard-gathering main quest, the player is building a map of a world that was whole before the Void arrived and will need to be whole again after. The Epic Battlegrounds are twenty points on that map. Each one is a specific thing the Void or its attendant chaos has broken. Each one, cleared, is a specific thing restored.

The world-building quest has no QUEST_DB entry. It has no waypoint. It has no completion screen. It is the sum of the EB return beats — twenty NPCs who have their problems resolved, twenty specific conditions of the world that are measurably better than they were before the player arrived.

By the time the player reaches CO with all seven Shards, they have not just collected artifacts. They have:
- Given a shepherd back a loch that is just water now
- Returned a formulary to an apprentice who needed to read his teacher's last work
- Given a first mate his first feeling of safety in twenty years
- Cleared a shipping lane so two islands have food
- Let a farmer back into her own house

The Void sealing is the dramatic event. The EB return beats are the real victory. The dramatic event happens once. The world that is better afterward is what the covenant was for.

Sweelinck knows this. His last line in the Covenant Ending is: *"They won't know you did it. That's correct. That's how a covenant works."*

## SECTION VI — THE TWO ENDINGS

The game produces one of two ending variants based on a **Curse Score** — a derived value from how the player moved through the world.

### Computing the Curse Score

```
curseScore = (ebQuestsStartedButNeverReturned × 3) + (ebQuestsNeverStarted × 1) - (allReturnBeatsCompleted ? 5 : 0)
```

- **curseScore ≤ 0:** The Covenant Ending
- **curseScore ≥ 15:** The Cursed Ending (soft variant)
- A player who completes all 20 EB quests and all 20 return beats always gets the Covenant Ending regardless of other factors.
- A player who defeats 10+ bosses and returns to fewer than 5 NPCs gets the Cursed Ending.

### The Covenant Ending

The player defeats Commander Auros. The Codex Cradle glows. Sweelinck reads Entry 41 aloud — the whole entry, slowly. Then he says:

*"Froberger got this far. He had all seven. He could have sealed it. He chose not to — he said the world didn't deserve it yet."*

He looks at the Cradle. *"He was wrong. The world doesn't have to deserve it. That's not how covenants work. A covenant is: I will do this because it needs to be done and I am able to do it. Not because they asked correctly. Not because they thanked you. Not because they're grateful enough. Just: because."*

He steps back. *"He couldn't stay uncertain with them. You did. That's the difference."*

The Codex reforges. The Void seals. The world doesn't know what happened. That's the point.

### The Cursed Ending (soft variant — not a punishment, a different truth)

The player defeats Commander Auros. The Codex Cradle glows. Sweelinck begins reading Entry 41. He stops before the last line.

*"Froberger wrote the rest of this."* He closes the journal. *"I won't read it. You already know what it says."*

He looks at the player. *"You got here. That's not nothing. Froberger got here too. The difference between you and Froberger is whether you come back."*

Pause.

*"You still can."*

The Codex reforges. The Void seals. The victory screen shows two columns: **Shards Gathered** (complete) and **People Returned To** (incomplete). Not as a grade. As a record.

The game doesn't tell the player they failed. It shows them what they did and what they didn't do and leaves the question open. The world is saved. The world needed saving. Whether the player was present for the people in it, or just for the crisis — that's the question the ending asks.

It doesn't answer it.

## SECTION VII — THE JADED JOURNAL AS WARNING

The journal serves a fourth function beyond character study, narrative mirror, and new information. It is a **warning that the player can use**.

In Entries 16 and 35 — the Crone Wane entry and the Sweelinck handwriting entry — Froberger makes observations about *other people* that are actually observations about himself. He notices Wane's distinction-making. He notices Sweelinck's shrinking handwriting. He recognizes both as meaningful. He writes them down.

He is still capable of seeing, in these moments. The curse hasn't taken his sight. It has taken his *application* of the sight to himself. He sees clearly and doesn't connect what he sees to what he's becoming.

The player reads this and gets a choice. They can recognize the pattern — a highly capable person observing symptoms of his own condition in other people, unable to turn the observation inward — and change course. Or they can read it as a character study and keep moving at speed.

The game never tells the player which they're doing. That's the point. The journal is the only place where the information exists. Whether the player reads the collectible entries, whether they sit with what they mean, whether they slow down enough to connect Entry 16 Froberger to Entry 39 Froberger — that's the actual gameplay of the narrative layer.

The curse of knowledge applies to the player too. Once you know what Froberger became, you can't unknow it. But you can choose whether the knowing changes how you move through the world.

## SECTION VIII — DIALOGUE ADDITIONS FOR CRANE (ACT-BASED VARIANTS)

Sweelinck appears at SQ (Node 35) in Act VI. His dialogue should evolve based on the player's curse score at the time of arrival. Four variants:

**Sweelinck — Low curse score (≤ 3, most EB returns completed):**
*"You've been busy. Not just with the Shards — with the world around them. Froberger was busy with the Shards. The difference is visible in the journal."* He sets it on the table. *"He wrote about terrain. You've been creating the kind of problems you have to go back for."*

**Sweelinck — Medium curse score (4–9, some returns completed):**
*"Froberger's journal describes this route in the same order you've been walking it. He noticed things. Good things, accurate things. He stopped writing about people around Entry 22. Do you know which entry you're on?"* He doesn't wait for an answer. *"That's a question I'm asking you to carry, not one I expect you to answer now."*

**Sweelinck — High curse score (10–14, few returns):**
*"The journal becomes a log around Entry 26. You can feel it — the handwriting steadies, the sentences shorten. He was getting efficient."* A pause. *"I've been watching you from the notes people send me. Wren's county request. Tula's harbor logs. There are gaps where you were and then weren't."* He looks at the journal. *"Froberger had gaps too."*

**Sweelinck — Maximum curse score (≥ 15, almost no returns):**
*"Froberger's name appears once in the county records and once in the harbor logs. No more."* He holds out the journal. *"He was very good at the Shards. He was the best I've seen at the Shards. He had seven. He stood in the Cosmic Realm. He looked at the Cradle."* Long pause. *"He left. He said the world didn't deserve it yet. I've been asking myself: did he mean the world, or did he mean the people? Because the Void doesn't care which you mean."*

## SECTION IX — PACING AND MODULAR CONTENT

The Tales Arcane one-shot framework identifies the core problem: everything takes longer than you think. The Epic Battlegrounds are modular content by design — dead-ends, each self-contained, completable in any order or not at all.

The five-step template within each EB quest:
1. **Establish the Objective:** NPC opening dialogue, wound, opening offer
2. **Initial Challenge:** The journey to the EB node (corridor traverse, notoriety-scaled random encounters)
3. **Main Challenge:** The Epic Boss — deadly tier, no warm-up, DANGER:EPIC banner, NPC warning verbatim
4. **New Information:** The boss itself recontextualizes the NPC — we understand Tula's ledger differently after meeting the professional vampire privateer who has been unable to leave the wreck for ninety years; we understand Rona's grief differently after seeing sixty years of the aboleth's slow work in the loch
5. **Final Challenge:** The return. Going back to the person. This is the step the cursed player skips.

Modular application: The DM (or the system, in roll2hit's case) can slot EBs in and out without breaking the main quest. A player who completes zero EBs still finishes the game. A player who completes all twenty gets a different ending and a victory screen that shows the full shape of the world they restored.

The one-shot principle — *the less content you try to stuff in, the more the content that's there can breathe* — applies to each EB quest individually. Each one is a short story with a single goal. The goal has a person behind it. That person has a return beat. That's the whole structure, done right, twenty times.
